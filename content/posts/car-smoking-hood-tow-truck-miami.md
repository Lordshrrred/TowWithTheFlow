> **Quick Answer:** Pull over immediately and turn off the engine. Do not open the hood if you see flames or heavy smoke. Call 911 for fire, or a tow truck for mechanical issues. In Miami's heat, engine overheating is common and can cause serious damage if you keep driving.

## What To Do

1. **Pull over safely** to the right shoulder or parking lot. Turn on hazard lights.

2. **Turn off the engine immediately** and remove the key from ignition.

3. **Exit the vehicle** and move away from the car. Stand upwind from any smoke.

4. **Assess the situation:**
   - White/gray steam: likely coolant leak or overheating
   - Black/dark smoke: oil burning or serious engine damage
   - Any flames visible: call 911 immediately

5. **Do not open the hood** if smoke is heavy or you suspect fire. Hot coolant can spray and cause severe burns.

6. **Call for help:**
   - Fire/flames: 911 first
   - Mechanical issues: tow truck or roadside assistance

7. **If safe to check:** Wait 15-20 minutes for cooling, then carefully open hood with a cloth or tool. Look for obvious leaks, broken hoses, or damaged components.

### Reading the Smoke More Carefully

The color and smell of the smoke tell you a lot before you touch anything. White or gray steam with a sweet smell almost always points to coolant escaping, either through a cracked hose, a failing radiator cap, or a blown head gasket. If that sweetish smell hits you the moment you step out, coolant is burning off somewhere on the hot engine block.

Black smoke that smells sharp and acrid means oil is burning. That can happen when an oil line rubs against an exhaust component and finally wears through, or when a valve cover gasket has been leaking for a while and oil drips onto the manifold. In either case, continuing to drive risks a fire, not just mechanical damage.

Blue-tinged smoke usually comes from the tailpipe rather than the hood, and indicates oil burning inside the cylinders, a separate problem, but worth noting so you describe it accurately when you call the shop.

Miami-specific note: ambient air temperatures regularly hit 95 degrees Fahrenheit or higher from June through September. That alone raises baseline coolant temps by 10 to 15 degrees compared to cooler climates. Stop-and-go traffic on I-95, the Palmetto, or US-1 removes the airflow your radiator depends on, so cars that run fine on the highway can overheat in 20 minutes sitting in a Miami traffic jam.

### After the Smoke Stops

Once you have waited 15 to 20 minutes and the hood is cool enough to touch barehanded, look for these specific things before deciding whether to attempt a short drive to a shop or call a tow:

- A collapsed or burst radiator hose is obvious and not safe to drive on.
- A slow coolant drip from a cracked reservoir might let you drive a mile or two if you add coolant and watch the temp gauge closely, but only if there are no signs of oil mixing with coolant (brown, frothy fluid on the dipstick or in the reservoir).
- If the oil level is critically low and you can smell burnt oil, do not restart the engine. Driving even a quarter mile on a starved engine can score the cylinder walls.

Always carry a quart of the correct coolant type in the trunk. Mixing green and orange coolant degrades both and can gel inside your system. Check your owner's manual before topping off.

## Common Questions

**Q: Can I drive a short distance to a shop if my car was smoking but seems fine now?**
A: Only if the temp gauge is reading normal, there are no visible leaks, and oil and coolant levels are both full. Even then, keep the trip under two miles and watch the gauge the whole way. If it climbs toward the red, pull over again immediately.

**Q: How long do I have to wait before it is safe to open the hood?**
A: At least 15 to 20 minutes after shutting the engine off, and longer if smoke is still rising. Test the hood surface with the back of your hand before unlatching it. If it is still very hot to the touch, wait another 10 minutes.

**Q: Is white smoke from under the hood always a coolant problem?**
A: Almost always, yes. The one exception is condensation burning off the exhaust manifold on a cold morning, which disappears within a minute or two. If white steam keeps coming or smells sweet, you have a coolant leak somewhere and the engine needs to cool completely before you investigate further.

- Do not add cold water to a hot engine
- Stay clear of the vehicle if smoke continues after shutdown
- Have your phone charged for emergency calls
