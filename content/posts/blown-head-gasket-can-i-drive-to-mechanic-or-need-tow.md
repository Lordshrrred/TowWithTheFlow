> **Quick Answer:** Do not drive a car with a blown head gasket to the mechanic. Even a short drive risks warping the aluminum cylinder head, hydrolock from coolant in the combustion chamber, or a full engine seizure. The repair goes from $1,500 to $12,000+ the moment the block or head takes secondary damage. Call a tow truck. A flatbed is best.

## How Do I Know If My Head Gasket Is Actually Blown?

The clearest signs are white smoke from the exhaust that smells sweet, coolant disappearing with no visible leak, milky brown oil on the dipstick or inside the oil filler cap, and an engine that overheats repeatedly even after you top off the coolant. You might also see bubbles in the coolant reservoir while the engine runs, which means combustion gases are pushing into the cooling system through the failed gasket. Any one of these is serious. Two or more together and it's almost certainly the head gasket.

A compression leak between cylinders can also cause a rough idle or misfiring under load. If your check engine light is on alongside any of the coolant or oil symptoms, don't let that distract you from the bigger picture. The engine is telling you it's in trouble.

## Why Can't I Just Drive It Slowly to the Shop?

Driving with a blown head gasket compounds the damage every minute the engine runs. Coolant leaking into the combustion chamber turns to steam instantly, but if enough pools in the cylinder, the piston hits liquid during the compression stroke. Liquid doesn't compress. That's called hydrolock, and it bends or snaps connecting rods, sometimes punching through the block entirely. That turns a $2,000 head gasket job into a $6,000 engine replacement, or a total loss on an older vehicle.

Even if hydrolock doesn't happen, the engine will overheat. Aluminum cylinder heads warp at temperatures above roughly 230 to 240 degrees Fahrenheit. Once warped, the head has to be milled flat or replaced before a new gasket will seal. That adds hundreds of dollars to an already expensive repair. Running the engine overheated also risks cracking the head, which can make the head unrepairable entirely.

If the car is already overheating on top of the head gasket failure, pull over immediately and shut it off. See [car overheating on highway: should I pull over immediately](/car-overheating-on-highway-should-i-pull-over-immediately-2/) for what to do in those first few minutes.

## What Should I Do Right Now While I Wait for the Tow?

Turn the engine off and leave it off. Do not restart it to "check" anything or to move the car a few feet. If you're on a highway shoulder, get yourself and any passengers out of the vehicle and behind the guardrail or well off the shoulder. If you're in a parking lot or side street, you're already in a safer spot.

Call for a tow, not a jump start or roadside fluid service. Tell the dispatcher you suspect a head gasket failure and that the engine has been overheating. A flatbed tow truck is the correct call here because it keeps all four wheels off the ground and eliminates any chance of the engine being bumped into running even briefly. ## Is There Any Situation Where Driving It Is Acceptable?

Only one edge case comes close: if you have a very minor head gasket seep, no active overheating, no coolant in the oil, no white smoke, and the shop is literally two blocks away, some mechanics will tell you it's a judgment call. But that situation is rare. Most people searching this question are already seeing symptoms, which means the gasket has failed past the "seep" stage.

If your temperature gauge is in the normal range, the oil looks clean, and you have zero white smoke, confirm with a mechanic by phone before you move the car. Describe every symptom in detail and let them make the call. Do not self-authorize a drive based on hope.

If the engine has already overheated once and then cooled down, treat it as a tow situation. An engine that has already hit thermal extremes may have a warped head that will overheat again within minutes of restarting. ## Common Questions

**Q: Can I add head gasket sealer and drive it to the shop?**
A: Products like Bar's Leaks or K-Seal can slow a minor seep temporarily, but they will not fix a fully blown gasket. If you're already seeing white smoke or coolant in the oil, a sealer will not hold long enough to make a shop run safe and can clog your heater core or radiator in the process.

**Q: How much does it cost to tow a car with a blown head gasket?**
A: A local flatbed tow typically runs $100 to $200 for the first 10 miles, depending on your city. After that, expect $3 to $7 per additional mile. It's a fraction of what you'll pay if you drive it and destroy the engine. Check your auto insurance policy for roadside assistance coverage before you pay out of pocket.

**Q: Will my car insurance cover the tow for a mechanical breakdown like this?**
A: Standard liability or collision coverage does not cover mechanical breakdowns. Roadside assistance add-ons through insurers like State Farm or Progressive often do. AAA membership also covers towing. Call your insurer before calling a tow company to find out your coverage limit.

**Q: How long can I wait on the side of the road for a tow truck?**
A: There's no safety clock on waiting itself, as long as you're positioned safely off the road. The danger is inside the car, not outside. Stay away from the vehicle if you're on a highway shoulder and keep your hazard lights running.

**Q: How do I know if my engine is already damaged from driving with the blown head gasket?**
A: The mechanic will do a compression test and a combustion leak test using a chemical block tester. A cylinder that has significantly lower compression than the others points to gasket failure or head damage. If two adjacent cylinders read very low, the gasket has likely blown between them. The shop may also scope the cylinders or inspect the head for warping with a straight edge.
