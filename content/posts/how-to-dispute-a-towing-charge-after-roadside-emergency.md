> **Quick Answer:** Call the tow company first and ask for an itemized invoice. If the charge still looks wrong, file a complaint with your state's consumer protection office or public utilities commission, and dispute it with your insurance or credit card company simultaneously. Most overcharges get resolved within two to four weeks if you document everything and push on multiple fronts at once.

## What Do I Do the Moment I Think My Towing Bill Is Wrong?

Get an itemized invoice in writing before you do anything else. Do not pay a lump-sum total and assume you can sort it out later. Once you pay without disputing, you have handed away your strongest leverage.

Call the tow company and say: "I need a line-by-line invoice showing every charge, the per-mile rate, the hookup fee, any storage fees, and the total mileage driven." Write down the name of whoever you speak with and the time of the call. If they refuse to provide one, that refusal is itself evidence for a complaint.

Then check those numbers against what actually happened. A standard tow includes a hookup fee (often $50 to $100) and a per-mile charge (commonly $3 to $7 per mile for a standard flatbed). If your car was towed 8 miles and the invoice shows 22, you have a clear, documentable error. Photograph your original breakdown location on Google Maps and compare it to the drop-off address. GPS mileage does not lie.

## What Are the Actual Rules Tow Companies Have to Follow?

Tow companies are regulated at the state level, and most states require them to post their rates, provide an itemized receipt, and give you a chance to retrieve personal property before charging storage. The specific rules vary, but the Federal Trade Commission's consumer protection framework still applies to deceptive billing practices anywhere in the country.

Look up your state's towing regulations through your state's public utilities commission, department of transportation, or attorney general's website. California, for example, has some of the strictest tow-rate rules in the country, limiting what a company can charge for non-consent tows. Texas regulates towing through the Texas Department of Licensing and Regulation. Knowing your state's specific agency name is useful because that is exactly where you file your formal complaint.

If your car was towed from private property after a breakdown rather than dispatched by you or a roadside service, you have even stronger rights in most states. Non-consent tows are more tightly regulated than consent tows precisely because the driver had no say in the matter.

## How Do I Actually File a Dispute and Get My Money Back?

Work all three channels at the same time: the tow company directly, your insurance or roadside assistance provider, and your state regulator.

**With the tow company:** Send a written dispute letter (email is fine, certified mail is better) that names every specific charge you believe is wrong, cites the correct amount you believe you owe, and states a deadline of 10 business days for a response. Attach your itemized invoice and your GPS mileage evidence.

**With your insurance:** If you have roadside assistance or towing coverage, your insurer has a vested interest in making sure you were not overcharged, because they may be on the hook for part of it. File a claim even if you already paid out of pocket. If you used a credit card with roadside benefits, contact that card issuer directly. ## What If My Insurance or Roadside Assistance Already Paid Part of the Bill?

Your coverage paying part of the charge does not waive your right to dispute the rest. If the tow company billed $800 and your policy paid $150, you still owe $650 of a potentially fraudulent charge. Dispute the full invoice with the tow company and loop in your insurer, because your insurer may also want to recover what they already paid.

Check what your policy's actual towing coverage limit is before assuming you are stuck with the balance. Coverage limits vary widely. ## Common Questions

**Q: Can a tow company charge me storage fees if I picked up my car the same day?**
A: Most states prohibit storage fees for the same calendar day the tow occurred, but some allow a one-day minimum. Get the exact storage fee line item on your invoice and compare it to your state's regulations. If you retrieved the car within hours and see a multi-day storage charge, that is a clear dispute.

**Q: What if I already paid the towing bill in full?**
A: You can still dispute it. Send the tow company a written demand for a partial refund citing the specific overcharge. If they refuse, you can file a chargeback with your credit card issuer (if you paid by card) within the card's dispute window, typically 60 to 120 days. You can also still file a state complaint and pursue small claims court.

**Q: How long does a towing dispute take to resolve?**
A: A direct resolution with the tow company can happen in one to two weeks if you are persistent and have solid documentation. State complaint processes typically take four to eight weeks. A credit card chargeback takes 30 to 90 days. File all three at once so you are not waiting on one process sequentially.

**Q: Can a tow company send me to collections while I am disputing the charge?**
A: Yes, and some will try. Send your dispute letter via certified mail so you have a timestamped record that the debt is contested. A debt in active written dispute is harder for a collection agency to act on, and the Fair Debt Collection Practices Act requires collectors to halt collection activity when a written dispute is filed.

**Q: What counts as a legitimate towing charge versus an overcharge?**
A: Legitimate charges include a hookup or dispatch fee, per-mile rate, after-hours fees if clearly posted, and storage fees after the first day. Overcharges include mileage inflation, undisclosed administrative fees added after the fact, storage charges for time the car was not actually in their lot, and rates above what the company has filed with state regulators.
