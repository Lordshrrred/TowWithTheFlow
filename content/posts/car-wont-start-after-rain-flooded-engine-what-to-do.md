> **Quick Answer:** Do not crank the engine again. If your car sat in standing water or stalled in heavy rain and won't restart, you may have a hydrolocked engine. Cranking it forces water through the cylinders and can bend connecting rods, turning a recoverable situation into a totaled engine. Get out safely, call a tow, and let a mechanic inspect it before anyone touches the ignition.

## What Exactly Happened to My Engine When It Flooded?

Water got into the combustion chamber, and engines cannot compress water the way they compress air and fuel. The moment enough water enters a cylinder, the piston has nowhere to go. This is called hydrolock. The engine either stalls immediately or, if you keep cranking, a connecting rod bends or snaps. Even a few ounces of water in one cylinder is enough to cause thousands of dollars in damage if you keep trying to start it.

Rain-related flooding usually enters one of three ways: a deep puddle that pushes water up through the intake, a submerged air filter, or water splashing into an open window and soaking electrical components. The symptom in every case is the same: the car stalls or won't turn over, and the starter may sound sluggish or make a grinding clunk instead of spinning freely.

## Should I Try to Start It Again or Wait?

No, do not try again. Every crank attempt risks catastrophic internal damage if water is sitting in the cylinders.

What you can do: open the hood and look at the air filter housing. If it is visibly wet or submerged, water almost certainly reached the intake. Check the oil dipstick too. If the oil looks milky, foamy, or the level reads higher than normal, water has mixed with the oil, and the engine needs immediate professional attention. A mechanic can pull the spark plugs and crank the engine by hand to expel water from the cylinders before attempting a restart. That is the only safe path forward.

If the car simply got soaked in rain without sitting in standing water and the air filter looks dry, the problem may be electrical: a wet ignition coil, a waterlogged distributor cap on older vehicles, or corroded battery terminals. In that case, drying out overnight sometimes resolves it. But if there is any doubt about water in the intake, treat it as a hydrolock situation and call a tow. If your car actually went into a ditch or low-water crossing, read our guide on [car hydroplaned into a ditch: towing options](/car-hydroplaned-into-ditch-towing-options/) for next steps specific to that situation.

## Is It Safe to Stay With the Car or Should I Get Out?

Get away from moving or rising water first, then deal with the car. Your car can be replaced or repaired.

If you are on a flooded road and water is still rising, do not stay in or near the vehicle. Water only six inches deep can knock a person down, and twelve inches can carry a small car off the road. Move to high ground, call 911 if you are in danger, and worry about the car once you are safe. If you stalled in a parking lot or on a dry road after heavy rain with no ongoing flood risk, stay with the vehicle with hazard lights on and call for a tow from there. ## How Do I Get a Tow for a Flooded or Water-Damaged Car?

Call a tow truck or your roadside assistance provider and tell them specifically that the car has potential water or flood damage and must not be started. This matters because some tow operators will attempt a jump start on a dead car by default.

AAA, your insurance company's roadside assistance line, or any local tow dispatch can handle this. Give them your exact location, describe the situation, and confirm the car will be flatbedded rather than dolly-towed if possible. Water-damaged vehicles can have compromised steering and braking systems, so a flatbed is the safer option. ## Common Questions

**Q: Can a flooded engine be fixed or is it totaled?**
A: It depends on how many times you cranked it and how much water entered. Caught early with no cranking, a hydrolocked engine often recovers with cylinder clearing and a parts swap. Bent rods mean rebuild or replacement, which can push an older vehicle into total-loss territory.

**Q: How long should I wait before trying to start a wet car?**
A: If the flooding was purely external (rain on electrical components, not water in the intake), waiting 24 hours for components to dry sometimes helps. If there is any chance water entered the air intake, do not wait and do not try. Have a mechanic inspect it first.

**Q: Will my insurance cover a flooded engine?**
A: Only if you carry comprehensive coverage. Comprehensive covers water and flood damage. Liability-only policies cover nothing here. Contact your insurer before authorizing repairs so they can send an adjuster if the damage is significant.

**Q: My car stalled driving through a puddle and won't start. Is that hydrolock?**
A: Very likely yes. A sudden stall mid-puddle followed by a no-start or a grinding, clunking crank is a textbook hydrolock presentation. Do not crank again. Call a tow.

**Q: Can I dry out a flooded engine myself?**
A: A mechanic can remove spark plugs and crank the engine by hand to expel water, then inspect for damage before reassembly. This is not a safe DIY job unless you know exactly what you are doing. Skipping a step and then cranking a still-wet engine causes the exact damage you are trying to avoid.
