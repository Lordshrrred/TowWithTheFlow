> **Quick Answer:** Get your car as far right as possible, turn on your hazard lights immediately, and get every person out of the vehicle and behind the guardrail or up an embankment away from traffic. Call 911 if anyone is in danger, then call a tow truck. Stay off the road itself. Night multiplies every risk on an interstate shoulder, so distance from the travel lanes is your only real protection.

## What Do I Do the Moment My Car Dies on the Interstate at Night?

Steer to the far right shoulder, coasting as much distance as you can from active lanes before you stop. The second the car is stopped, hazards on, interior lights on so rescuers can see you're inside, and then get everyone out through the passenger-side doors, never the driver's side facing traffic.

Move at least 50 feet behind your vehicle and climb over the guardrail if one is there. A guardrail is not decoration. It will stop a drifting car. The open shoulder will not. If there is no guardrail and no embankment, walk as far from the roadway as the terrain allows and sit down low so you are not silhouetted against headlights.

Do not stand behind your car. Rear-end strikes on stopped vehicles are one of the most common causes of roadside fatalities, and at night the risk is significantly higher because approaching drivers have less reaction time.

## How Do I Signal for Help So Other Drivers and Police Can See Me?

Your hazard lights are your first signal and they should already be on. If you have road flares or LED triangles in your trunk, place them 100 to 300 feet behind the vehicle in the lane or shoulder. Reflective triangles are the safer choice because flares occasionally ignite dry grass on the roadside, which is a genuine risk in dry climates.

If you have a phone, call 911 first to report your location, then call a tow service. Telling 911 your mile marker and direction of travel (northbound, southbound, etc.) is the fastest way to get a highway patrol unit to you. Most interstates have mile markers posted on small green signs on the right shoulder every tenth of a mile, and they are worth noting the moment you realize you are losing the car.

Do not rely on someone stopping to help. Accept help from a stranger only if you have no other option and keep the conversation through a cracked window with your doors locked.

## Is It Safe to Wait Inside the Car or Outside on the Shoulder?

Outside and behind the guardrail is safer than inside the car on an active interstate shoulder at night. Inside the car feels safer, but it is not. If a vehicle strikes yours from behind, being belted inside means you take the full impact. Behind a guardrail, you are physically separated from that scenario.

The one exception: if there is no barrier, no embankment, no safe place to stand, and you are on a high-speed rural interstate with no shoulder, staying belted inside with hazards running may be the least-bad option until help arrives. In that case, move to the passenger side, away from the travel lane, and stay low.

## Who Do I Call and How Long Will It Take to Get Help?

Call 911 first to report your location and get a highway patrol response. Then call your roadside assistance provider (AAA, your insurance company, or a credit card benefit if you have one). If you have none of those, search "tow truck" plus the nearest city or interstate name to find a 24-hour service.

## What If I Have Kids or Passengers With Me?

Get them out and behind the barrier before you do anything else. Children should be farthest from the road, and if you have a blanket or jacket in the car, put it on them because temperatures drop fast when you are standing still at night and stressed.

Keep everyone together and seated away from the road. Do not let children stand near the guardrail or wander. If a child is in a car seat and the car is not in immediate danger of being struck, it is acceptable to leave the seat in the car and carry the child rather than wrestling the seat out on a dark shoulder. Speed and distance from traffic matter more than the seat at this moment.

## Common Questions

**Q: Should I try to drive to the next exit even if something is wrong with my car?**
A: Only if the problem is minor and you can safely control the vehicle. A flat tire, overheating engine, or transmission slipping means you should stop now. Driving on a flat or overheated engine can cause a loss of control or a fire, both of which are worse than stopping on the shoulder.

**Q: What if I can't make it to the shoulder and I stop in a lane?**
A: Turn hazards on immediately and get everyone out of the car and over the barrier as fast as possible. Then call 911. A car stopped in a travel lane on an interstate at night is one of the most dangerous situations on the road. Do not stay in the vehicle waiting for help.

**Q: Will police tow my car if I leave it on the shoulder?**
A: Yes, if it sits long enough. Most states allow highway patrol to call a tow after one to two hours on an interstate shoulder, sometimes sooner at night or in high-traffic areas. You will owe towing and impound fees. Call a tow truck proactively so you control where the car goes.

**Q: Do I need to stay with my car while waiting for the tow truck?**
A: You do not have to stand next to it. You need to be close enough to meet the driver when they arrive. Stay behind the barrier, keep your phone on, and confirm your exact location with the tow dispatch so the driver can find you without you needing to stand in the open.

**Q: What should I keep in my car for a night interstate breakdown?**
A: At minimum: a phone charger, LED road triangles (not flares), a flashlight with fresh batteries, a reflective safety vest, and a phone number for a tow service saved in your contacts before you need it. A basic first aid kit and a warm layer round out a practical emergency kit for any season.
