> **Quick Answer:** If the temperature gauge is buried in the red and you see steam or smoke, stop driving immediately and call a tow. If the gauge crept up but you caught it early with no steam and no warning lights, pulling over and letting it cool for 30-45 minutes is reasonable. The difference comes down to what you see and how far gone the gauge is.

## Should I Tow It or Let It Cool Down?

It depends on one thing: whether coolant is still doing its job or has already failed. If steam is venting from under the hood, your coolant has boiled over, and the engine is running at temperatures that warp aluminum cylinder heads, anywhere above 240-250 degrees Fahrenheit. At that point cooling down is not a fix, it is just a pause before more damage. Call a tow.

If you caught the gauge early, pulled over before it hit the red, and there is no steam or puddle under the car, a cool-down period can get you out of a jam. Shut the engine off completely. Do not open the radiator cap. Wait at least 30 to 45 minutes before you even consider restarting.

## What Do the Symptoms Tell Me About How Bad It Is?

Steam from the hood means coolant is already boiling. That is a tow situation, not a wait-and-see situation. White smoke from the tailpipe is worse: it often means coolant is leaking into the combustion chamber, which points to a blown head gasket. If you drive on that, you will turn a $1,500 repair into a $4,000-plus engine rebuild or replacement.

A rising temp gauge with no steam and no smoke is a warning, not a crisis yet. A sweet, syrupy smell near the engine is burning coolant and tells you there is an active leak somewhere. If the heater stopped blowing hot air right before the gauge spiked, your coolant level is probably critically low. In both cases, check the coolant reservoir once the engine cools, not the radiator cap directly.

If the temperature gauge spiked and then came back down on its own, a stuck thermostat is a likely culprit. That part costs $15-50 and is a relatively simple fix, but you still should not drive far on an engine that is thermally unstable. See [car thermostat stuck open overheating: when you need a tow](/car-thermostat-stuck-open-overheating-tow-needed/) for how to read that specific situation.

## Can I Drive Slowly to the Nearest Shop Instead of Calling a Tow?

No, not if you have steam, smoke, or a gauge in the red. Every mile you put on an overheating engine risks scoring the cylinder walls or cracking the head. A tow that costs $75-$150 is cheap compared to an engine that needs new head gaskets or a full rebuild.

If the engine has fully cooled, the gauge is normal again, no warning lights are on, and you can top off the coolant reservoir with the correct coolant type for your car (check the cap on the reservoir, it will say), then driving a short distance to a shop with the heat running and the gauge watched is a calculated risk some mechanics would accept. A mile or two on a fully cooled engine with proper fluid level is very different from crawling five miles with the gauge climbing.

## What Do I Do Right Now While I'm on the Side of the Road?

Get fully off the road, turn on your hazard lights, and shut the engine off. That is the sequence, in that order. If you are on a highway, get as far onto the shoulder as possible and stay behind the guardrail if there is one. Do not stand between your car and traffic.

Do not open the hood while steam is actively coming out. Wait for it to stop before you get near the radiator. Do not unscrew the radiator cap while the engine is hot. The cooling system is pressurized: opening it hot can spray 200-degree coolant on your hands and face.

Call for a tow. If you have AAA, State Farm roadside, USAA, or any insurance-based roadside assistance, this is exactly what that coverage is for. ## Common Questions

**Q: How long should I let an overheated engine cool before I restart it?**
A: At least 30 to 45 minutes with the engine fully off. The metal components inside the block need time to contract back to safe operating temperatures. Rushing it and restarting too soon can cause the same overheating cycle to repeat immediately.

**Q: Can I add water to the radiator if I don't have coolant?**
A: Plain water works as a short-term emergency measure if the coolant level is low and the engine is fully cooled. It will not provide freeze protection or corrosion protection, and you need to flush and refill with the correct coolant mix as soon as you reach a shop. Never add cold water to a hot engine.

**Q: Is my engine ruined if it overheated once?**
A: Not necessarily. A brief overheat caught early with no steam or smoke often causes no permanent damage. An engine that ran hot for several minutes or showed steam is a different story. A mechanic needs to pressure-test the cooling system and check for a blown head gasket before you drive it again.

**Q: What does it cost to tow an overheated car to a shop?**
A: A local tow of five to ten miles typically runs $75 to $125. Longer distance tows are priced per mile after the base fee, usually $3 to $5 per mile beyond the first five. Your roadside assistance plan, if you have one, may cover the full cost or a set dollar amount.

**Q: My temperature gauge came back down after I pulled over. Is it safe to drive now?**
A: Possibly, but only if the engine is fully cooled, the coolant reservoir is at the proper level, there is no steam or leaks, and no warning lights are on. Drive directly to a shop and watch the gauge the whole way. A gauge that spiked and recovered on its own usually means there is an underlying problem that will happen again.
