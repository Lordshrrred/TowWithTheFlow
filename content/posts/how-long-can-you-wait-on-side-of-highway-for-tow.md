> **Quick Answer:** You can physically wait as long as needed, but you should not stay in or near your car on a highway shoulder for more than 10 to 15 minutes if traffic is fast and close. Get behind a guardrail or up a bank if possible. Average tow truck response times run 30 to 60 minutes, sometimes longer in rural areas or heavy traffic. Call for help immediately, then get yourself to a safe distance from the road.

## How Long Will It Actually Take for a Tow Truck to Reach Me?

In most metro areas, expect 30 to 60 minutes. On rural interstates or two-lane highways far from the nearest town, that window stretches to 90 minutes or more. Time of day matters too: a breakdown during rush hour in a dense city can push wait times past an hour even when the truck is close, because the driver is fighting the same traffic you are. If you have roadside assistance through AAA or your insurance, call immediately because dispatch can give you a real ETA and track the truck.

Factors that stretch the wait:
- You broke down far from an exit or service area
- It is late at night and fewer trucks are operating
- Weather is bad and calls are stacking up
- Your vehicle needs a flatbed, which is less common than a standard wheel-lift truck

If your ETA is over an hour and you are on a high-speed interstate, that changes your safety calculation significantly.

## Where Should I Stand While I Wait for the Tow Truck?

Get out of the car and off the roadway. The single most dangerous place you can be during a highway breakdown is sitting in the driver's seat on the shoulder, especially on interstates where speeds are 65 mph or higher. Move to the far side of the guardrail, up a grassy embankment, or as far from the travel lanes as the terrain allows.

Leave your hazard lights running, and if you have road flares or reflective triangles, place them 100 to 200 feet behind your vehicle to give approaching drivers early warning. Do not stand between your car and oncoming traffic for any reason, including to flag down the tow truck. The driver will see your flashers. If it is dark, wear anything light-colored and stay back. See more on staying safe after dark in [Car Broke Down on Highway at Night: Safety Tips That Could Save Your Life](/car-broke-down-on-highway-at-night-safety-tips/).

## Is There a Time Limit on How Long Police or Highway Patrol Will Let Me Stay?

Yes, on most U.S. interstate highways an abandoned or disabled vehicle can be cited or towed by authorities if it sits on the shoulder for more than 24 hours, and in some states that window is as short as a few hours during peak traffic. In practice, a trooper or highway patrol officer will often stop to check on you within the first 20 to 30 minutes on a busy corridor. They can call for help and will sometimes tell you your vehicle needs to move faster than your tow is arriving.

Do not ignore a trooper who tells you to vacate the area. They can arrange an emergency tow from a contracted rotation service, which may cost more than your preferred shop's truck, but your safety and clearing the shoulder legally matters more. ## What Do I Do If My Tow Truck Is Taking Way Too Long?

Call back. Do not just sit and wait. Call your roadside assistance provider or the tow company directly and ask for an updated ETA. If the truck is more than 30 minutes past the original estimate, ask them to resend or find a different provider. You are not locked in.

If you have no roadside coverage, search for a local towing company using Google Maps or call 511, the national highway information line, which in many states can connect you to DOT motorist assistance. If you are on a Dallas or Texas highway, for example, the Texas DOT runs a service patrol program on certain freeways during peak hours. Your state likely has something similar. For tips on who to call when you break down far from help, [Car Broke Down on Interstate in the Middle of Nowhere: Towing Cost and What to Do](/car-broke-down-on-interstate-middle-of-nowhere-towing-cost/) covers that situation in detail.

While you wait, keep your phone charged, stay hydrated if it is hot outside, and do not accept a ride from a stranger unless you are in a genuine safety emergency. If you feel unsafe at any point, call 911. That is always the right call.

## Common Questions

**Q: Can I sleep in my car on the highway shoulder while waiting for a tow?**
A: No. Sleeping in a stationary car on a highway shoulder is one of the most dangerous things you can do. Distracted or drowsy drivers drift onto shoulders constantly, and you would have no time to react. Get out of the vehicle and wait at a safe distance.

**Q: Will AAA tell me exactly how long the wait is?**
A: AAA gives an estimated ETA when you call and their app shows real-time truck tracking in most regions. It is an estimate, not a guarantee, but it is usually accurate within 10 to 15 minutes. If the truck is running very late, call back to check.

**Q: Is it legal to walk along the highway to find help?**
A: It is legal in most states but genuinely dangerous, especially on an interstate with no sidewalk or barrier. Stay put and use your phone. Walking along a highway shoulder in low visibility or at night puts you in serious danger from passing vehicles.

**Q: What if I have kids or pets in the car while waiting?**
A: Get everyone out of the vehicle immediately and move them behind the guardrail or up a safe embankment. Do not leave children or pets inside a car on a highway shoulder. If it is extremely hot, flag down a passing state trooper or call 911 to expedite help.

**Q: Does my insurance roadside assistance cover a long-distance tow if the nearest shop is far away?**
A: It depends on your policy. Many standard roadside plans cap towing at 15 to 25 miles. GEICO, State Farm, and others have specific mileage limits, and anything beyond that comes out of pocket. Check your coverage before you need it.
