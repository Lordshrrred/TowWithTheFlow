> **Quick Answer:** No. Liability-only insurance pays for damage and injuries you cause to other people, not for moving your own car. Towing for your vehicle usually requires collision coverage (after a crash) or a roadside assistance add-on (for breakdowns). If you only carry liability, you will probably pay the tow bill yourself, unless the other driver was at fault or you have a separate roadside plan.

## Does Liability-Only Insurance Cover Towing My Car?

No, liability-only insurance does not cover towing your own vehicle. Liability pays for the other party's property damage and medical bills when you cause a wreck. It never pays to repair, move, or store your car.

That includes the tow from the scene. If you hit a guardrail, slid on ice, or broke down with a dead alternator, liability pays nothing toward getting your car off the road.

The only gray area is a tow ordered by police that insurance sometimes handles when the other driver's liability policy is paying for the accident. That depends on who was at fault, covered below.

## What Do I Do Right Now If I'm Stranded With Only Liability?

Get safe first, then price out your cheapest legitimate tow. Pull as far onto the shoulder as you can, turn on your hazards, and get everyone out and away from traffic if the car can't be moved. If you're in a live lane on a highway, call 911.

Then work through these in order:

1. **Check for any other roadside coverage.** Look at your credit card benefits, your auto club membership, your car's manufacturer warranty, and your phone carrier. Some people have coverage they forgot about. Read [does my credit card cover roadside assistance towing](/does-my-credit-card-cover-roadside-assistance-towing/) before you pay out of pocket.
2. **Ask who was at fault, if there was a crash.** If the other driver caused it, their insurer may owe you the tow. More on that below.
3. **Get a quote before the truck hooks up.** Ask for the hookup fee, the per-mile rate, and the total to your destination. Get it in a text if you can. Prices can be negotiated more than people think, see [can you negotiate tow truck price](/can-you-negotiate-tow-truck-price/).
4. **Tow to a shop, not a random lot.** A tow to your mechanic or home is usually cheaper than a tow to the driver's preferred yard, and it avoids storage fees.

## Who Pays for the Tow If the Other Driver Hit Me?

The at-fault driver's liability insurance should pay for your tow, because towing counts as property damage they caused. This is the one situation where someone else's liability coverage helps you.

You can usually pay the tow yourself, keep the receipt, and submit it to their insurer as part of your property damage claim. Document everything: photos of the scene, the other driver's insurance information, and the police report number.

Expect some friction. Insurers sometimes delay payment until fault is confirmed. Our guide on [who pays for towing after a car accident that wasn't your fault](/who-pays-for-towing-after-a-car-accident-not-my-fault/) walks through how to get reimbursed.

## How Much Will I Pay Out of Pocket Without Towing Coverage?

A typical local tow runs roughly $75 to $150 for the hookup plus a few miles, then a per-mile charge after that. Flatbeds, nights, weekends, and highway recoveries cost more. Prices vary a lot by city and time of day, so treat any number as a range and always get the quote first.

Storage is where costs balloon. If the car sits at the tow yard overnight, daily fees start immediately. See [how much does an impound lot charge per day](/how-much-does-impound-lot-charge-per-day/) so you can plan to move the car fast.

If you truly can't afford it, you have options, laid out in [car broke down and no money for a tow truck](/car-broke-down-no-money-for-tow-truck-what-are-my-options/).

## Should I Add Towing Coverage or Switch From Liability-Only?

If you drive a car you can't afford to lose or can't afford to haul, adding roadside assistance is usually the cheapest fix. It's often a few dollars a month as an add-on to a policy, and it covers tows, jump starts, and lockouts that liability never will.

Full towing after a collision requires collision coverage, which is a bigger commitment and only makes sense if the car is worth repairing. For an older paid-off car, a standalone roadside plan often beats upgrading your whole policy.

## Common Questions

**Q: Does liability-only insurance cover towing after an accident I caused?**
A: No. Liability only pays for damage and injuries to other people. Your own tow, repairs, and storage are your responsibility unless you carry collision coverage or a roadside add-on.

**Q: Does liability insurance cover towing for the other driver's car?**
A: Yes, if you caused the accident. Your liability coverage pays for the other party's property damage, which can include their tow and storage, up to your policy limits.

**Q: Will my insurance pay for a tow if my car just breaks down?**
A: Not with liability-only. A breakdown tow requires a roadside assistance add-on, a membership like an auto club, a credit card benefit, or a manufacturer warranty plan.

**Q: Can I get reimbursed for a tow if someone else caused the crash?**
A: Usually yes. Pay for the tow, keep the receipt, and include it in your property damage claim against the at-fault driver's insurer along with the police report.

**Q: What's the cheapest way to cover towing going forward?**
A: A standalone roadside assistance plan or an add-on to your current policy. It costs far less than collision coverage and handles breakdown tows, which liability never covers.
