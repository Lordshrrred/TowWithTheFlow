> **Quick Answer:** If your engine seized on the highway, get off the road immediately. Shift to neutral if it's still rolling, coast to the shoulder, turn on your hazards, and stay in the car until it's safe to exit. Do not try to restart it. A seized engine means internal metal contact has stopped the rotating assembly. You need a flatbed tow to a shop. There is no roadside fix for this.

## What Does It Feel Like When an Engine Seizes at Highway Speed?

A seized engine usually announces itself with a sudden, violent lurch, a loud knock or bang, and then either the engine cuts off completely or grinds to a stop. The steering wheel may go heavy if you lose power assist. The dashboard will light up immediately, often with the oil pressure warning and check engine light together. In some cases the engine locks mid-rotation and the drive wheels resist turning, which can feel like hitting a brake hard.

If you felt a progressive rod knock for miles before this happened, the connecting rod bearing likely spun and eventually let go. That is a textbook spun bearing scenario, and it means pieces of metal are now circulating through your oil passages. Even a brief attempt to restart will cause more damage.

## What Do I Do in the First 60 Seconds After My Engine Seizes?

Shift to neutral the moment you feel the engine stop. This keeps the wheels rolling freely so you can steer to the shoulder. Do not jam the brakes in a panic. Keep both hands on the wheel, signal right, and coast as far right as you can get, ideally past the white fog line and onto the paved shoulder.

Once stopped, hazard lights on immediately. If you have emergency triangles or road flares, place them 100 to 300 feet behind your vehicle. Stay inside with your seatbelt on if traffic is fast and close. A stopped car on a high-speed highway shoulder is a real danger. Only get out if you can exit on the passenger side away from traffic and reach a guardrail or barrier. The [emergency triangle vs flares guide](/emergency-triangle-vs-flares-which-is-safer-on-highway/) covers exactly how to set these up safely.

Call 911 if you are in a dangerous spot and cannot exit the vehicle. Otherwise, call a tow truck next.

## Can I Try to Restart It or Drive It to the Next Exit?

No. Do not attempt to restart a seized engine. If the crankshaft or connecting rods have locked, the starter motor will either do nothing or snap something else. If the engine stopped due to oil starvation, running it again without oil pressure will destroy the cylinder walls and pistons in seconds. The next exit is not worth the difference between a repairable engine and a total engine replacement.

This is different from a stall caused by a fuel issue or a [fuel pump failure](/fuel-pump-failed-on-highway-signs-and-next-steps-2/), where restarting may be possible. A true seizure has physical resistance. You often cannot even turn the engine over by hand. That physical lock is your signal to stop trying.

## Do I Need a Flatbed or Will a Regular Tow Truck Work?

Request a flatbed. When an engine seizes, the transmission is often in park or the drivetrain may be partially locked depending on what failed internally. A wheel-lift truck that drags the drive wheels can damage the transmission, the axle, or what remains of the engine's rotating assembly. A flatbed loads the entire car onto the bed without turning any wheels at all.

## What Is the Repair Going to Cost, and Is the Car Worth Fixing?

A seized engine usually means a complete rebuild or a replacement engine. A rebuild on a four-cylinder runs roughly $2,500 to $4,500 in parts and labor. A used replacement engine from a salvage yard can be $1,000 to $2,500 plus installation. A new crate engine on a common platform like a 2.5L four-cylinder can push $5,000 to $8,000 installed.

The real question is what the car is worth versus what the repair costs. If the car has 180,000 miles and the body has rust, a $4,000 engine job does not pencil out. If it is a 60,000-mile vehicle with a clean body, fixing it makes more sense than replacing it. Ask the shop to do a full teardown assessment before you commit. The cause matters too: a seized engine from oil neglect is different from one that seized because of a factory defect or a faulty oil pressure sensor that gave no warning.

## Common Questions

**Q: What causes an engine to seize at highway speed?**
A: The most common cause is oil starvation. If oil pressure drops, metal parts make direct contact and friction stops the engine. A spun rod bearing, a snapped timing chain that lets valves contact pistons, or severe overheating that warps internal parts can also cause a seizure.

**Q: Is a seized engine covered by car insurance?**
A: Standard collision or comprehensive insurance does not cover mechanical failure. If your car has a manufacturer's powertrain warranty or an active extended warranty, a seizure from a defect may be covered. Roadside assistance through your insurer covers the tow but not the engine repair itself.

**Q: How do I know if the engine is seized or just stalled?**
A: Try turning the crankshaft pulley by hand using a breaker bar on the center bolt. If it will not rotate at all, the engine is seized. A stalled engine will turn freely by hand. You can also check whether the starter motor is engaging but not turning the engine versus not engaging at all.

**Q: Can a seized engine be unseized without a full rebuild?**
A: Sometimes, if caught early. A light hydraulic lock from water intrusion can sometimes be cleared. A minor seizure from temporary oil starvation where damage is limited may be partially recoverable. But on the highway at speed, by the time the engine stops, the damage is almost always severe enough to require a rebuild or replacement.

**Q: Who do I call first when my engine seizes on the highway?**
A: Call 911 first if you are in an unsafe position on the road. Otherwise, call a tow truck directly or your roadside assistance provider. Check out [who to call first when your car dies on the interstate](/car-died-on-interstate-who-do-i-call-first/) for a clear call-order breakdown.
