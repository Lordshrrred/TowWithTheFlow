> **Quick Answer:** Reflective triangles are safer for most highway breakdowns. They last indefinitely, create no fire risk, and can be placed near fuel leaks without danger. Road flares burn brighter and are more visible in heavy rain or fog, but they ignite, they burn out, and they can start fires near spilled gas. Keep triangles in the car as your standard tool. Carry flares only if you drive in rural or low-visibility areas where extra intensity matters.

## Which One Actually Works Better at Warning Drivers on the Highway?

Both work, but reflective triangles are the safer default for the vast majority of highway breakdowns. A properly placed triangle reflects headlights back at approaching drivers from a quarter mile or more in clear conditions, giving them time to move over. Road flares burn at roughly 15,000 candela and are visible in rain and fog when a triangle might not bounce enough light. The tradeoff is that flares burn for 15 to 30 minutes and then go dark, while a triangle sits there until you pick it up.

## How Far Away Should I Place These Devices from My Car?

The Federal Motor Carrier Safety Administration standard calls for placing warning devices at 10 feet, 100 feet, and 300 feet behind the disabled vehicle on a straight road. For triangles, that spacing is easy: unfold, place, repeat. For flares, that means you are walking on or near live traffic lanes to place them, which adds real personal risk. If the breakdown happens near a curve or crest in the road, push the farthest device back to 500 feet so drivers coming over the hill have reaction time.

On a highway with a 70 mph speed limit, a car travels 103 feet per second. That 300-foot marker buys drivers under three seconds. Put the farthest device as far back as you can safely reach.

## Are Flares Dangerous Near a Car That's Leaking Gas or Oil?

Yes, and this is where flares lose decisively. Gasoline vapor ignites at concentrations well below what you can smell reliably. If your car is leaking fuel after a collision or a ruptured fuel line, an open-burning flare placed anywhere near that vehicle is a serious fire hazard. Reflective triangles carry zero ignition risk. If you smell gas after a breakdown, get away from the car, do not use flares, and do not allow anyone else to light one nearby. Use triangles or, if you have neither, turn on your hazard lights and get well clear of the vehicle while you wait.

## Does the Type of Road or Weather Change Which One I Should Use?

Weather matters. In heavy rain, dense fog, or blowing snow, a flare's active burn cuts through conditions that reduce reflectivity. A wet triangle still reflects, but a film of water on the reflective surface cuts its range significantly. If you are on a rural two-lane highway in a rainstorm with no shoulder lighting and traffic coming at 60 mph, a burning flare is more likely to catch a driver's eye in time.

That said, LED emergency triangles have largely closed this gap. Newer units like the Wagan Tech LED Road Flares include built-in LEDs with a blinking mode that is visible in rain without any combustion risk. ## What Should I Actually Keep in My Car Right Now?

Keep a set of three collapsible reflective triangles in your trunk as your primary warning device. They are reusable, take up almost no space, and have no expiration date. If you regularly drive rural highways, mountain passes, or roads that see heavy fog or snow, add a package of road flares or a set of LED emergency flares as a supplement. Check your flares once a year: traditional flares degrade and can fail to ignite if they have sat in a hot trunk for years.

## Common Questions

**Q: Can I use road flares on a concrete highway without starting a fire?**
A: The flare itself will not ignite concrete, but burning slag drips from the flare and can ignite roadside grass, dry leaves, or any spilled fuel. On a clean dry pavement far from any vegetation or leak, the risk is low but not zero. Always check what is around before lighting one.

**Q: How long do reflective triangles last before I need to replace them?**
A: The reflective sheeting on a quality triangle stays effective for several years under normal use, but heat and UV exposure degrade it over time. If your triangles have lived in a hot trunk for more than five years, hold one up to headlights in the dark and check whether it still lights up brightly. Dull or faded triangles need replacing.

**Q: Are road flares legal everywhere in the United States?**
A: Traditional fusee-style flares are legal in all 50 states for emergency roadside use by private drivers. Some states have restrictions on carrying them in certain quantities in commercial vehicles, but a few flares in a personal car glove box or trunk is universally permitted for emergency use.

**Q: What if I have neither triangles nor flares when I break down?**
A: Turn on your hazard flashers immediately, that is your first move regardless. If you have a flashlight, hold it and wave it toward oncoming traffic. Move as far off the roadway as possible and stay behind the guardrail if one is present. Call 911 if you are in a position where traffic cannot see you or you cannot safely exit the car.

**Q: Do LED emergency road flares replace both traditional flares and triangles?**
A: For most drivers, yes. A set of LED emergency discs gives you visibility in rain and darkness without combustion risk, they recharge via USB on many models, and they run for hours. They do not have the raw burn intensity of a traditional flare in extreme fog, but for the overwhelming majority of highway breakdowns, LED discs are the single best tool to keep in your car.
