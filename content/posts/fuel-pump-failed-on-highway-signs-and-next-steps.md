> **Quick Answer:** A failing fuel pump on the highway typically feels like sudden power loss, engine sputtering, or the car dying and refusing to restart. Pull safely to the right shoulder immediately, turn on your hazard lights, and call for a tow. You cannot drive on a dead fuel pump, and attempting to restart it repeatedly can burn out the pump entirely if it hasn't already.

## What Does a Fuel Pump Failure Feel Like at Highway Speed?

Your engine starts sputtering like it's starving for fuel, loses power suddenly, and may die within seconds. You might also hear a high-pitched whine from the rear of the car (where the pump lives, inside the fuel tank on most modern vehicles) before it quits. The engine RPMs will surge and drop erratically right before shutdown. If the car dies and cranks without catching when you try to restart, and you have adequate fuel in the tank, a dead fuel pump is one of the most likely culprits.

Three signs that point specifically to the pump rather than something else:

- Engine dies at speed with no warning lights preceding it
- Car cranks normally but won't fire
- You can hear the pump not priming. On most cars, if you turn the key to "on" without cranking, you'll hear a faint hum from the rear for 1-2 seconds. No hum often means no pump.

## What Do I Do the Moment My Car Loses Power on the Highway?

Get to the right shoulder as quickly and smoothly as you can. You still have steering and brakes even with a dead engine, but your power assist will fade fast. Brake firmly and steer right. Signal even if you think nobody is close.

Once stopped: hazard lights on, stay in the car if you're on a busy interstate with no barrier, and call for help immediately. If you're on a rural two-lane and there's a guardrail or embankment giving you clearance, get out on the passenger side and stand away from traffic. A dead fuel pump is not something you fix on the shoulder. The pump assembly on a modern car sits inside the fuel tank and requires dropping the tank or accessing a pump access panel, neither of which is a roadside job. You need a tow.

For help choosing between a tow truck and a mobile mechanic once you're safe, see [Fuel Pump Failed on Highway: Tow Truck or Mobile Mechanic?](/fuel-pump-failed-on-highway-tow-truck-or-mobile-mechanic/).

## Can I Restart the Car and Limp to an Exit?

Probably not, and trying repeatedly makes things worse. If the pump motor has failed electrically, no amount of cranking will get it going again. If the pump is failing intermittently due to heat, a clogged fuel filter starving it, or a failing fuel pump relay, you might get one restart. But you are not getting predictable, sustained power at 65 mph.

Repeated cranking with a failing pump burns out the motor faster and runs your battery down. If the pump is making it to the engine only intermittently, you risk stalling again in a worse position, like an on-ramp or an intersection. The risk is not worth saving a tow bill.

If your check engine light came on before the car died, there may be a stored fault code like P0087 (fuel rail pressure too low) or P0230 (fuel pump primary circuit). That's useful information to give your mechanic.

## Is This Going to Be a Tow or Can Someone Bring Me a Part?

It's a tow. Fuel pump replacement is not a parking lot job in most cases. On fuel-injected cars built after the mid-1990s, the pump is a modular assembly mounted inside the tank. Access requires either removing the tank or using a pump lock ring tool to open an access hatch. Most vehicles also require depressurizing the fuel system first.

A mobile mechanic with the right parts and tools can do this job in a driveway or

## What Will This Cost Me Once I'm Off the Highway?

Fuel pump replacement typically runs $400 to $900 at an independent shop, parts and labor combined. Dealer pricing runs higher. The spread is wide because pump assemblies vary significantly: a replacement pump for a 2015 Ford F-150 3.5L EcoBoost runs around $150-$250 in parts alone, while a BMW or European import can be $400+ just for the part.

Labor runs 2-4 hours at most shops. The tank drop adds time and cost on vehicles without a top-access panel.

On top of that, you're paying for a tow. A local tow of 5-10 miles to the nearest shop might run $75-$150 in most markets. A longer haul off an isolated highway stretch costs more. Check your roadside assistance coverage before you pay out of pocket. AAA, GEICO, State Farm, and Allstate all offer towing benefits that may cover the haul to a shop, depending on your plan.

## Common Questions

**Q: My car died on the highway and I have half a tank of gas. Is it the fuel pump?**
A: It's a strong possibility. A half-full tank rules out running dry, and if the car cranks but won't start, the fuel pump (or the fuel pump relay) is one of the first things to check. Turn the key to "on" without cranking and listen for a 1-2 second hum from near the rear of the car. No hum points directly at the pump or its power supply.

**Q: Can I drive to the nearest gas station if the car restarts after sitting for a few minutes?**
A: Only if you can do it safely and the restart feels normal. Some pumps fail due to overheating and will run briefly after cooling down. If you get a restart, keep it to the nearest exit or parking lot, not back up to highway speed. A second stall at 65 mph is a real safety hazard.

**Q: How long does fuel pump replacement take at a shop?**
A: Most shops can complete it in 2-4 hours. If the tank has to be dropped and the car is older with rusted hardware, it can stretch to 5-6 hours. Call ahead with your year, make, and model so they can confirm part availability before you get towed in.

**Q: Does roadside assistance cover fuel pump failure or just towing?**
A: Roadside assistance covers the tow, not the repair. Your AAA membership or insurance add-on will pay to haul your car to a shop up to a mileage or dollar limit. The fuel pump replacement itself is on you or your auto insurance's mechanical breakdown coverage if you have it.

**Q: What causes a fuel pump to fail suddenly instead of warning me first?**
A: Most pumps do give warnings: intermittent stumbling at highway speed, hard hot-starts, or a whining noise that gets louder over time. These are easy to miss or dismiss. The pump motor itself wears out from heat, debris in the fuel, and simply running low on gas frequently (fuel cools and lubricates the pump). When the motor finally seizes or the brushes wear out, it stops without a dramatic warning.
