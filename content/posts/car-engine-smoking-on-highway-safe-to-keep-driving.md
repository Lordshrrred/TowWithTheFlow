> **Quick Answer:** No. If your engine is smoking on the highway, don't keep driving. Signal, get to the right shoulder, shut the engine off, and get everyone away from the car and the traffic lane. Smoke means overheating, burning oil, or a fluid leak, and each can wreck the engine or start a fire within minutes. Call for a tow instead of gambling on the next exit.

## Is It Safe to Keep Driving With Smoke Coming From My Engine?

No, it's not safe. Every extra mile with a smoking engine raises your odds of a fire, a seized engine, or a warped cylinder head.

Smoke is the car telling you something is failing right now. Coolant boiling off, oil dripping onto a hot exhaust manifold, or a fluid leak hitting hot metal can all produce it. You don't have time to diagnose it at 65 mph. Slow down, put your hazards on, and move toward the shoulder. Do not wait for the next exit if it's more than a minute away. A pull-off with a wide, flat shoulder beats a better location you may never reach.

## What Do I Do Right Now If My Engine Is Smoking on the Highway?

Pull onto the right shoulder, shut the engine off, and get out of the car. Do these in order:

1. Turn on your hazards and signal right.
2. Coast to the shoulder as far from traffic as you can.
3. Put the car in park, set the parking brake, and turn the engine off.
4. Get everyone out through the passenger side, away from traffic.
5. Move well off the shoulder, behind a guardrail if there is one, and stay upwind of the smoke.
6. Call 911 if you see flames or thick smoke pouring out, otherwise call your roadside provider or a tow company.

Do not open the hood right away. If there's a fire, fresh oxygen feeds it. If it's overheating, opening the hood or radiator cap can spray scalding coolant. Wait at least 30 minutes after smoke stops before touching anything under there.

## What Does the Color of the Smoke Tell Me?

White steam from under the hood usually means overheating, while blue or gray smoke points to burning oil, and black smoke points to fuel or electrical trouble.

- **White, sweet-smelling steam:** Coolant is boiling or leaking onto hot parts. Common causes are a burst hose, failed water pump, or stuck thermostat. See [radiator hose burst while driving](/radiator-hose-burst-while-driving-can-i-drive-to-shop/) for what that failure looks like.
- **Blue or gray smoke with a burning smell:** Oil is hitting the exhaust manifold, often from a leaking valve cover gasket or a loose oil filler cap. Check your oil pressure light too: [oil pressure warning light came on](/oil-pressure-warning-light-came-on-do-i-pull-over-immediately/).
- **Black smoke or an acrid, plastic smell:** Could be an electrical short or a fuel issue. Treat it as a fire risk and stay clear of the car.
- **Smoke from the exhaust pipe, not the hood:** White clouds with a sweet smell can mean a blown head gasket. See [blown head gasket symptoms](/blown-head-gasket-symptoms-should-i-tow-or-drive/).

If you do check, use a rag over the radiator cap and only open it when the engine is cold to the touch. Look at the coolant reservoir first. If it's empty, there's a leak, and topping off just dumps fluid onto the pavement a few miles later. A hose or water pump that has failed once will fail again under load.

Skip the driving attempt if you see any of these: coolant puddling under the car, oil dripping, steam returning after a restart, a temperature gauge that spikes into the red, or a burning smell. Running an overheated engine can warp the head or seize it outright, and that turns a $200 hose into a multi-thousand-dollar engine job. ## How Long Will a Tow Truck Take on the Highway, and What Will It Cost?

Highway tow response typically runs 30 to 90 minutes depending on traffic, time of day, and your location, and a short tow generally costs less than a repair that follows a bad gamble. Costs vary widely by city and distance, with a hookup fee plus a per-mile charge being the usual structure.

While you wait, stay well away from the car and traffic. Don't sit in the driver's seat on a live shoulder. Have your location ready: the highway name, direction of travel, and the nearest mile marker or exit. Your phone's map pin works too. ## Common Questions

**Q: Can I drive a car that is smoking if it's only a little bit?**
A: No. A small amount of smoke can become a large problem within a mile. Pull over, shut it off, and let it cool. Light smoke that stops after cooling still means something is leaking or failing.

**Q: How long should I wait before opening the hood on a smoking engine?**
A: Wait at least 30 minutes after the smoke stops, and never open it if you see flames. Keep your face and hands back, because coolant and steam can cause serious burns.

**Q: Is white smoke from my engine always overheating?**
A: Most of the time, yes. White steam usually means coolant is hitting hot metal. If it comes from the tailpipe with a sweet smell and your coolant keeps disappearing, a head gasket may be the cause.

**Q: Can a smoking engine catch fire?**
A: Yes. Oil or fuel on a hot exhaust manifold can ignite. If you see flames or smell burning plastic or fuel, get far from the car and call 911 immediately.

**Q: Will my insurance or roadside plan cover a tow for a smoking engine?**
A: Many roadside plans and auto policies with towing coverage do, but limits on distance and number of uses apply. Check your policy, and see [GEICO roadside assistance usage limits](/geico-roadside-assistance-how-many-times-per-year/) as an example of how those caps work.
