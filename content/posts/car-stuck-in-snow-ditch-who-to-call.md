> **Quick Answer:** Call a tow truck or roadside assistance first. If you have AAA or roadside coverage through your insurer, call them now. If not, search for a local tow company and expect to pay $75 to $150 for a basic winch-out. If the car is on a highway or your situation feels unsafe, call 911 before anything else. Stay inside the vehicle with your hazards on until help arrives.

## Who Should I Actually Call When My Car Is in a Snow Ditch?

Your first call depends on where you are and how safe you feel. If you're on a highway shoulder or in a dangerous position where traffic could hit you, call 911 first. Otherwise, your roadside assistance provider is the right call: AAA, your insurance company's roadside line, or a credit card with roadside benefits if you have one.

If you have none of those, call a local tow company directly. Search "tow truck near me" or use Google Maps. Dispatchers are used to snow calls and will ask your exact location, so know your road name and the nearest cross street or mile marker before you dial.

## Is It Safe to Stay in the Car While I Wait?

Yes, stay inside with your seatbelt on and hazard lights flashing. Getting out and standing near the road in snowy conditions is genuinely dangerous, especially on two-lane highways or interstates where visibility is already poor.

One critical check: make sure your exhaust pipe is not buried in snow. If the pipe is blocked and the engine is running, carbon monoxide will back up into the cabin. Get out briefly, clear the exhaust, then get back in. Run the heater in 10-minute cycles if you're worried about fuel, and crack a window slightly to keep fresh air moving.

## What Will It Cost to Get Pulled Out of a Snow Ditch?

A winch-out, which is what most snow ditch recoveries require, typically runs $75 to $150 for a straightforward pull on flat or gently sloped ground. If you slid down a steep embankment or the truck needs specialized equipment like a snatch block to redirect the pull, the price can climb to $200 to $300 or more.

Mileage fees apply if you need a full tow to a shop afterward, usually $3 to $7 per mile depending on your region. If you have roadside assistance through State Farm or another major insurer, check your coverage limits before assuming the whole bill is covered. [Some policies cap winch-out reimbursement](does-state-farm-roadside-assistance-cover-towing/) at a specific dollar amount, which may not be enough for a technical recovery. If you have no coverage at all, see [what roadside assistance costs without a membership](how-much-does-emergency-roadside-assistance-cost-without-membership/) before you commit to a price over the phone.

## What Information Do I Need to Give the Tow Dispatcher?

Give them your exact location first. "I'm on Route 9 heading north, about a mile past the junction with County Road 14" is far more useful than "I'm near the gas station." If you're on a numbered highway, look for green mile marker signs along the guardrail or shoulder. Those are accurate to within a few hundred feet and dispatchers use them constantly.

## Can I Try to Drive Out Myself Before Calling Anyone?

Sometimes, yes. If the car is on a gentle slope and the tires have traction, you might be able to rock it out. Alternate between Drive and Reverse in short bursts, keep the steering straight, and don't spin the tires hard. Spinning digs you in deeper and can burn through your transmission fluid in a hurry, especially in automatics.

If your tires are spinning freely with no movement after three or four attempts, stop. Sand or kitty litter under the drive wheels can help if you have it. Floormats placed under the tires also work in a pinch. But if the car is high-centered on a snow bank, meaning the undercarriage is resting on packed snow with the tires barely touching the ground, no amount of gas will get you out. That requires a winch. Similar logic applies if you [broke down on an interstate and can't move the car safely](car-died-on-interstate-who-do-i-call-first/).

## Common Questions

**Q: Will 911 send someone to pull my car out of a ditch?**
A: Not directly. Police or highway patrol will respond to make sure you're safe and may call a tow truck through their dispatch, but that truck can take much longer to arrive than one you call yourself. In some states, a police-dispatched tow can also cost more than a tow you arrange independently.

**Q: Does my car insurance cover a snow ditch recovery?**
A: Only if you have roadside assistance or comprehensive coverage with a towing rider. Liability-only policies cover nothing in this situation. Call your insurer's roadside line or check your app to confirm before you agree to a price with a tow company.

**Q: How long will I wait for a tow truck during a snowstorm?**
A: During heavy snow events, wait times can stretch to two to four hours. Tow companies get slammed with calls. Call early, stay warm, and keep the dispatcher updated on your location. Some drivers in very cold conditions also call a second company as a backup and cancel whoever arrives second.

**Q: Can a regular tow truck pull a car out of a ditch or do I need a special recovery truck?**
A: Most standard tow trucks carry a winch capable of pulling a passenger car out of a typical roadside ditch. For steep ravines, heavy trucks, or vehicles that have rolled, you may need a heavier recovery unit. Describe your situation clearly when you call so they send the right equipment.

**Q: What if my car is damaged after being pulled out of the ditch?**
A: Inspect it before the tow truck leaves. Check for bent wheels, fluid leaks under the car, and damage to the undercarriage. If there is obvious suspension or drivetrain damage, do not drive it. Have it towed to a shop and document everything with photos. If the slide was caused by another driver, get a police report at the scene.
