> **Quick Answer:** In most cases, you pay the impound release fee yourself, out of pocket. Insurance rarely covers impound storage charges directly. If another driver caused the accident that got your car towed, their liability coverage may reimburse you. If police ordered the tow, you can sometimes dispute the fee but you still pay first and fight later.

## Who Is Actually Responsible for Paying the Impound Release Fee?

The registered owner of the vehicle is responsible by default. It does not matter who called the tow or why your car ended up in the lot. The impound yard will not release the car to anyone until the fees are cleared, period.

That default shifts in specific situations: if another driver caused a crash that resulted in the tow, their at-fault liability coverage can cover reasonable towing and storage costs as part of your property damage claim. If the city or police ordered the tow due to a parking violation or roadway obstruction, local regulations may cap what the lot can charge, but you still owe it.

## Will My Car Insurance Cover Impound Lot Fees?

Standard auto insurance does not cover impound storage fees. Roadside assistance or towing coverage, which you might carry through GEICO, Progressive, State Farm, or AAA, typically covers the tow itself, not daily storage charges. Check your [GEICO roadside assistance towing coverage](/geico-roadside-assistance-towing-distance-limit/) or [Progressive towing coverage](/progressive-insurance-towing-coverage-how-much-will-they-pay/) policy documents for this distinction. They usually say something like "one tow per disablement event" with no mention of lot fees.

The one exception: comprehensive or collision claims sometimes include "reasonable towing and storage" as part of the total loss or repair settlement. Your adjuster has discretion here. Call your insurer the same day the car goes in, because storage fees compound fast, often $40 to $75 per day at municipal lots, and your insurer may only cover fees up to the date they inspected the vehicle.

## What If the Tow Was Ordered After an Accident That Wasn't My Fault?

You can seek reimbursement from the at-fault driver's insurance company. This falls under property damage liability coverage, the same coverage that pays for your car repair. Document everything: get an itemized receipt from the impound lot, note the exact date and time of tow, and submit it with your claim.

The at-fault insurer is not obligated to pay storage fees that piled up because you waited two weeks to retrieve the car. Get the car out as soon as it is safe and legal to do so. If there is a dispute about who was at fault, read through the details on [who pays tow truck cost when not at fault in an accident](/who-pays-tow-truck-cost-when-not-at-fault-accident/) before you call the adjuster.

## Can You Negotiate or Dispute an Impound Release Fee?

Yes, in some cases. Many states have laws capping what a towing company or impound lot can charge for a police-ordered tow. California, Texas, and Illinois all have rate regulations for non-consent tows, meaning the tow was ordered by police without your permission. ## What Should You Do the Moment You Find Out Your Car Was Impounded?

Call the impound lot immediately and ask for a daily itemized fee schedule. Storage fees are usually charged per calendar day, not per 24-hour period, so a car towed at 11 PM and retrieved at 9 AM the next morning may already owe two days of storage. Retrieve the vehicle as fast as possible.

Then call your insurance company and find out whether your current claim, if any, includes towing and storage coverage. If you have a roadside assistance plan through AAA or your insurer, ask them specifically: "Does my plan cover impound storage fees?" Get the answer in writing or at least noted with a claim number.

Bring these items to the lot: your driver's license, proof of registration, proof of insurance, and payment. Some lots accept credit cards. Some do not. Confirm payment methods before you drive over.

## Common Questions

**Q: Does AAA cover impound lot fees?**
A: AAA covers the tow to a location of your choice, not impound storage fees. Once the car is in a lot, daily storage charges are your responsibility unless a specific AAA plan you hold explicitly states otherwise.

**Q: How much do impound lots typically charge per day?**
A: Most municipal and private impound lots charge between $35 and $100 per day for standard passenger vehicles, with the first day often bundled into the release fee. Specialty storage for oversized vehicles costs more.

**Q: Can someone else pay and pick up my car from the impound lot?**
A: It depends on the lot and local law. Many lots require the registered owner or a person with a notarized letter of authorization to sign for release. Call ahead and ask exactly what documentation they need.

**Q: What if I can't afford the impound release fee right now?**
A: The car keeps accruing storage fees while it sits. Ask the lot if they offer a payment plan. Some do, some do not. If fees exceed the car's value and it is older, ask about a voluntary title surrender process, which releases you from further fees in exchange for signing the title over.

**Q: If my car was wrongfully towed, can I get a full refund of all fees?**
A: If a court or local authority determines the tow was unlawful, you are typically entitled to a full refund including storage. Document everything, file a complaint with your city or county, and consider small claims court for amounts under the local threshold if the towing company refuses to refund.
