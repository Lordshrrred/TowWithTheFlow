> **Quick Answer:** A grinding noise when braking almost always means metal-on-metal contact, usually worn brake pads grinding against the rotor. If the grinding is severe, the pedal feels soft or pulls to one side, or you smell burning, stop driving and call a tow. Mild grinding that just started may get you to a nearby shop, but do not push it. Brakes are not a "see how it goes" situation.

## What Is That Grinding Noise When I Hit the Brakes?

Metal-on-metal contact is the most common cause. When brake pads wear down past their friction material, the steel backing plate digs directly into the rotor. That is the grinding you hear. Other causes include a rock or road debris lodged between the pad and rotor, a seized caliper that is not releasing properly, or a rotor that has already been worn through or warped beyond use.

The sound tells you a lot. A grinding that happens only when you first move the car in the morning, then clears up, is often surface rust on the rotor burning off. That is usually harmless. Grinding that happens every single time you brake, gets louder over time, or comes with vibration through the pedal or steering wheel means something is actively failing.

## Is It Safe to Drive, or Do I Need to Pull Over Right Now?

If the grinding is new and mild, you may have a short window to get to a shop, but drive with real caution. If any of these are true, stop immediately and call a tow:

- The brake pedal feels soft, spongy, or sinks toward the floor
- The car pulls hard to one side when you brake
- You smell something burning, especially a sharp chemical or hot metal smell
- You hear or feel grinding even when you are not braking
- The grinding started suddenly after hitting a pothole or curb

A seized caliper, for example, can generate enough heat to ignite brake fluid or cause a wheel fire. If smoke is coming from a wheel well after you stop, get everyone out of the car and stay away from it. ## What Exactly Is Failing Inside My Brakes?

The most common culprit is worn brake pads. Most pads have a small steel wear indicator, a tab that is designed to contact the rotor and squeal when pads get thin. If you ignored that squeal long enough, you are now in the grinding stage where the pad's steel backing is cutting grooves into the rotor face.

Beyond worn pads, watch for these:

- **Seized caliper:** The caliper piston or slide pins freeze and keep the pad pressed against the rotor constantly. The rotor overheats, the pad destroys itself, and the whole corner of your car runs dangerously hot.

## What Will a Tow Cost If My Brakes Are Gone?

A local tow for a brake-related breakdown typically runs $75 to $150 for the first few miles, with per-mile rates of $3 to $7 beyond that. If you broke down on a highway and need a flatbed, expect the higher end of that range. If you are a AAA member or have roadside assistance through your insurer, a local tow is likely covered. Check your policy before you pay out of pocket.

Once at the shop, a full brake job on one axle with new pads and resurfaced or replaced rotors typically runs $200 to $450 depending on the vehicle. If you let it go to the point of scoring the rotor deeply, you are buying new rotors, which adds cost. Catching it at the squealing stage is always cheaper.

## Common Questions

**Q: Can I drive a few miles to the mechanic if my brakes are grinding?**
A: Only if the pedal still feels firm and the car stops straight. Drive slowly, leave extra stopping distance, and avoid highways. If anything changes, pull over immediately.

**Q: Why does my car grind when braking but the pads look fine?**
A: A seized caliper or debris between the pad and rotor can cause grinding even with pad material remaining. The pad thickness is not the only thing that matters. Have someone look at the rotor surface and caliper function.

**Q: My car pulls to the left when I brake and grinds. What is that?**
A: Pulling combined with grinding almost always means one side is doing all the work, usually a seized caliper on the side you pull toward. This is a tow situation. Uneven braking at speed can cause you to lose control.

**Q: Will grinding brakes damage anything else if I keep driving?**
A: Yes. A worn-through pad scoring the rotor can turn a $300 brake job into a $600 one fast. A seized caliper can overheat the wheel bearing, warp the hub, and in extreme cases cause a fire.

**Q: Is the grinding noise when braking always the brakes?**
A: Usually, but not always. A failing wheel bearing can also grind, though it typically changes pitch with speed rather than pressure on the pedal. A CV joint issue can produce grinding during turns. Either way, get it looked at the same day.
