> **Quick Answer:** Dealership roadside assistance is usually bundled with a new vehicle purchase or extended warranty and covers basics like towing, jump-starts, and lockouts. It is worth it only if the coverage limits match your actual driving habits and you do not already have duplicate coverage through your insurance, credit card, or AAA. Most drivers are paying for overlap they do not need.

## What Does Dealership Roadside Assistance Actually Cover?

Most dealership roadside programs cover four core services: towing to the nearest authorized dealer, battery jump-starts, flat tire changes (using your spare), and lockout service. Some add fuel delivery or winching. The key phrase is "nearest authorized dealer," which matters enormously if you break down far from home. A Ford dealership roadside plan will send your truck to the closest Ford dealer, not your personal mechanic three exits back.

Coverage terms vary by manufacturer. Toyota's roadside assistance through ToyotaCare runs two years with unlimited mileage on new vehicles. BMW's Roadside Assistance is tied to the warranty period. Hyundai bundles five years of roadside with their powertrain warranty. If you bought a used car and the dealer is selling you a separate roadside plan, you are looking at a third-party administrator, not the manufacturer, and the fine print matters more.

## How Do Dealership Programs Compare to AAA or Your Insurance?

Dealership plans are narrower and more rigid than AAA or a standalone roadside add-on from your insurer. AAA Premier, for example, covers towing up to 200 miles per incident and lets you choose your destination. A dealership plan typically limits towing to 15 to 25 miles, and only to their network shops. If your car needs a specialist or a shop you trust, you may be paying out of pocket for the extra miles.

Your auto insurance policy may already include roadside assistance for a few dollars a month. [Check whether your insurer covers towing after a non-accident breakdown](/does-insurance-cover-towing-if-car-breaks-down-not-accident/) before paying the dealer for something you already own. Credit cards are another layer of overlap worth auditing. Many Visa Signature and certain World Mastercard products include roadside dispatch at no added cost, though reimbursement limits vary. See [what credit card roadside assistance actually covers for towing](/does-credit-card-roadside-assistance-cover-towing-cost/) before you assume it fills all your gaps.

## When Is the Dealership Plan Actually the Better Choice?

It makes sense in two situations. First, if the manufacturer includes it free with a new vehicle purchase, take it. There is no cost decision to make, and it is a solid backup layer during the warranty period. Second, if you bought a luxury or specialty vehicle and the dealer's plan covers transportation to a certified shop with loaner car service, that benefit alone can justify the cost. BMW, Mercedes-Benz, and Lexus dealership programs sometimes include towing plus a loaner vehicle or hotel reimbursement if you break down far from home. ## What Are the Real Limitations You Need to Know Before You Sign?

Tow destinations are the biggest gotcha. Dealer plans almost always restrict towing to their certified repair network, and if the nearest in-network shop is 40 miles away while your trusted independent mechanic is 5 miles away, you are stuck or paying the difference. Read the towing mileage cap and the destination restriction before agreeing to anything.

Response times are not guaranteed. Dealer programs typically dispatch through third-party networks, the same contractors that insurance companies use. A rural breakdown on a busy weekend will get the same wait whether you called your dealer's hotline or your insurer. If reliable response time matters to you, [compare AAA's actual towing coverage and limits](/aaa-towing-coverage-miles-per-year-limit/) against what the dealer is selling.

Also check whether the plan covers your spouse or household drivers, or just the registered vehicle owner. Some dealership programs are vehicle-based, meaning any driver is covered. Others are person-based and exclude anyone who did not sign the contract.

## Common Questions

**Q: Is dealership roadside assistance the same as the manufacturer's roadside assistance?**
A: Not always. Manufacturer programs (like ToyotaCare or BMW Roadside Assistance) come directly from the automaker and are usually more reliable. Dealership-sold plans on used vehicles are often third-party products with different coverage terms and a separate claims process.

**Q: Can I use dealership roadside assistance to tow my car to any mechanic I choose?**
A: Usually no. Most dealer plans restrict towing to their authorized repair network. If you want to choose your own mechanic, AAA or an insurance roadside add-on gives you more flexibility on destination.

**Q: Does dealership roadside assistance cover flat tires if I have no spare?**
A: Typically no. Most plans cover changing a flat using your existing spare. If your spare is also flat or your car has run-flat tires with no spare at all, you will likely need a tow instead of a tire change, and mileage limits still apply.

**Q: What happens to my dealership roadside plan if I sell the car?**
A: Vehicle-based plans usually transfer to the new owner. Person-based plans do not. Check your contract. If you are buying a used car with a claimed roadside plan, verify whether it transferred properly or whether it expired with the previous owner.

**Q: Is it worth adding dealership roadside assistance on top of AAA?**
A: No. There is almost no benefit to carrying both. If you have AAA, it covers more mileage, gives you more destination flexibility, and works on any vehicle in your household. Paying extra for a dealer plan on top of that is wasted money.
