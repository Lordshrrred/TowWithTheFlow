> **Quick Answer:** Stop driving. Signal, get to the right shoulder as fast as safely possible, shut the engine off, and get everyone out and away from the road. Smoke under the hood means overheating, a fluid leak, or an electrical fault, and driving on can destroy the engine or start a fire. Do not open the hood until the smoke stops. Call for a tow.

## Should I Stop or Keep Driving When My Car Is Smoking Under the Hood?

Stop. Do not try to make the next exit unless it is within a few hundred feet and you can reach it without braking hard or crossing lanes.

Smoke means something is overheating or burning. A cooling system failure can warp the cylinder head in minutes. A fluid leak onto a hot exhaust manifold can ignite. Every extra mile risks turning a repair into a replacement.

Do this in order:

1. Turn on your hazards and move toward the right shoulder.
2. Ease off the gas and coast. Do not slam the brakes.
3. Shift to park (or neutral and set the parking brake in a manual), then shut the engine off.
4. Get everyone out the passenger side, away from traffic.
5. Stand well back from the car, behind the guardrail if there is one.

## What Does the Color of the Smoke Tell Me?

The color points to the likely cause, but it does not change what you do next: stop and shut it off. Here is how to read it:

- **White or gray, sweet smell:** Coolant on a hot engine. Think a burst radiator hose, a failed water pump, or a leaking radiator. If your temp gauge was climbing before the smoke, this is almost certainly it. Our guide on [car overheating on the freeway](/car-overheating-on-freeway-should-i-pull-over-or-keep-driving/) covers what happens inside the engine.
- **Blue or blue-gray:** Burning oil. Often a leaking valve cover gasket dripping onto the exhaust manifold, or an overfilled crankcase.
- **Black:** Rich fuel burn or burning rubber and plastic. A shorted wire or a failing component can smoke black. Treat this one as a fire risk.
- **Thin wisps with a burning smell:** Could be a slipped belt, a seized accessory, or an electrical short. See [car making a burning smell after driving](/car-making-burning-smell-after-driving-should-i-pull-over/).

Steam from a radiator leak looks like smoke but disappears quickly and smells sweet. Smoke lingers and stinks. Either way, you are done driving.

## What Do I Do After I've Pulled Over?

Leave the hood closed until the smoke stops and the engine has cooled, at least 30 minutes. Opening it feeds the fire oxygen if there is one, and you can get scalded by pressurized coolant.

Never open the radiator cap on a hot engine. The system runs at roughly 15 psi and sprays boiling coolant.

While you wait:

- Call for help. If you see flames, smell fuel, or the smoke is thick and growing, call 911 first.
- Stay at least 100 feet from the car if you suspect fire.
- Do not stand between your car and traffic.
- Check your location on a highway sign or mile marker so a dispatcher can find you.

## Can I Add Water and Drive Again After It Stops Smoking?

No. Adding water might get you a mile or two, but if the smoke came from a coolant leak, it pours out again and the engine overheats within minutes. If it was oil on the exhaust, water fixes nothing.

Once the engine is cold, you can check the coolant reservoir and oil dipstick. A bone-dry reservoir or a dipstick with no oil on it tells you the car is not drivable. But even if levels look fine, you do not know what failed. Skip the guessing and tow it. More detail in [adding water to a leaking cooling system](/car-leaking-coolant-on-side-of-road-can-i-add-water-and-drive/).

The one exception: a small amount of smoke that clears in under a minute after you add oil to a car that was low. Even then, drive straight to a shop, not home.

Yes, tow it. A flatbed is the safest choice because it keeps a damaged engine and drivetrain from turning. Not sure what you need? Use [this flatbed checklist](/how-to-know-if-my-car-needs-a-flatbed-tow/).

## Common Questions

**Q: Is it safe to drive a car that is smoking from under the hood?**
A: No. Smoke signals overheating, a fluid leak, or an electrical fault. Driving on risks a warped cylinder head, a seized engine, or a fire. Pull over and shut the engine off.

**Q: How long should I wait before opening the hood?**
A: Wait until the smoke fully stops and at least 30 minutes have passed. Never open a hot radiator cap. If you saw flames, do not open the hood at all. Call 911.

**Q: Can a car catch fire from smoke under the hood?**
A: Yes. Oil or fuel dripping onto a hot exhaust manifold, or a shorted wire, can ignite. If you see flames or smell raw fuel, get away from the car and call 911.

**Q: What if the smoke stops after a few minutes?**
A: The cause is still there. Smoke stopping just means the part cooled or the fluid burned off. Do not restart the engine. Tow it and have it diagnosed.

**Q: Will my insurance cover the tow if my engine smokes?**
A: If you have roadside assistance through your insurer or a club like AAA, a breakdown tow is typically covered up to a mileage limit. Basic liability coverage alone usually does not include towing. Check your policy before you call.
