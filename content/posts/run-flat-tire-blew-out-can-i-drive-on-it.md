> **Quick Answer:** Maybe, but only if it's a true run flat tire that lost air through a puncture and the sidewall is intact. Run flats can go about 50 miles at no more than 50 mph after losing pressure. If the tire blew out, shredded, or separated from the rim, do not drive. Stop in a safe spot and call for a tow.

## Can I Drive on a Run Flat Tire That Blew Out?

Only if the tire went flat from a puncture and is still in one piece. A tire that truly blew out, meaning the sidewall split, the tread peeled off, or the bead came off the rim, cannot be driven.

Run flat tires have reinforced sidewalls that hold the car up with zero air. Most manufacturers rate them for roughly 50 miles at up to 50 mph. That limit assumes the tire is damaged but whole.

Check these before you decide:

- **Sidewall:** A bulge, gash, or torn rubber means stop driving.
- **Tread:** Missing chunks or exposed belts mean stop driving.
- **Rim:** If the tire is off the rim or the wheel is bent, stop driving.
- **Smell and sound:** Burning rubber or a loud flapping means the tire is still coming apart.

If the tire looks flat but intact, the TPMS light is on, and the car drives straight, you have a short window to reach a shop.

## What Do I Do Right After the Blowout?

Grip the wheel with both hands, ease off the gas, and do not slam the brakes. Let the car slow on its own, then steer gently toward the shoulder.

Sudden braking after a blowout is how drivers spin out. Keep the steering straight and let speed bleed off before you touch the pedal.

Once you're stopped:

1. Turn on your hazard lights.
2. Move as far from traffic as you can. On a highway like I-70 or I-95, that means well right of the white line.
3. Stay in the car with your seatbelt on if traffic is close. Exiting on the traffic side is the most dangerous move you can make.
4. Look at the tire from a safe angle to see whether it's intact or shredded.

## How Far Can I Actually Go on a Run Flat?

About 50 miles at 50 mph or less, and that's the ceiling, not a promise. Your owner's manual gives the exact figure for your tires, and some run flats are rated lower.

Several things shorten that distance:

- **Heavy load.** A full trunk or passengers cut your range.
- **Heat.** Hot pavement and summer temperatures break down a deflated sidewall faster.
- **Speed.** Going 60 or 70 mph can destroy the tire in a few miles.
- **Distance already driven flat.** If the tire has been low for a while before you noticed, the sidewall may already be cooked.

Drive to the nearest tire shop, not your favorite one across town. Keep it slow, avoid potholes, and skip sharp turns.

## Should I Just Call a Tow Instead?

Yes, if the tire is shredded, you're on a fast or dangerous road, or the nearest shop is farther than you can safely go. A tow costs far less than a wrecked wheel, a damaged suspension, or an accident.

Call for a tow when:

- The sidewall is split or the tread is gone.
- The wheel looks bent or the car pulls hard to one side.
- You're more than 30 miles from a shop that stocks your size.
- It's dark, raining, or you don't feel steady driving on it.

Driving on a destroyed tire grinds the rim down to metal, and a bent or cracked wheel can run hundreds of dollars to replace. Many roadside plans cover this, so check whether [roadside assistance covers a flat tire or just towing](/does-roadside-assistance-cover-flat-tire-or-just-towing/) before you pay out of pocket.

## Can I Put a Regular Tire On a Run Flat Car?

You can fit a standard tire, but you give up the run flat safety net. Many run flat cars have no spare tire and no jack, so a standard tire on one corner means you're driving with no backup.

Some points to know:

- Mixing run flats and standard tires on the same axle can change handling.
- Cars with run flats often have no spare well, so a flat on a standard tire leaves you stranded.
- Run flats should not be repaired if you drove on them flat, since heat damage inside the sidewall is invisible. Most tire shops replace them.

If a shop wants to patch a run flat you drove on deflated, ask how far and how fast you went. Past the 50 mile mark, replacement is the safe call.

## Common Questions

**Q: How far can you drive on a run flat tire after it goes flat?**
A: About 50 miles at no more than 50 mph, but check your owner's manual for your exact rating. Heavy loads, heat, and speed all cut that distance.

**Q: Can you repair a run flat tire after it's been driven flat?**
A: Usually not. Driving on a deflated run flat can damage the inner sidewall in ways you can't see, so most shops will not patch it and will recommend replacement.

**Q: Why did my run flat tire blow out if it's supposed to be puncture proof?**
A: Run flats resist going flat, but they are not blowout proof. Impact damage, old age, curb strikes, or driving on them while underinflated can still split the sidewall.

**Q: Do run flat tires come with a spare?**
A: Most cars with run flats have no spare tire and no jack. If you shred one, you'll need a tow or mobile tire service rather than a roadside swap.

**Q: Will AAA or my insurance cover a run flat tire blowout?**
A: Roadside plans often cover a tow or a spare change, but not the replacement tire itself. Replacement is usually your cost unless you bought road hazard or tire protection coverage.
