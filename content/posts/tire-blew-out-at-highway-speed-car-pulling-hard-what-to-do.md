> **Quick Answer:** Do not brake hard and do not jerk the wheel. Grip the steering wheel firmly with both hands, ease off the gas, and steer straight. Let the car slow on its own, then signal and move gradually toward the shoulder. Once stopped completely and safely off the road, turn on your hazard lights and call for help. Do not attempt to change the tire on an active highway lane.

## What Do I Do in the First 10 Seconds After a Blowout?

Keep the wheel straight and do not lift off the gas all at once. That sounds wrong in the moment, but a sudden hard deceleration after a blowout causes the car to pull even harder toward the flat and can send you spinning.

Here is the sequence:

1. Grip the wheel with both hands. Squeeze, don't yank.
2. Hold your lane. Correct small drifts with small inputs only.
3. Ease off the accelerator gradually over three to five seconds.
4. Let engine braking and road friction slow you down.
5. Once you are below 30 mph, apply the brakes gently and steer toward the shoulder or an exit ramp.
6. Hazard lights on as soon as you have the car under control.

The pulling sensation you feel is the shredded tire creating massive drag on one corner of the car. It is not a steering failure. The car is controllable if you do not overcorrect.

## Why Is the Car Pulling So Hard After the Blowout?

The pull comes from the blown tire generating friction drag on one side while the opposite tires roll freely. On a front blowout, the pull is stronger because the front wheels steer the car. On a rear blowout, you will feel the back end swing or fishtail. A rear blowout on a rear-wheel-drive vehicle is the most dangerous scenario because the driven wheels are also the compromised ones.

If the car is pulling hard left, the left front or left rear tire blew. Same logic applies to the right. This tells you which corner to avoid loading when you stop, and which direction to steer slightly to compensate while slowing down.

## Is It Safe to Change the Tire Myself on the Highway Shoulder?

Changing a tire on a highway shoulder is one of the most dangerous things a driver can do, and you should avoid it unless you are on a low-speed rural road with clear sightlines and minimal traffic. Move-over laws in every U.S. state require drivers to shift over or slow down for stopped vehicles on the shoulder, but not every driver does.

If you have a full-size spare and you are well off the road on a wide shoulder with good visibility, changing the tire yourself is possible. Use wheel wedges or at least your remaining lug nuts to block movement, jack on solid frame points (not the pinch welds), and stay on the passenger side of the car, away from traffic. If the shoulder is narrow, skip the change. Call a tow.

If you have no spare at all, you do not have a decision to make. Get off the wheel and call for help. For more detail on what to do without a spare, see [Blew a Tire on the Highway with No Spare: What to Do Right Now](/blew-a-tire-on-highway-no-spare-what-to-do-now/).

## How Do I Stay Safe While Waiting on the Shoulder?

Get everyone out of the car and away from the traffic side, every time. Standing between your car and the guardrail is far safer than sitting inside a vehicle that could be rear-ended at highway speed.

Put distance between yourself and the car. At least 50 feet in front of or behind the vehicle, on the far side of a guardrail if one is present. Keep your phone with you.

## Do I Need a Tow After a Blowout?

Yes, in most cases. A blown tire shreds the rubber, and in many cases damages the wheel itself. Driving on a flat, even to the next exit, destroys the rim, bends the brake rotor, and can collapse the wheel entirely into the road. That turns a $200 tire replacement into a $1,000 repair.

If you have a full-size spare in good condition and can safely change it off the highway, you may not need a tow. A donut spare gets you to a tire shop, but keep your speed under 50 mph and do not use it for more than 50 miles total.

If you drove any distance on the flat before stopping, assume the wheel needs inspection before you put weight on it. Request a flatbed tow rather than a wheel-lift tow if you suspect rim damage, since lifting by that axle can cause additional damage. You can check [how to tell if your car needs a flatbed tow](/how-do-i-know-if-my-car-needs-a-flatbed-tow/) if you are unsure what to ask for.

## Common Questions

**Q: Should I brake hard when a tire blows out at highway speed?**
A: No. Hard braking after a blowout shifts weight onto the blown corner and worsens the pull, which can cause a spinout. Ease off the gas gradually, let the car slow itself, then apply light brake pressure only once you are well below highway speed.

**Q: Can I drive to the next exit on a blown tire?**
A: Only if the exit is extremely close, you are going slow, and the road is clear. Even a quarter mile at highway speed on a flat destroys the rim and can cause the tire to separate entirely. Stopping on the shoulder and calling a tow is almost always the better call.

**Q: What causes a tire to blow out at highway speed?**
A: The most common causes are underinflation over time, which weakens the sidewall until it fails under heat and load, impact damage from a pothole or road debris that was not visible, and tires that are past their service life. Tires older than six years should be inspected even if they look fine, because the rubber degrades internally.

**Q: Will my insurance cover the tow after a tire blowout?**
A: Roadside assistance coverage through your insurer or a membership like AAA typically covers the tow. Standard liability-only policies do not. Call your insurer from the shoulder to confirm before you authorize a tow from an unknown provider, since rates vary significantly.

**Q: The car pulled hard and then I heard metal grinding after I stopped. What does that mean?**
A: Metal grinding after a blowout usually means the rim contacted the pavement while the tire shredded. The wheel may be cracked or severely bent. Do not drive on it. You need a flatbed tow and a shop inspection before putting any load on that corner of the car.
