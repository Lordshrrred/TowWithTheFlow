> **Quick Answer:** Call 911 first if on a major bridge or highway. They'll dispatch state police who handle bridge emergencies. For smaller bridges, call local police non-emergency line, then your roadside assistance. Get out of traffic immediately and stay behind barriers.

## What To Do

1. **Turn on hazard lights immediately** and try to reach the shoulder or emergency lane if possible. Even rolling 20-30 feet further can move you out of a live traffic lane. Don't overcorrect or brake hard to do it.

2. **Call 911 if you're on a major bridge or interstate.** State police handle these emergencies and coordinate with bridge authorities. They know which tow trucks are authorized for bridge work. Give the dispatcher the bridge name, your direction of travel, and any mile markers you can see.

3. **For local bridges, call local police first** using their non-emergency number if it's not immediately dangerous. They'll tell you if special procedures apply. A two-lane county bridge has very different protocols than a suspension span on an interstate corridor.

4. **Exit your vehicle only if it's safe.** Stay behind concrete barriers or guardrails, never between your car and traffic. If your car is blocking a lane and you cannot move it, stay inside with your seatbelt on until help arrives. A moving vehicle hitting a stationary car is survivable. A pedestrian struck at highway speed is not.

5. **Call your roadside assistance or insurance** after notifying authorities. Tell them you're on a bridge so they send an authorized operator. Sending an unlicensed tow truck to a restricted bridge can result in the driver being turned away, which wastes time when you're exposed.

6. **Note your exact location.** Look for bridge name signs, mile markers, or cross streets to tell emergency responders. On major spans, there are often emergency call boxes every few hundred feet. Use them if your phone has no signal.

7. **Stay visible** but safe. Wear bright clothing if you have it, but don't sacrifice safety to retrieve items from your car.

## Bridge-Specific Rules

Many bridges have special towing regulations. Only certain tow trucks are licensed to work on bridges, especially drawbridges or major spans. Regular tow services might not be allowed. This is not bureaucracy for its own sake. Bridge decks are rated for specific load limits, and an overweight or improperly rigged truck can cause structural stress or block emergency lanes in ways a standard road breakdown cannot.

Some bridges require police escorts for towing operations. Others have dedicated emergency response teams stationed at or near the span. Drawbridges add another layer: if your breakdown stops bridge traffic, the operator may not be able to open the bridge for marine traffic until your car is cleared, which can involve port authority coordination as well.

Bridge authorities often monitor major spans with cameras and may already know about your breakdown before you call. On well-monitored bridges, expect a response in under 10 minutes. On rural or older infrastructure, response times can stretch to 30-45 minutes, so staying calm and positioned safely matters more.

If your car breaks down mid-span on a drawbridge and the bridge needs to open, authorities can legally require emergency repositioning of your vehicle even if it means towing without your full consent.

## Common Questions

**Q: Can I just call any tow truck when my car breaks down on a bridge?**
A: Not always. Many major bridges only allow tow operators who are pre-approved and licensed for that specific span. Calling an unauthorized tow truck means they may be turned away at the bridge entrance, leaving you waiting longer while exposed to traffic. Always call police first and let them dispatch the right operator.

**Q: What if I break down on a bridge and my phone has no signal?**
A: Look for orange or yellow emergency call boxes mounted along the railing or on support structures. Most major bridges have them spaced every few hundred feet. Press the button and stay on the line. If no call box is visible and traffic has stopped behind you, it is safer to stay in your car with your seatbelt on than to walk along the bridge deck searching for a signal.

**Q: Will my roadside assistance cover a bridge tow?**
A: It depends on your plan. Many standard roadside plans cover towing up to a set dollar amount, often $75-100, which will not come close to covering a bridge tow on a major span. Specialty equipment, permits, and escort fees push costs higher. Call your provider from the scene and ask specifically about bridge coverage so you know what out-of-pocket costs to expect before you authorize the tow.

## What It Might Cost

Bridge towing typically costs more than regular roadside service:
- Standard bridge tow: $150-300
- Major span or specialty bridge: $300-500+
- After-hours or emergency rates: add 50-100%

Your roadside assistance may not cover the full cost if special equipment or permits are required.

## Stay Safe

- Never attempt repairs on a bridge
- Don't walk in traffic lanes under any circumstances
- Stay behind barriers, not on the shoulder if possible
- Keep car doors locked if staying inside
- Have your phone ready with location details
- Don't accept help from random strangers on bridges
