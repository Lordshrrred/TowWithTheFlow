> **Quick Answer:** Most tow trucks arrive within 30 to 90 minutes under normal conditions. Peak hours, bad weather, rural roads, and high-demand events can push that to 2 to 3 hours or longer. If you're on a high-speed highway shoulder, that wait becomes a genuine safety issue. Know your options before you call, and know what to do while you wait.

## How Long Does a Tow Truck Actually Take to Get to You?

The honest range is 30 minutes on the short end, 2 to 3 hours when things go sideways. A tow through AAA during rush hour on a Friday afternoon in a busy metro will take longer than a call to a local independent tow company at 10 a.m. on a Tuesday. Dispatch has to locate an available driver, route them to you, and navigate the same traffic you're sitting in. Rural calls are even slower because trucks may be dispatched from a town 20 to 30 miles away.

For a deeper look at AAA-specific wait times, [How Long Can You Wait for a AAA Tow Truck](/how-long-can-you-wait-for-aaa-tow-truck/) breaks down real numbers by region and time of day.

## What Makes the Wait Shorter or Longer?

Several factors move that needle a lot:

- **Location.** A breakdown on I-95 near a major interchange puts you close to multiple tow yards. A breakdown on a two-lane state highway at midnight puts you at the back of a very short list.
- **Time of day.** Morning rush, Friday afternoon, and post-storm windows all overload dispatch. Tow companies run fixed fleets and cannot conjure extra trucks.
- **Your service provider.** An independent tow company you call directly often beats a roadside assistance network because there is no relay. Roadside assistance networks like AAA or your insurer's dispatch center have to assign a contractor, and that contractor may already have a job.
- **Weather.** A single ice storm or heavy rain event will generate dozens of simultaneous calls. Waits can triple.
- **Vehicle type.** A standard sedan needs a standard flatbed or wheel-lift. A pickup truck, SUV, or all-wheel-drive vehicle may need a flatbed specifically, and those are fewer in number.
- **The nature of your breakdown.** If your car is blocking a lane, dispatch will often flag it as a priority because police can request expedited service. If you're safely off the road on a wide shoulder, you go in the queue like everyone else.

## Is It Safe to Wait on the Highway Shoulder for That Long?

Not always. A highway shoulder is one of the most dangerous places to stand next to a car. Distracted drivers drift onto shoulders regularly, and a waiting driver or passenger standing behind or beside a vehicle is at serious risk. The rule is simple: get as far off the road as possible. If there is a guardrail, get on the other side of it. If there is an exit ramp or a parking area within walking distance, use it.

Turn on your hazard lights the moment you stop. If you have road flares or reflective triangles, place them 50 to 100 feet behind the vehicle. Stay in the car with your seatbelt on if you cannot get clear of traffic. A seatbelt in a parked car sounds unnecessary until a car clips yours from behind at 65 mph.

Give exact location details. A highway exit number or mile marker is more useful than a street name. On interstate highways, green mile-marker signs appear every mile, and they are the fastest way to give a tow driver your precise position. If your wait is approaching two hours with no update, call back. Dispatch queues get reshuffled. Drivers finish earlier-than-expected jobs and become available. A second call often moves you up.

## Should You Call Your Insurance or a Tow Company Directly?

It depends on your coverage. If you have roadside assistance through your insurer, AAA, or a credit card, use it first because there may be no out-of-pocket cost. But if wait times are running long or your coverage is limited, calling an independent tow company directly gets you a truck faster at a known price. Paying out of pocket for a local tow typically runs $75 to $125 for the hookup fee plus $3 to $7 per mile. If your coverage limit won't stretch far enough, it can make sense to pay part and claim part.

## Common Questions

**Q: How long is too long to wait for a tow truck on the highway?**
A: More than 90 minutes on a live highway shoulder is a serious safety concern. If your wait is extending past that and you feel unsafe, call 911 and ask for a non-emergency police assist. Officers can help manage traffic around your vehicle and sometimes expedite a tow.

**Q: What happens if the tow truck never shows up?**
A: Call your roadside assistance provider back and get a new ETA. If they cannot give you one, cancel and call a local independent tow company directly. Document the failed dispatch in writing because you may be able to get a reimbursement if you pay out of pocket as a result of their failure.

**Q: Can I leave my car on the shoulder and come back later?**
A: You have a short window, typically 24 to 48 hours before police or highway patrol will begin the process of having it removed as an abandoned vehicle. For specifics on that clock, [How Long Can You Leave a Broken Down Car on the Highway](/how-long-can-you-leave-broken-down-car-on-highway/) has the real numbers.

**Q: Does calling AAA versus my car insurance change how long I wait?**
A: Often yes. AAA maintains its own contractor network and has dedicated dispatch. Insurance-based roadside assistance routes through a third-party dispatch service that may have fewer local contractors available. In rural or low-coverage areas, AAA tends to have a slightly wider network, but neither is immune to long waits during high-demand periods.

**Q: What should I do if my car is smoking or overheating while I'm waiting for the tow?**
A: Get everyone out of the vehicle immediately and move away from the car, especially if you see smoke from the engine bay or smell burning. Do not open the hood if you see steam.
