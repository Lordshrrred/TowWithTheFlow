> **Quick Answer:** Do not open the hood yet. Turn off the engine, get yourself and passengers away from the car, and call for a tow. White or gray smoke usually means coolant burning off from an overheated engine. Blue or black smoke points to oil or fuel burning. Either way, driving it further is the wrong call. Wait for the engine to cool at least 30 minutes before touching anything under the hood.

---

## Is the Smoke a Sign the Car Is About to Catch Fire?

Most roadside engine smoke is not a fire, but you treat it like one until you know otherwise. Steam from an overheated cooling system looks like thick white or grayish smoke and has a sweet smell. That is not flames, but it is still serious. Actual fire smoke is dark gray or black, smells acrid, and may be accompanied by visible flames near the firewall or wheel wells.

If you see orange flames or smell burning plastic or rubber, get everyone out immediately, move at least 100 feet from the car, and call 911. Do not try to grab belongings. Do not open the hood, because fresh oxygen feeds a fire.

If it looks like steam and smells sweet, you likely have an overheating or coolant problem. Still get out of the car and stay away from the engine bay until things cool down.

---

## What Should I Actually Do in the First Five Minutes?

Pull as far off the road as possible, turn on your hazard lights, and shut the engine off. That is the sequence. Once stopped, get passengers out of the car on the side away from traffic and move to a guardrail or embankment, not the roadway shoulder.

Do not open the hood. Pressurized coolant can spray at temperatures above 200 degrees Fahrenheit and cause serious burns. The radiator cap and overflow reservoir are under pressure when hot.

Do not restart the engine to "just move it a little." Running a seized or overheated engine compounds the damage fast, and you risk a cracked block or warped head gasket.

Call a tow truck. This car is not driving anywhere safely. While you wait, turn on your hazard lights if you have not already, and if you have road flares or triangles, place them 100 to 200 feet behind the car. If you are on a busy highway and feel unsafe outside, get back in the car with your seatbelt on and stay against the passenger side away from traffic lanes. For more on staying safe roadside, see [how long can you wait on the side of a highway for a tow](/how-long-can-you-wait-on-side-of-highway-for-tow/).

## What Is Actually Causing the Smoke Under My Hood?

The color and smell tell you a lot. White or gray steam with a sweet or neutral smell almost always means the coolant system failed: blown radiator hose, failed thermostat, cracked overflow reservoir, or a blown head gasket letting coolant into the combustion chamber. This is the most common cause of roadside engine smoke.

Blue smoke means burning oil. Common culprits are worn valve seals, a blown PCV valve, or a cracked turbo oil line. If your oil pressure warning light came on before the smoke appeared, a failing oil pump or catastrophic oil loss may be involved. Do not run the engine even briefly. See [engine oil pressure warning light came on while driving](/engine-oil-pressure-warning-light-came-on-while-driving/) for a full breakdown of that scenario.

Black smoke with a fuel smell means unburned fuel is burning off somewhere it should not be. That can be a stuck fuel injector, a fuel line leak near a hot exhaust component, or a failing fuel pressure regulator.

Any of these situations means the car needs diagnosis before you drive it. None of them resolve themselves on the roadside.

## Should I Open the Hood to Check Coolant or Oil?

Wait at least 30 minutes after the engine shuts off before you touch anything. After that, use a cloth or glove to slowly open the hood latch. Stand to the side when you pop the hood in case steam releases.

Never remove the radiator cap when the engine is hot. Check the plastic coolant overflow reservoir instead. It is

If the smoke was minimal and the engine has fully cooled, check the oil dipstick. Oil that looks milky or foamy indicates coolant mixing with oil, which is a head gasket symptom and means the car needs a trailer, not a top-off.

If everything looks full and normal but you still saw significant smoke, the problem may be external: oil dripped on a hot exhaust manifold, a coolant hose sprayed coolant onto the engine block, or a plastic bag melted onto the catalytic converter. That last one is more common than you think and looks alarming but is not engine damage.

## Do I Need a Tow or Can I Drive It After It Cools Down?

You need a tow for any of the serious causes listed above. Driving a car with a blown head gasket, cracked radiator hose, or active oil leak will turn a $500 repair into a $4,000 engine replacement in under 20 miles.

The only scenario where you might carefully drive it: the smoke was clearly from an external source like a melted plastic bag on the exhaust, the engine cooled completely, all fluid levels are normal, no warning lights are on, and the smoke does not return when you restart. Even then, drive straight to a shop, not back to your normal routine.

If your coolant was low and you topped it off and the engine cooled, understand that something caused it to be low. There is a leak somewhere. You may get home, but the problem is not fixed.

Call a tow, get it to a mechanic, and let them tell you what failed. Depending on your coverage, roadside assistance through AAA or your insurer may cover the tow. ## Common Questions

**Q: Can I pour water on the engine to cool it down faster?**
A: No. Pouring cold water on a hot engine block or exhaust manifold can crack the metal due to thermal shock. Let it cool on its own. It takes 30 to 45 minutes for most engines to drop to a safe temperature after shutdown.

**Q: My car overheated but the coolant reservoir looks full. What does that mean?**
A: A full reservoir with overheating symptoms often points to a failed thermostat that is stuck closed, a blown head gasket letting combustion gases into the coolant, or a clogged radiator. Full coolant does not mean the cooling system is working. See [car overheating but coolant is full](/car-overheating-but-coolant-is-full-should-i-tow-it/) for the next steps.

**Q: White smoke came from under the hood but stopped after a minute. Do I still need a tow?**
A: Yes. A brief burst of white steam usually means a coolant hose sprayed coolant onto a hot surface. The steam stopped because the coolant burned off, not because the problem went away. The leak is still there and will empty your cooling system if you keep driving.

**Q: The smoke smelled like something burning but not coolant. What should I check?**
A: A burning rubber or plastic smell without sweet notes usually means a belt, a plastic debris melt on the exhaust, or an electrical short. A belt shredding can also take out your power steering, alternator, and water pump at the same time. Check your [serpentine belt condition](/serpentine-belt-snapped-while-driving-emergency-steps/) if that system is involved.

**Q: How much will a tow cost if I am stuck on a highway?**
A: A standard tow from a highway to a nearby shop typically runs $75 to $150 for the first five miles, then $3 to $7 per additional mile, depending on your state and the time of day. After-hours and weekend calls cost more. Your roadside assistance plan may cover it entirely.
