> **Quick Answer:** You cannot drive a car with a snapped timing chain. The engine will not run, and if it did run briefly, the damage would be catastrophic. Valves and pistons lose synchronization the moment the chain breaks, often bending valves or cracking pistons on interference engines. Shut the car off immediately if it is still running, get it off the road, and call a tow.

## What Actually Happens When a Timing Chain Snaps?

The timing chain keeps your camshaft and crankshaft rotating in sync. When it snaps, that sync disappears instantly. On an interference engine (which includes most modern four-cylinder and V6 engines, including popular ones like the GM Ecotec 2.4L and many Honda K-series motors), the pistons and valves now share the same space with nothing coordinating them. Valves get bent. Pistons crack. In some cases the chain whips through the timing cover and you hear a loud bang followed by a dead engine. On a non-interference engine the damage is less severe, but the car still will not run.

Either way, the engine stops. There is no "limp home" mode with a snapped timing chain.

## Is There Any Chance I Can Still Drive It to the Shop?

No. If the engine is dead, the car is not going anywhere under its own power. If by some rare chance the engine is still running after a partial chain skip (the chain jumped teeth but has not fully snapped), stop immediately. A chain that has already skipped will fully let go soon, and every second you run that engine risks bending additional valves, scoring cylinder walls, or seizing the motor entirely. The repair bill can go from $1,500 to $5,000 or higher depending on the damage. Driving it even a quarter mile can push a repairable engine into junk-engine territory.

Pull over, turn the key off, put on your hazard lights.

## How Do I Know It Was the Timing Chain and Not Something Else?

A snapped timing chain usually announces itself in one of two ways: a sudden loud rattling or slapping from the front of the engine followed by the engine dying, or the engine just cutting out completely with no warning. You may hear a bang. The car will crank but not start, and it will often crank unusually fast because there is no compression. That fast, easy cranking with no firing is a strong indicator. Compare this to a dead battery, which gives you slow or no cranking at all. If it cranks fast and freely but will not fire, the timing chain (or another timing component like a snapped belt) is high on the list.

An oil pressure warning light that came on before the failure is another clue. A stretched or worn timing chain often chatters on cold starts before it finally goes, so if you had that rattle for a few weeks, this is likely the cause. See also our post on [oil pressure warning light came on while driving](/oil-pressure-warning-light-came-on-driving-what-to-do/) for related symptoms that show up before major engine failures.

## What Is This Going to Cost to Fix?

Timing chain replacement on a typical four-cylinder or V6 engine runs $800 to $2,000 in labor and parts if the chain snapped without causing secondary damage. That number climbs fast if valves are bent. Bent valves on an interference engine typically add $1,000 to $3,000 depending on how many cylinders were affected and whether the head needs machining or replacement. In the worst cases, total engine damage pushes the repair cost past the value of the car. Get the engine inspected by a trusted shop before authorizing any work, and ask specifically about valve and piston damage before you agree to a repair quote.

## Common Questions

**Q: Can a snapped timing chain destroy my whole engine?**
A: Yes, on an interference engine it often does. Valves and pistons occupy overlapping space when timing is lost, and high-speed contact bends valves and can crack pistons or damage cylinder walls. The extent of the damage depends on engine RPM at the moment the chain snapped.

**Q: My car is still cranking but won't start. Is the timing chain definitely broken?**
A: Fast, easy cranking with no start is a strong sign. Compression relies on correct valve timing, and without it the engine has nothing to ignite. A compression test at the shop will confirm it quickly.

**Q: How long does timing chain replacement take at a shop?**
A: Plan on four to eight hours of labor minimum. Timing chains sit deep in the front of the engine and require removing covers, tensioners, guides, and sometimes the front of the engine accessories. Some vehicles take longer, particularly V8s and certain transverse-mounted V6s.

**Q: Can I get this covered under my car insurance or roadside assistance?**
A: The tow itself may be covered if you have roadside assistance through your insurance. The engine repair is a mechanical failure and is not covered by standard auto insurance. It may be covered under an extended warranty if you have one. Check your policy before you pay out of pocket for the tow.

**Q: Will AAA tow my car with a snapped timing chain?**
A: Yes. AAA responds to mechanical breakdowns, not just accidents. Depending on your membership level, they will tow you a set number of miles for free and charge a per-mile rate beyond that.
