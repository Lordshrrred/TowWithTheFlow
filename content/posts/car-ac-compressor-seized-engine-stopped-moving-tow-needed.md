> **Quick Answer:** A seized AC compressor locks up the serpentine belt, which stops the engine dead. Your car is not driveable. Turn it off immediately if it is still running, get off the road safely, and call a tow. Attempting to drive it risks snapping the belt entirely or damaging the alternator, power steering pump, and water pump on the same belt loop.

## Did My AC Compressor Actually Seize or Is Something Else Going On?

A seized AC compressor has a specific signature: the engine suddenly bogs down, then stalls or stops completely, often right after the AC kicks on or during a hot idle. You may hear a loud squeal or grinding screech for a second or two before everything goes quiet. If the engine dies and you notice the AC was running hard beforehand, a locked compressor is the most likely cause. Overheating and fuel delivery failure can also stall an engine, but those usually come with warning lights, rough running, or a gradual loss of power. A compressor seize tends to be sudden and total.

Check for a burning rubber smell under the hood. That is the serpentine belt skipping or slipping over the frozen compressor pulley. If you see belt debris on the ground or the belt is visibly shredded, that confirms it.

## Is It Safe to Keep Driving or Do I Have to Stop Right Now?

Stop driving immediately. A seized compressor that has already stalled your engine means the serpentine belt may be moments away from breaking completely, if it has not already. That single belt drives your alternator, water pump, and power steering on most modern vehicles. If the belt snaps while you limp to a shop, your engine will overheat within minutes from loss of coolant circulation, and the repair bill goes from an AC compressor job to a potentially warped cylinder head. Pull to the shoulder, turn on your hazard lights, and do not restart the engine to "test" it repeatedly. Every restart attempt with a seized compressor grinds the belt further.

If you are on a highway, get as far right as possible. Stay in the car if traffic is fast and moving. For more on what to do while you wait, [car broke down on highway at night has safety tips that apply any time of day](/car-broke-down-on-highway-at-night-safety-tips/).

## What Do I Do Right Now While I Wait for the Tow?

Get safe first, then make your calls. Here is the sequence:

1. Pull completely off the travel lanes. Parking lot, shoulder, exit ramp, anywhere away from moving traffic.
2. Hazard lights on immediately.
3. If you are on a highway shoulder, exit the vehicle from the passenger side when safe and stand behind a guardrail or well away from the road. Do not stand between your car and traffic.
4. Call a tow. If you have AAA, call them. If you have roadside assistance through your insurance, call that number. If you are paying out of pocket, expect a local tow to run $75 to $150 for the first few miles, more if you need a flatbed or a longer haul to a specific shop.
5. Let the tow driver know the car may not start. They need to know upfront so they bring the right equipment.

Do not try to disconnect the AC compressor yourself on the side of the road to "bypass" it. That bypass requires removing the belt and installing a shorter bypass belt sized for your specific vehicle. It is a shop job, not a shoulder job.

## What Is This Going to Cost to Fix?

The tow is the immediate cost: $75 to $150 for a local pull is typical, more for distance or after-hours. The compressor replacement itself is where the real money is. An AC compressor on a mid-size sedan like a Honda Accord or Toyota Camry runs $300 to $600 in parts alone. Labor adds $150 to $400 depending on where you take it. Most shops also recommend replacing the receiver-drier and expansion valve at the same time, plus a full refrigerant recharge, which can push the total job to $800 to $1,200 or more. If the belt shredded before you stopped, add a serpentine belt to the invoice, which is usually a $50 to $150 repair but critical.

Ask the shop specifically whether the compressor failure sent metal debris into the AC system. A compressor that seizes and fails catastrophically can send aluminum shavings through the condenser and evaporator, which means flushing or replacing those components too. Get that answer in writing before approving work. ## Common Questions

**Q: Can I just turn off the AC and drive to the shop without a tow?**
A: Not safely. If the compressor has already seized and stalled the engine, the belt is under extreme stress. Restarting and driving risks snapping the belt entirely, which kills your water pump and causes rapid overheating. Call a tow.

**Q: Will my insurance pay for this tow?**
A: If you have roadside assistance coverage or a comprehensive policy with towing benefits, yes, the tow is usually covered. The mechanical repair to the compressor is not covered by standard auto insurance, only by a vehicle service contract or extended warranty if you have one.

**Q: How do I know if my serpentine belt is already broken?**
A: Open the hood and look at the front of the engine. If the belt is missing, hanging loose, or visibly shredded, it is gone. Also check your dashboard: a broken belt will kill the alternator immediately, so the battery warning light will be on.

**Q: Can the tow truck driver tell me if the compressor is really the problem?**
A: Most tow drivers can identify obvious signs like a shredded belt or a locked pulley, but they are not diagnosing your car. A shop needs to confirm the failure, check for debris in the AC system, and assess belt and pulley condition before you approve repairs.

**Q: Is it worth fixing a seized AC compressor or should I just bypass it?**
A: A bypass using a shorter serpentine belt is a real option if you want to get the car running without AC, and some shops offer it cheaply. But you lose air conditioning entirely, and in summer heat that is a serious comfort and safety issue. Get a quote for both options and decide based on the car's age, mileage, and overall condition.
