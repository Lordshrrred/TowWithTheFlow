> **Quick Answer:** A tow truck typically arrives in 30 to 60 minutes under normal conditions. In a city during business hours, you might see one in 20 to 30 minutes. At night, in rural areas, or during bad weather, that stretches to 90 minutes or more. AAA and private companies both follow this same rough pattern. Call, get an ETA, then focus on staying safe while you wait.

## How Long Will I Actually Wait for a Tow Truck?

Most drivers wait between 30 and 60 minutes from the moment they call. That is the realistic middle ground for a suburban or urban breakdown during normal traffic conditions. Rush hour, storms, or a high-volume dispatch day can push that past 90 minutes without much warning.

Here is what actually controls your wait:

- **Your location.** A breakdown on I-95 near a major metro will get a truck rolling faster than a breakdown on a state highway outside a small town. Population density drives truck availability.
- **Time of day.** Tow companies run smaller overnight crews. A 2 a.m. call in a mid-size city can mean a 60 to 90 minute wait even if nothing else is wrong.
- **Weather and accidents.** A single multi-car accident on a freeway can tie up every truck in a 20-mile radius for hours. Snowstorms, ice events, and flooding do the same thing, and every car that slides off the road ahead of you is in line before you.
- **Who you call.** AAA typically dispatches through a contracted local company. That adds a layer. A private tow company you call directly cuts out the middleman, which sometimes shaves 10 to 20 minutes. Your insurance roadside assistance line works the same way AAA does, with similar wait variability.

If the dispatcher gives you an ETA of 45 minutes and it has been over an hour, call back. Do not assume they forgot you, but do not assume they are almost there either.

## Is It Safe to Wait on the Side of the Road That Long?

Get off the roadway immediately. Do not stay in the driver's seat on a highway shoulder waiting for your phone to ring.

The shoulder is one of the most dangerous places to stand or sit in a vehicle. If you can move your car further from traffic, even onto an exit ramp or into a parking lot, do it. Turn on your hazard lights the moment you stop. If you have road flares or triangles, place them at least 200 feet behind your vehicle so approaching drivers have time to react.

If you are on a highway and cannot safely exit the vehicle on the driver's side, exit from the passenger side and move up the embankment or behind a barrier. Sitting inside a stopped car on a live shoulder puts you at risk from distracted or drowsy drivers, especially at night.

Yes, it often does. When you call AAA or your insurance's roadside line, they take your information, then dispatch a contracted local tower. That relay adds time. You are not calling a truck directly. You are calling a call center that calls a truck.

## What Should I Do While I Wait for the Tow Truck?

Confirm the address or cross street you gave the dispatcher, then stay accessible by phone. Towers will call when they are close, and if you do not answer, some will move on to the next call.

A few things worth doing during the wait:

- Text someone your location and the situation. If something changes, they know where you are.
- Keep your hazards on. Do not turn them off to save battery.
- If your car has an oil leak, coolant leak, or you saw smoke from the hood, do not restart the engine to run the heat or AC while you wait. [Smoke from under the hood is a sign that running the engine can make things significantly worse.](https://towwiththeflow.com/smoking-from-hood-stopped-on-side-of-road-what-to-do/)
- Stay on the opposite side of a guardrail from traffic if you are on a highway with no shoulder.

## Common Questions

**Q: Can I track my tow truck after I call?**
A: Some companies and roadside apps like AAA's mobile app offer real-time GPS tracking of your truck. If yours does not, call the dispatcher after 30 minutes and ask for an updated ETA directly.

**Q: What if the tow truck doesn't show up within the estimated time?**
A: Call the dispatch number back, not the original roadside assistance line if they are separate. Ask for the driver's contact number or a revised ETA. If you used a third-party service and the wait has exceeded 90 minutes past the original estimate, you have every right to cancel and call another company.

**Q: Does being on a highway get me a faster tow than a parking lot?**
A: Not automatically, though a highway breakdown is considered higher urgency and some dispatchers prioritize it. Parking lots are lower risk, so they may get a lower priority when trucks are stretched thin.

**Q: Will the tow truck driver call me before arriving?**
A: Most will call when they are 10 to 15 minutes out. Make sure your ringer is on and your phone is charged. If your battery is low, turn off apps and screen brightness to extend it.

**Q: Does bad weather make tow trucks harder to reach or just slower to arrive?**
A: Both. Ice storms and floods increase the number of calls dramatically while also slowing trucks down on the road. During a major winter event, wait times can realistically hit two to three hours in cities that do not have heavy-duty tow capacity ready to scale up.
