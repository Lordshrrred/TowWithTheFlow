> **Quick Answer:** Get an itemized invoice before you pay anything more. Compare it to your quote, your motor club or insurance coverage, and any state or city towing rate rules. If it doesn't match, pay under protest if you need the car back, then dispute in writing to the tow company, your credit card issuer, and the state or local agency that regulates towing.

## Can I Get My Car Back If I'm Disputing the Charge?

Yes, but you usually have to pay first and fight second. A tow yard can generally hold your car until the bill is settled, and every day it sits adds storage fees that make the dispute more expensive.

If the amount is clearly wrong but you can't afford a standoff, pay by credit card, not cash or debit. Ask for a signed, itemized receipt. Write "paid under protest" on the paperwork if they'll let you, and note the date and time.

Many states require tow yards to release personal items and medications from the vehicle even if you haven't paid. Ask for them. Photograph the car, the lot, and the posted rate sign before you leave.

## How Do I Know If I Was Actually Overcharged?

You were overcharged if the invoice doesn't match what you agreed to, or if it includes fees your state or city doesn't allow. You can't tell without an itemized bill, so demand one first.

Check these against the invoice:

- **The quote.** If the dispatcher said a price on the phone, that's your baseline. Any text, email, or call record backs you up.
- **Mileage.** Compare their billed miles to the real distance on a map app. Padded mileage is common.
- **Hook-up and "after-hours" fees.** Some are legitimate, some are stacked on top of each other. See [what weekend and night towing normally costs](/how-much-does-emergency-towing-cost-on-weekends/) before you assume a surcharge is fake.
- **Storage.** Daily storage rates and when the clock started should match posted rates and the time of your tow.
- **Services you didn't need.** A flatbed charge for a car that didn't need one, or labor fees for a simple hook-up. Our guide on [how to know if your car needs a flatbed](/how-to-know-if-my-car-needs-a-flatbed-tow/) helps you judge that.

Heavy-duty or winch-out charges can also be legitimate if the car was stuck or off the pavement.

## What Are the Steps to Dispute an Overcharge?

Dispute in writing, in this order, and keep copies of everything. Phone calls leave no proof.

1. **Request the itemized invoice** and the company's license or permit number if you don't have it.
2. **Email or send a certified letter** to the tow company. State the charge in question, what you were quoted, and the amount you want refunded. Set a deadline, such as 10 days.
3. **Dispute the card charge** if you paid by credit card and they don't respond. Your issuer will ask for the invoice, the quote, and your written complaint. This is often the fastest way to get money back.
4. **File a complaint with the regulator.** Towing is regulated at the state, county, or city level, depending on where you live. Search your state's name plus "towing complaint" or check the state attorney general's consumer protection page. ## What If My Car Was Towed Without My Permission?

Non-consensual tows, like private-lot or parking tows, are the most tightly regulated and the easiest to dispute. Many jurisdictions cap the fees and require visible signage, a notice to police, and fast release of the car.

Look for these violations: no warning signs at the lot, no report filed with local police within the required window, charges above the posted or legal maximum, or a yard that refuses a card payment when the law requires accepting one.

If the tow was from an accident scene or breakdown on a highway, the rules differ. Our article on [what happens if a tow truck damages your car](/what-happens-if-tow-truck-damages-my-car/) covers the separate problem of damage claims, which is a different dispute from an overcharge.

## Should I Check My Insurance or Roadside Coverage First?

Yes, before you argue with the tow company, check whether you were owed a covered tow. If your auto policy or motor club covers towing and you paid out of pocket, you may be able to get reimbursed.

Call your insurer or club and ask what your coverage limit is and whether they reimburse tows from a company they didn't dispatch. If the bill exceeded the covered amount, you may still owe the difference, but the covered portion shouldn't come out of your pocket. See [whether your insurance covers towing after an accident you caused](/does-my-car-insurance-cover-towing-if-i-cause-the-accident/) for how limits and fault interact.

Keep your policy declarations page handy when you call.

## Common Questions

**Q: Can a tow company charge more than the price they quoted me?**
A: Generally not without your agreement. If the final bill is higher than a verbal or written quote, dispute it and use any text, email, or call record as proof. Add-on fees you weren't told about are the most common grounds for a successful dispute.

**Q: How long do I have to dispute a towing charge?**
A: It varies by state and by payment method. Credit card chargebacks typically have a limited window from the statement date, so act within days or a couple of weeks, not months. Don't wait on the tow company's response before starting the card dispute.

**Q: Can a tow yard refuse to give me my belongings?**
A: In many states, no. They must let you retrieve personal property like medications, ID, child car seats, and documents without paying the full bill. Ask calmly, and cite your state's towing law if they refuse.

**Q: Should I pay with cash or card if I think I'm being overcharged?**
A: Card, preferably credit. Cash leaves you almost no recourse, while a credit card lets you dispute the charge through your issuer. Always get an itemized, signed receipt.

**Q: Who regulates tow companies if I want to file a complaint?**
A: It depends on your location. It may be a state agency, a county, or a city. Check your state attorney general's consumer protection page or your city's towing ordinance, and search for "towing complaint" plus your state.
