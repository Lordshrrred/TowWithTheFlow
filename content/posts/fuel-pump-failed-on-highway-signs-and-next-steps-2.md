> **Quick Answer:** A failing fuel pump usually gives you warning: engine sputtering at highway speed, loss of power on acceleration, a whining noise from the rear of the car, or the engine dying and refusing to restart. When it quits completely, coast to the right shoulder immediately, turn on your hazards, stay in the car, and call for a tow. This is not a roadside fix.

## What Does a Failing Fuel Pump Feel Like at Highway Speed?

The most common sign is a sputtering or surging engine when you hold steady throttle around 60 to 70 mph. The car feels like it hiccups, almost like it briefly ran out of fuel, then catches again. You may also notice the engine losing power on uphill grades or during hard acceleration, even though you have fuel in the tank. A high-pitched whine coming from near the rear axle, where the fuel pump sits inside the tank on most modern cars, is another early red flag. If you hear that whine getting louder over several days of driving, the pump is wearing out.

If the pump quits entirely, the engine will die as if you turned off the key. It may restart once or twice before refusing to turn over at all. That no-start after highway stalling is the clearest sign you are dealing with a fuel delivery problem rather than an ignition or battery issue.

## What Do I Do the Moment My Car Starts Dying on the Highway?

Get to the right shoulder. The second you feel the engine losing power, signal right and start coasting. Do not brake hard unless you have to. You still have steering and brakes even with a dead engine, but you lose power assist quickly, so move while you still have momentum. Once stopped, turn on your four-way flashers immediately.

After that:

- Stay inside the car with your seatbelt on if you are on a high-speed road with no guardrail separation from traffic.
- If you are on a bridge, in a tunnel, or on a curve with no visibility, [your priority is getting as far off the roadway as possible](/car-broke-down-in-tunnel-what-to-do/), even if it means walking along the guardrail to a safer spot.
- Call 911 if you are in a genuinely dangerous spot. Call a tow service for a standard breakdown.
- Do not attempt to restart the engine repeatedly. You may burn out the fuel pump relay or cause vapor lock, and if the pump is already dead, cranking accomplishes nothing.

Do not try to pour fuel into the throttle body or add any additives. A failed fuel pump is a mechanical part failure. It requires replacement, not a workaround.

## Can I Drive It to a Shop or Do I Need a Tow?

You need a tow. A car with a dead fuel pump will not move under its own power, full stop. Even if it restarts temporarily, driving on a failing pump risks leaving you stranded in a worse location, such as an active travel lane or an intersection. The pump itself can fail completely mid-restart, and repeated cranking with a weak pump accelerates the damage.

Have the car flatbedded if possible. Fuel pump replacement requires dropping the fuel tank on most vehicles, so the shop will need it there intact. ## How Much Does Fuel Pump Replacement Actually Cost?

The part and labor typically run between $400 and $900 at an independent shop. Dealer pricing can push past $1,000. The wide range comes down to where the pump is mounted. On most fuel-injected cars built after the mid-1980s,

If your shop quotes you a price, ask specifically whether the fuel strainer and filter are included. Replacing those at the same time while the tank is already out is cheap insurance against doing this job twice.

## How Do I Know It Is Actually the Fuel Pump and Not Something Else?

A mechanic will check fuel pressure at the rail using a gauge before condemning the pump. A healthy fuel pump on a typical port-injected engine holds somewhere in the range of 35 to 65 psi depending on the system, while a direct-injection system can run 2,000 psi or higher at the high-pressure pump. Low or zero pressure at the rail with the engine cranking points directly at the pump or the fuse and relay that power it.

Before assuming the worst, a good tech will also check the fuel pump fuse and relay first. Those are $5 to $15 parts and take five minutes to test. A blown fuse from a short, or a failed relay, produces the exact same symptoms as a dead pump. If either checks out fine and pressure is still low, the pump is the diagnosis.

A check engine light may or may not be present. Codes like P0087 (fuel rail pressure too low) or P0230 (fuel pump primary circuit malfunction) point toward fuel delivery, but a pump can fail without triggering any codes at all if it simply stops spinning. ## Common Questions

**Q: Will a bad fuel pump always give warning signs before it dies completely?**
A: Usually, but not always. Most pumps degrade gradually and sputter at highway speed before quitting. Some fail suddenly with no prior symptoms, especially if a relay or wiring connection fails rather than the pump motor itself.

**Q: Can I replace a fuel pump myself on the side of the road?**
A: No. It requires dropping the fuel tank, which needs a lift or at minimum jack stands on level ground, plus fuel line disconnect tools and proper ventilation. This is a shop job, not a roadside repair.

**Q: Why did my car restart once and then die again a few miles later?**
A: A failing pump often delivers just enough fuel to restart when the engine is cool or when you give it a few minutes. Heat soaking inside the tank can cause an intermittent pump to fail again quickly once you get back up to speed. Do not trust that restart. Get off the road.

**Q: Is it safe to wait in my car on the highway shoulder for the tow?**
A: Generally yes, with your seatbelt on and hazards running. If you are on a narrow shoulder, past a blind curve, or near a high-speed merge lane, standing outside the guardrail is actually safer than sitting in the car. Use your judgment based on the specific spot you are in.

**Q: How long will a tow truck take to reach me on a highway?**
A: In a metro area, typically 30 to 60 minutes. On a rural interstate stretch, plan for 60 to 90 minutes or longer. Call dispatch and ask for an estimated arrival time, then use 511 or your state DOT app to check if there are incidents that might delay them.
