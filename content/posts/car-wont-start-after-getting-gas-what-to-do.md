> **Quick Answer:** Your car likely isn't starting due to vapor lock, a flooded engine from overfilling, or contaminated fuel. Wait 10-15 minutes before trying again. Check that your gas cap is properly tightened and you didn't accidentally put diesel in a gas car.

## What To Do

1. **Wait it out first.** Turn off everything (AC, radio, lights) and wait 10-15 minutes. Vapor lock from hot fuel lines often resolves itself as things cool down. This is especially common in summer heat or after short trips where the engine never fully cooled between fills. Modern fuel-injected engines are less prone to classic vapor lock than older carbureted ones, but hot fuel rails can still cause hard-start conditions that disappear on their own after a brief cooldown.

2. **Check your gas cap.** Remove it completely and reinstall until you hear 2-3 clicks. A loose cap triggers the evaporative emission system and prevents starting. Some vehicles will also throw a check engine light for this. If your cap feels cracked or the rubber seal looks dried out, a replacement cap runs about $10-20 at any auto parts store and is worth keeping as a spare.

3. **Try the flooded engine procedure.** Press the gas pedal to the floor and hold it there while cranking the starter for 10-15 seconds. This clears excess fuel from the cylinders. Do not pump the pedal while cranking. Holding it steady tells the engine management system to cut fuel injection, giving the cylinders a chance to clear. If the engine fires briefly and dies, repeat the procedure once more before moving on.

4. **Verify you used the right fuel.** If you accidentally put diesel in a gas car, stop immediately. Do not keep trying to start it. Even a small amount of diesel can clog fuel injectors and damage the high-pressure fuel pump. The fix involves draining the entire tank and flushing the fuel system, which is why wrong-fuel removal costs run $200-500. The reverse situation, gasoline in a diesel, is equally serious and causes injector damage fast under compression.

5. **Check for contaminated fuel signs.** If the engine sputters, runs rough, or dies immediately after starting, you may have gotten bad gas. This happens more at older or low-traffic stations where fuel sits in underground tanks for weeks. Water contamination is the most common culprit and typically shows up as rough idling, misfires, and stalling. Phase separation in ethanol-blended fuels is another issue, especially after rain or humidity shifts.

6. **Look under the car.** Fresh fuel puddles indicate a leak that could have been caused by overfilling or damaged fuel lines. A small amount of fuel smell right at the filler neck immediately after filling is normal as vapors escape. A visible puddle is not normal and means you should not attempt to start the car at all until it is addressed.

7. **Try a jump start.** Gas station stops often coincide with a weak battery finally giving up. The fuel pump draws significant power during startup, typically 4-8 amps continuously, and a battery that is borderline will sometimes fail under that load even if the engine turns over slowly. If the starter sounds sluggish or clicks, the battery is the first thing to rule out.

8. **Call for help if nothing works.** Don't keep cranking. You'll flood the engine further or drain your battery completely. Most roadside assistance services can reach you within 30-60 minutes, and a tow to a shop beats a seized starter or a fire from persistent cranking with fuel present.

## Common Questions

**Q: How long should I wait before trying to start again if I think the engine is flooded?**
A: Wait at least 10-15 minutes with everything off to let excess fuel evaporate from the cylinders. After that, use the flooded engine procedure (pedal to the floor, crank for 10-15 seconds) rather than normal starting. If it still won't fire after two attempts, stop cranking and call for help.

**Q: Can bad gas from a station ruin my engine, or does it just cause a no-start?**
A: Bad gas usually causes a rough-running engine, misfires, or stalling rather than outright engine damage, but prolonged use of contaminated fuel can clog fuel injectors and the fuel filter. If you suspect a full tank of bad fuel, a professional fuel system cleaning for $150-300 is worth it before the problem compounds.

**Q: What happens if I keep cranking when I smell gas strongly?**
A: Stop immediately. Strong fuel smell means raw fuel is present near hot engine components or electrical systems, and continuous cranking creates ignition risk. Get out of the car, move away from the vehicle, and call for roadside assistance rather than troubleshoot further yourself.

## Stay Safe

- Never smoke or use your phone near spilled fuel
- Turn off your engine while diagnosing fuel-related issues
- Don't keep cranking if you smell gas strongly
- Move away from the pumps if possible before troubleshooting
- Keep windows down to prevent fume buildup inside the car
