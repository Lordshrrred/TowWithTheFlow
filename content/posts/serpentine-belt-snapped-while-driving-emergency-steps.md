> **Quick Answer:** Pull off the road immediately. A snapped serpentine belt kills your power steering, disables your alternator, and will cause your engine to overheat within minutes once the water pump stops spinning. Turn on your hazards, coast to a safe stop, and do not restart the engine. This car is not driveable. Call a tow.

## What Just Happened to My Car and Why Did It Happen?

Your serpentine belt is a single ribbed rubber belt that runs the alternator, power steering pump, water pump, and AC compressor simultaneously. When it snaps, all of those systems stop at once. You likely felt sudden heavy steering, saw the battery warning light and temperature gauge spike at the same time, and may have heard a loud slap or squeal from under the hood just before.

The most common causes are age and wear (most belts are rated for 60,000 to 100,000 miles), a seized tensioner pulley, or a failed idler pulley that put uneven load on the belt. If the belt looked cracked or glazed at your last oil change and nobody flagged it, this is why.

## What Do I Do the Moment the Belt Snaps?

Get off the road now. Steering feels heavy because the power steering pump is dead, but the car still steers, it just takes more effort. Signal, check your mirror, and move to the right shoulder or the nearest parking lot. Do not try to limp it to a shop.

Once stopped, turn off the ignition immediately. The water pump is belt-driven on most gasoline engines, which means coolant stopped circulating the instant the belt snapped. Continuing to run the engine will overheat it fast, and serious overheating can warp the cylinder head or crack the block. Restart only to inch forward if your safety requires it, then shut it off again.

Turn on your hazard lights. If you are on a highway, stay in your car with your seatbelt on until help arrives. If you stopped on a narrow shoulder with traffic close, exit from the passenger side and move behind the guardrail. ## How Long Can I Wait Before the Engine Gets Damaged?

The overheating risk is immediate once the engine is running without a belt. With the engine off and sitting still, there is no further damage happening. The belt itself is already gone, so the clock is only ticking if you keep the key turned.

Do not let anyone talk you into nursing it down the road. Even a quarter mile can push coolant temps past 240 degrees Fahrenheit on a warm day, which is enough to cause head gasket failure. The repair bill for a snapped belt and pulley swap is usually $150 to $400 in parts and labor depending on the vehicle. A blown head gasket runs $1,500 to $3,000. Turn the car off and wait for the tow.

## What Does the Repair Actually Involve and Can I Drive After?

A serpentine belt replacement is a straightforward job. The mechanic removes the damaged belt, inspects every pulley (tensioner, idler, and accessory pulleys) for damage or bearing wear, and installs a new belt to spec. On most vehicles this takes under an hour.

Here is what to insist on: have the tensioner and idler pulleys inspected before the new belt goes on. A seized tensioner is often what caused the belt to fail in the first place, and putting a new belt on a bad tensioner means you are back on the side of the road in short order. A new belt is roughly $25 to $75. Adding a tensioner pulley might run another $50 to $150 in parts. Pay for the inspection now and skip the second breakdown.

## Common Questions

**Q: Can I drive my car if the serpentine belt snapped?**
A: No. Without the belt, your water pump, alternator, and power steering pump are all offline. Driving even a short distance risks overheating the engine and causing damage that costs far more than the belt repair.

**Q: How long does a serpentine belt replacement take?**
A: On most cars it takes 30 to 60 minutes at a shop. If the tensioner or idler pulleys also need replacement, add another 15 to 30 minutes. It is not a complex job, but it should not be rushed.

**Q: Will a snapped serpentine belt cause my battery to die?**
A: Yes, quickly. The alternator charges the battery while the engine runs. With the belt gone, the alternator stops and the car runs entirely on battery power. Expect the battery to drain within minutes of continued driving, which is another reason to stop immediately.

**Q: How much does it cost to replace a serpentine belt after a breakdown?**
A: The belt itself is typically $25 to $75 in parts. Labor brings the total to $100 to $350 at most independent shops. Dealer rates run higher. If the tensioner pulley also failed, budget up to $500 for the full repair.

**Q: How do I know if my serpentine belt is about to fail before it snaps?**
A: Look for visible cracks, fraying on the edges, or a glazed shiny surface on the ribbed side. A squealing noise at startup, especially in cold or wet weather, is another common warning. Most manufacturers recommend inspection every 30,000 miles and replacement by 100,000 miles regardless of appearance.
