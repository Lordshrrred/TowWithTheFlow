> **Quick Answer:** Get off the travel lanes immediately. If the car moves, roll it as far onto the right shoulder as possible, past the white fog line. Turn on your hazard lights the second something feels wrong. Once stopped, stay inside with your seatbelt on, call 911 or a tow service, and keep your interior lights off so you can see outside. Do not stand between your car and traffic.

## What Do I Do the Moment I Realize My Car Is Failing on the Highway?

Start steering toward the right shoulder right now, even if the engine is still running or limping. Coasting to a complete stop while you're still in a travel lane is one of the most dangerous things you can do at night, because drivers behind you cannot judge your speed until they're very close.

Hit your hazard lights before you've even fully decided to pull over. The moment something feels wrong, flashing lights buy you reaction time from other drivers. Then aim for the right shoulder and get as far from the travel lane as the terrain allows. If there's an exit ramp within sight, take it. A parking lot, a gas station, a frontage road: any of those are safer than the highway shoulder.

If your car dies completely in a lane, put it in neutral and try to coast to the right. Do not abandon it in the travel lane.

## Is It Safer to Stay in the Car or Get Out?

Stay inside the car in most situations, with your seatbelt on. At night on a highway, the biggest threat to your life is not someone approaching your car on foot, it is getting struck by passing traffic. The car's steel structure protects you from a glancing blow that would kill you standing outside.

There are two exceptions. If your car is on fire, get out immediately and move well away from the vehicle at an angle away from traffic, not parallel to the lanes. If you've been rear-ended and another impact is likely (such as a multi-car pileup developing), exit from the side away from traffic and get behind a guardrail if one exists.

Otherwise: seatbelt on, hazard lights flashing, and stay put until help arrives. Keep your doors locked. You can crack a window to talk to someone without fully unlocking.

## How Do I Signal for Help and Who Do I Call?

Call 911 first if you feel unsafe, if you're injured, or if your car is in or near a live traffic lane. A highway patrol unit can close a lane or put out flares, which dramatically cuts your risk. If you're safely on the shoulder, call your roadside assistance provider or a tow company directly.

For signaling: your hazard lights are your primary tool. If you have road flares or LED triangles in your trunk, place them at least 200 feet behind your car along the shoulder, not in the lane. A white cloth or piece of paper tied to your driver's side door mirror or antenna is a recognized distress signal that highway patrol officers are trained to look for.

If you have no phone, [here's how to signal for help without one](/how-to-signal-for-help-car-breaks-down-no-phone/). Many states also have highway emergency call boxes at regular intervals along rural interstates.

Do not walk down the highway to find help. The risk of being struck while walking on a dark highway shoulder is severe.

## How Long Will I Be Waiting, and What Should I Do While I Wait?

Typical wait times for a tow truck at night run 45 minutes to over two hours depending on your location and provider. Rural stretches of highway, anything more than 30 miles from a metro area, consistently push toward the longer end. ## What If I Don't Have Roadside Assistance or AAA?

You can still get a tow without a membership. Call any local tow company directly. A highway tow at night typically runs $75 to $150 for a short haul to the nearest town, though after-hours and after-midnight calls sometimes carry a surcharge of $25 to $50.

Your auto insurance policy may include roadside assistance even if you didn't realize it. Check your insurer's app right now. GEICO, State Farm, and Progressive all offer it as an add-on, and [some credit cards also include roadside coverage](/does-credit-card-roadside-assistance-cover-towing-cost/) at no extra charge.

If you genuinely have nothing and no money, 911 will still respond. Officers cannot tow your car for free in most states, but they can stay with you until help arrives and are often willing to help you locate a tow company.

## Common Questions

**Q: Should I leave my car running while I wait for a tow at night on the highway?**
A: Only if you need heat or AC for safety, like in extreme cold or heat. If your car broke down due to an overheating engine, oil pressure warning, or burning smell, do not run it. Running a mechanically compromised engine can turn a repairable problem into a total loss.

**Q: Is it safe to accept a ride from a stranger who stops to help?**
A: Use your judgment, but it is generally safer to stay in your locked car and wait for police or a tow than to get in a stranger's vehicle at night. If you feel you are in immediate danger and someone trustworthy stops, that changes the calculation. Call 911 instead whenever possible.

**Q: What if I break down on a bridge or in a tunnel at night?**
A: Get your hazard lights on and call 911 immediately. These locations have no safe shoulder and officers need to respond fast. Do not exit the vehicle if you're stopped in a tunnel; exhaust fumes build up and traffic has nowhere to go around you.

**Q: My car died in a live lane and I can't get it to the shoulder. What do I do?**
A: Turn on hazards, stay in the car with your seatbelt on, and call 911 right away. Do not try to push it alone in the dark. Give the dispatcher your exact location including the highway number, direction of travel, and the nearest landmark or mile marker. Stay on the line.

**Q: Will a tow truck come to a highway at night, or do I need to get to an exit first?**
A: Tow trucks respond to highway breakdowns directly all the time. Give the dispatcher the highway number, mile marker if you can see one, and which direction you're traveling. They're used to it. You do not need to walk to an exit.
