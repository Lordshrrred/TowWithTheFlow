> **Quick Answer:** Yes, in most cases you need a tow. Pull over immediately, shut the engine off, and do not drive further until the temperature gauge drops and you know why it overheated. If the gauge hit the red, coolant is leaking, or steam is coming from the hood, call a tow truck. Driving a hot engine even a few miles can turn a $300 repair into a $5,000 engine replacement.

## Should I Pull Over Right Now or Can I Make It to an Exit?

Pull over now. Do not gamble on reaching the next exit. Once the temperature gauge enters the red zone, you have minutes before heat damages the cylinder head gasket or warps the aluminum head itself. Neither of those is a roadside fix.

Put your hazard lights on, move to the right shoulder as smoothly as you can, and shut the engine off. If you are stuck in traffic with no shoulder available, put it in neutral and let it idle with the heat turned to max and the AC off. That pulls heat away from the engine and buys you a few extra minutes to reach a safe stopping point.

## How Do I Know If I Actually Need a Tow or Can Just Wait It Out?

You need a tow if any of these are true: steam or smoke is coming from under the hood, you see coolant pooling under the car, the low coolant warning light came on before the temp spike, or the gauge hit the red and stayed there. You can also check out related symptoms in our guide to [car overheating white smoke and when you need a tow truck](/car-overheating-white-smoke-tow-truck-needed/).

If the gauge crept up slowly with no other symptoms and drops back to normal after 20 minutes of cooling, you may have a minor issue like low coolant from a slow evaporation. Top it off with water if that is all you have, check for visible leaks, and drive cautiously to a shop. But if anything from the list above applies, stop guessing and call for a tow. Running an overheated engine is the single fastest way to seize it completely. Once the engine seizes, you are looking at a bill that can exceed the car's value. See what that situation looks like at [engine seized while driving: towing cost and what your insurance actually covers](/engine-seized-while-driving-towing-cost-insurance/).

## What Do I Do While I Wait on the Side of the Highway?

Stay out of the vehicle or stay inside with your seatbelt on. On a high-speed highway, standing outside your car near moving traffic is genuinely dangerous. If you are on a busy interstate with no guardrail protection, stay buckled in the passenger seat and keep your hazards running.

Do not open the radiator cap while the engine is hot. Pressurized coolant at 200-plus degrees will spray out and burn you badly. Wait at least 30 minutes after shutdown before touching it.

Call your roadside assistance provider or a tow company. ## What Probably Caused This and Will My Engine Be Okay?

The most common causes of sudden highway overheating are a failed thermostat, a burst coolant hose, a blown head gasket, a cracked radiator, or a water pump that stopped circulating coolant. The thermostat and hoses are cheap fixes. The head gasket is not.

Whether your engine is okay depends entirely on how quickly you stopped. If you pulled over as soon as the gauge moved toward red and shut it off, there is a real chance the engine is fine and you just need to fix whatever caused the coolant loss. If you drove several miles in the red zone because you were trying to reach an exit, there may be damage. A shop will pressure-test the cooling system and check for exhaust gases in the coolant, which is the definitive test for a compromised head gasket. The key point: getting a tow now is always cheaper than finding out later you cooked the engine.

## Common Questions

**Q: Can I add water to my overheated engine to cool it down faster?**
A: Only after the engine has cooled completely, which takes at least 30 minutes. Adding cold water to a hot engine can crack the block or warp the head from thermal shock. Once it is cool, water works as a short-term coolant substitute to get you to a shop, but get it flushed and replaced with proper 50/50 coolant mix as soon as possible.

**Q: Is it safe to restart the engine after it overheated and cooled down?**
A: If the temperature gauge is back to normal, there is no steam, and you can see no obvious coolant leak under the car, a brief restart to check if the gauge holds steady is reasonable. If it climbs again within a minute or two, shut it off and do not drive it. Call a tow.

**Q: Will my insurance cover the tow if my car overheats?**
A: It depends on your policy. Standard liability insurance does not cover towing breakdowns. Roadside assistance add-ons through insurers like GEICO, Progressive, or USAA typically do cover it. Check whether you have that add-on before you assume you are covered.

**Q: How long does it take for an overheated engine to cool down enough to check?**
A: Plan on at least 30 minutes with the engine off. On a hot day with a fully redlined engine, 45 minutes is safer. The upper radiator hose going limp is a tactile indicator that pressure has dropped, but still use a rag when opening the cap and turn it slowly.

**Q: Can a blown head gasket happen just from one overheating event?**
A: Yes. A single severe overheat event, especially one where coolant boiled out entirely, is enough to blow a head gasket or warp an aluminum cylinder head. Modern engines use aluminum heads that are more heat-sensitive than the old cast-iron ones. That is not a scare tactic, it is just how the metallurgy works.
