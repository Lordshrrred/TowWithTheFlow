> **Quick Answer:** Yes, call a tow. A car that overheated and now won't start has likely vapor-locked its fuel system, seized something internally, or blown a head gasket. Waiting 20-30 minutes and trying again once is reasonable. If it still won't fire, stop cranking and get it towed before you turn a repairable problem into a destroyed engine.

## What Happens to an Engine When It Overheats and Won't Restart?

When coolant temps spike past around 240-250°F, several things can fail at once. The most common: the fuel in the fuel rail boils and vapor-locks, the engine management system goes into a protective shutdown, or a head gasket cracks and dumps coolant into a cylinder. That last one is the serious one. A hydrolocked engine, where coolant fills a cylinder and the piston tries to compress it, can bend a connecting rod with a single crank attempt. Stop cranking if the engine feels unusually stiff or makes a heavy thudding noise on the starter.

## Should I Wait for It to Cool Down or Call a Tow Right Now?

Wait 20-30 minutes with the hood up, then try once. If it cranks normally but doesn't start, that points toward a fuel system or sensor issue that cooling will often resolve. If it won't crank at all, cranks very slowly, or makes a clunking sound, call a tow immediately. Do not keep cranking. Every attempt risks bending a rod or cracking a piston if there's coolant in the cylinder. Check the coolant reservoir while you wait. If it's empty and it wasn't before, coolant went somewhere, and that somewhere is likely your combustion chamber.

## How Do I Know If It's a Blown Head Gasket vs. Something Less Serious?

A blown head gasket shows specific, readable signs. White smoke from the exhaust when you do get it running, a sweet burning smell, oil that looks like chocolate milk on the dipstick (milky and frothy), or coolant that mysteriously keeps disappearing. If you see any of those, stop driving immediately and get a tow. A warped head or cracked block from continued driving can turn a $1,500 head gasket repair into a $5,000-plus engine replacement. [Read more about head gasket decisions here.](/blown-head-gasket-can-i-drive-to-mechanic-or-need-tow/)

A vapor lock or a faulty coolant temp sensor, by contrast, leaves no symptoms once the car cools and restarts cleanly. If it fires right up after 30 minutes, runs smoothly, the temp gauge stays normal, and no warning lights appear, you may be okay to drive to a shop. Still get it diagnosed that day.

A local tow runs $75 to $150 for the first few miles, with per-mile rates typically between $3 and $7 after that. If you don't have roadside coverage, [check what you'll pay out of pocket](/how-much-does-roadside-assistance-cost-without-aaa/) before you call so you're not surprised.

## Common Questions

**Q: Can vapor lock cause a car not to start after overheating?**
A: Yes. When fuel boils in the fuel rail or carburetor, it forms bubbles that prevent normal fuel delivery. The fix is letting it cool completely, usually 30-45 minutes. If it starts cleanly and runs fine after that, vapor lock was likely the culprit.

**Q: Will my car start again after overheating if I just let it cool?**
A: Sometimes, but it depends on what caused the overheat. If no internal damage occurred, yes. If a head gasket failed or the engine hydrolocked, restarting it will make things worse. Look for the warning signs: white exhaust smoke, milky oil, empty coolant reservoir.

**Q: What does it mean if my car cranks slowly after overheating?**
A: Slow cranking after overheating can mean a battery weakened by heat stress, a starter struggling against a seized engine, or coolant in the cylinder resisting the piston. None of those are drive-it-home situations. Have it towed.

**Q: How long should I wait before trying to restart after an overheat?**
A: At least 20-30 minutes at minimum, and closer to an hour if the engine was seriously hot. You want the coolant temp gauge to read stone cold before you try again. Rushing it risks restarting with a hot engine and doing additional damage.

**Q: Does overheating always mean I need a new engine?**
A: No. A single overheat caught quickly, where you pulled over and shut it down fast, often causes minimal lasting damage. The damage scales with how long you kept driving after the temp spiked. Pull over the moment the gauge hits red and you have a much better chance of a relatively cheap fix.
