A dead car in a parking lot at night is one of the most common roadside situations, and one of the most stressful. You're not on a highway, which is good. But you're also alone, possibly in a dark or unfamiliar lot, and your options feel limited. Here's how to handle it.

> **Quick Answer:** Yes, waiting in a parking lot for a tow is generally safer than waiting on a roadside or highway shoulder. Stay inside your locked car, keep your hazard lights running, and call a tow service or roadside assistance right away. Most tows arrive within 45 to 90 minutes. If the lot feels unsafe, call a rideshare to relocate while the tow handles the car.

## Is It Actually Safe to Wait Alone in a Parking Lot at Night?

It depends on the lot, but most parking lots are safer than a highway shoulder for one simple reason: you're not near 60 mph traffic. If you're in a well-lit lot attached to a 24-hour business, a hospital, or a gas station, you're in a reasonable spot to wait. If it's a dark, isolated lot behind a closed strip mall with no foot traffic, that changes things.

The single best thing you can do immediately: lock your doors and stay in the car. Don't stand outside. A locked car is a barrier. An unlocked car or a person standing outside it is not.

Turn on your hazard lights so you're visible. If the lot has security cameras or a security guard station, note where it is. Text someone your exact location, including the lot name or address if you can pull it up on your phone. That way someone knows where you are before your phone dies.

## What Should I Do First When My Car Won't Start in a Parking Lot?

Try to figure out what you're dealing with before you call anyone. This affects whether you need a tow or a simpler fix.

Turn the key or press the start button. If you hear a rapid clicking noise, that's almost always a dead battery. Single click or nothing at all points to a bad starter relay or the battery being completely flat. If the engine cranks normally but won't fire, that's a different problem: fuel, spark, or a sensor.

A dead battery is the most common culprit. If someone else is in the lot, a jump start from jumper cables connected to their battery might get you going. Ask for help from the employee of a nearby open business rather than a stranger if you have options. If the jump start doesn't work after two attempts, stop trying. You likely need a tow. You can read more about [what happens when a jump start fails and what a tow will cost you](/dead-battery-jump-start-failed-towing-cost/) before you make the call.

Once you've assessed and decided you need a tow: call your roadside assistance first. AAA, your insurance's roadside program, or a credit card benefit can get a truck moving without an out-of-pocket charge at the moment. If you have none of those, call a local tow company directly.

## How Long Will I Wait for a Tow Truck in a Parking Lot?

Typical wait times run 45 to 90 minutes, though metro areas with heavy call volume can push past two hours on weekend nights. Rural areas are even less predictable.

When you call, ask the dispatcher for an estimated time of arrival and ask them to call or text you when the driver is 10 minutes out. That's standard, and most will do it. Keep your phone charged. If you're running low on battery, turn off Wi-Fi and lower your screen brightness right now.

Do not leave the lot without telling the tow company. If you decide to relocate to a safer spot nearby, call dispatch and give them your new location before you move. A tow driver who arrives at the wrong spot will cancel the call, and you may face a re-dispatch fee or a longer wait. ## What If the Parking Lot Feels Unsafe?

Get out of the car and into a safer location, then deal with the car. The car can be towed without you present in most cases.

If the lot is isolated, poorly lit, or there's someone making you uncomfortable, call a rideshare and go somewhere safe: a nearby open business, a gas station, a 24-hour diner. Give the tow company your new location and let them know you won't be with the vehicle. Most tow companies will tow without the owner present if you've given authorization over the phone and can tell them where to deliver the car.

If you feel immediately threatened, call 911. Don't hesitate on that. Your safety is not worth protecting the car.

If you're dealing with a situation in a genuinely rough area, the same principles from [car broke down in a bad neighborhood](/car-broke-down-bad-neighborhood-safe-steps/) apply here, even if it's a lot rather than a street.

## Will My Insurance or Roadside Assistance Cover This?

Most likely yes, if you have roadside assistance through your insurer, AAA, or a credit card. A parking lot breakdown is a standard covered event: no different from breaking down on a surface street.

Check whether your policy covers towing to any shop or just to the nearest one. Some plans, like basic AAA Classic membership, cover towing up to 5 miles for free before per-mile charges kick in. Others cover a flat dollar amount regardless of distance. If your mechanic is across town, the overage could cost you money, so ask before you authorize the tow destination.

If you're paying out of pocket, expect a $75 to $150 hookup fee plus $3 to $7 per mile after the first few miles, depending on where you are. ## Common Questions

**Q: Can I leave my car in the parking lot overnight and get it towed in the morning?**
A: You can, but check the lot's posted towing policy first. Many commercial lots will tow unauthorized vehicles after hours or after a certain time. If it's a public lot or you have reason to believe it's safe to leave, call ahead to confirm the car won't be towed before your tow truck arrives.

**Q: My car won't start and I have no roadside assistance. What's the cheapest option?**
A: Call a local independent tow company rather than a national chain dispatch service. Local operators typically charge less. Also check whether your credit card has a built-in roadside benefit. Visa Signature and certain Mastercards include it, and most cardholders don't know.

**Q: Should I try to push my car to a better spot in the lot while I wait?**
A: Only if it's truly necessary for safety and you have someone to help steer. Pushing a car alone in the dark is how people get hurt. If you need to move it, ask for help from a nearby business employee, not a stranger in the lot.

**Q: My hazard lights are on but my battery is already dead. Will they drain it further?**
A: Yes, but a dead battery that won't start a car can often still power lights for a while. Run the hazards. Being visible is more important than preserving a battery that isn't going to start the car anyway.

**Q: What do I tell the tow company when I call?**
A: Give them your exact location (lot name, street address, nearest cross street), describe where in the lot you're parked, tell them the year, make, and model of your car, and tell them the destination for the tow. Have your insurance or roadside membership info ready before you dial.
