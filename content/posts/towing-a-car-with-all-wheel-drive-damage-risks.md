> **Quick Answer:** Never let an AWD car be towed with two wheels on the ground. Spinning half the drivetrain while the other half sits still can overheat and destroy the center differential or transfer case, a repair that often runs into the thousands. Insist on a flatbed. If the driver says "it'll be fine," call another company.

## Can I Tow an All Wheel Drive Car With Two Wheels on the Ground?

No. Towing an AWD vehicle with the front or rear wheels lifted is the single most common way people wreck a drivetrain during a tow. The wheels on the ground turn the axle, the driveshaft, and the center coupling, but the lifted wheels stay still. Those parts are built to work together, and forcing them to spin out of sync generates heat and wear with no lubrication circulating the way it does when the engine runs.

Subaru, Audi Quattro, most Hondas and Toyotas with AWD, and Mitsubishi systems all carry this warning in the owner's manual. Many manuals say "flatbed only," and some say all four wheels must be off the ground, period.

## What Actually Breaks When an AWD Car Is Towed Wrong?

The center differential, viscous coupling, or transfer case takes the damage first. These components let the front and rear axles turn at slightly different speeds. When you drag the car with only one axle rotating, internal clutches and fluid couplings can cook, seize, or shred.

Symptoms after a bad tow show up later, which is why people miss the connection:

- A binding or shuddering feel in tight, slow turns, like in a parking lot
- A whining or humming noise from the middle or rear of the car
- AWD or drivetrain warning lights
- A burning smell from the transfer case area

If you see any of these after a tow, stop driving and get it inspected before it turns into a full failure.

## Is a Flatbed the Only Safe Way to Tow an AWD Car?

Yes, a flatbed is the safe default, because all four wheels sit off the ground and nothing in the drivetrain turns. Dollies under the drive wheels can work on some vehicles, but you need the manual to confirm it, and most dispatchers won't verify that for you.

When you call, say these words clearly: "All wheel drive, flatbed only." Then ask the dispatcher to confirm a flatbed is what shows up. Some companies send a wheel-lift truck anyway because it's closer or already free.

If a wheel-lift truck arrives, you have two options. Refuse the tow and have them send a flatbed, or have the driver confirm in writing that your car will be loaded with all four wheels off the ground. Do not accept "just a few miles, it's fine." Short distance does not protect the drivetrain. Damage can start within a mile or two.

## What If the Car Is Stuck in Park or the Wheels Won't Roll?

A flatbed handles this too, since the car gets winched or loaded without needing to roll normally. If the transmission is locked, the parking brake is stuck, or the car is wedged in a ditch or snowbank, tell the dispatcher up front. They may need to send a rig with a winch and dollies.

Do not shift into neutral and let someone push or drag it a long way as a workaround. Neutral on an AWD car does not make it safe to tow on the ground. Pulling it onto a flatbed from a stuck position is the correct fix.

## What Do I Do If a Tow Truck Already Damaged My AWD Car?

Document everything before you pay or sign. Photograph how the car was loaded, the truck type, and any visible damage. Note the driver's name and the company on the invoice. If drivetrain symptoms show up afterward, get a written inspection from a shop that names the cause.

## Common Questions

**Q: Can I tow my AWD car on a dolly?**
A: Sometimes, but only if your owner's manual says so. Dollies lift the drive wheels, which can still spin the other axle. When in doubt, use a full flatbed.

**Q: Is it okay to tow an AWD car just a few miles with wheels on the ground?**
A: No. Damage can begin within the first mile or two, so short distance gives you no real protection. Wait for a flatbed.

**Q: Will roadside assistance send a flatbed for my AWD car?**
A: Often only if you ask. State "all wheel drive, flatbed only" when you call, and confirm it again if the dispatcher says a different truck is coming.

**Q: How do I know if my car is truly AWD and not just 4WD or front wheel drive?**
A: Check the owner's manual towing section or the badge on the trunk. If you aren't sure, say "AWD, treat it as flatbed only." Part-time 4WD systems have their own rules, so don't guess.

**Q: How much does it cost to fix a transfer case or center differential?**
A: It varies widely by make and model, but this is typically a major repair, often well into four figures. That's why paying a bit more for a flatbed up front is worth it.
