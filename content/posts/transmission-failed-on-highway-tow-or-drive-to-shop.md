> **Quick Answer:** If your transmission has fully failed, meaning the car won't move, shifts into neutral on its own, or is making grinding/clunking noises, get it towed. Do not drive it. Driving a failed automatic or CVT transmission even a few miles can turn a $2,000 repair into a $5,000 rebuild or full replacement. A flatbed tow is the right call here.

## My Transmission Just Died on the Highway. What Do I Do Right Now?

Get off the road immediately and safely. If the car is still rolling, signal, coast to the right shoulder, and stop completely before you do anything else. Turn on your hazard lights the moment you feel something wrong.

Once you are stopped:

1. Shift to Park if the car will let you. If it won't engage Park, leave it in Neutral and set the parking brake.
2. Stay in the car if you are on a high-speed highway. The shoulder of an interstate is one of the most dangerous places to stand.
3. Call for a tow. Do not restart the engine and try to nurse it to the next exit unless the transmission is only slipping mildly (more on that below).

If you have roadside assistance through AAA, your insurer, or a manufacturer program, call them now. If not, search for a tow truck in your current city or check [what roadside assistance costs without a membership](/how-much-does-emergency-roadside-assistance-cost-without-membership/) so you know what you are getting into before you agree to anything.

## How Do I Know If the Transmission Actually Failed or If It's Just Slipping?

A complete failure and a slip feel different, and the difference matters for your next decision.

**Signs of a complete failure:**
- The engine revs but the car does not move at all, in any gear
- Hard clunk or bang followed by loss of drive
- The car suddenly drops into neutral on its own at highway speed
- Burning smell coming from underneath, not from the engine

**Signs of slipping (not a full failure yet):**
- Engine revs higher than normal before the gear catches
- Delayed engagement when shifting from Park to Drive
- Occasional shudder between gears at highway speed

If you are in the slipping category and the car still drives, you may be able to reach a shop. Keep speeds under 45 mph, avoid hard acceleration, and go directly there without stopping and restarting the engine more than necessary. Every restart and gear engagement risks finishing off a transmission that is already on its way out. A related situation to read before you decide: [transmission slipping on highway](/transmission-slipping-on-highway-do-i-need-a-tow/).

If you are in the complete failure category, you are done driving. Call a tow.

## Will Driving a Failed Transmission Make It Worse?

Yes, significantly. This is the question that costs people the most money.

An automatic transmission relies on pressurized fluid to engage clutch packs and planetary gear sets. When the transmission fails, that pressure is gone or the internal components are already damaged. Running the engine and forcing the drivetrain to turn damaged parts grinds metal into the transmission fluid. That metal contamination spreads through the valve body, torque converter, and every solenoid in the unit. What might have been a solenoid replacement or a single clutch pack repair becomes a full rebuild or junkyard swap.

A flatbed tow is the correct choice for a failed transmission, not a wheel-lift. A wheel-lift tow on a rear-wheel-drive car raises the rear wheels, which means the front wheels spin the driveshaft and turn the transmission internals even while the engine is off. On an all-wheel-drive or four-wheel-drive vehicle, all four wheels need to be off the ground, which means a flatbed is mandatory.

## What Should I Tell the Tow Truck Driver?

Tell them the car needs a flatbed, not a wheel-lift, and tell them why.

Be specific: "The transmission failed. I need all four wheels off the ground." If your car is AWD or 4WD, repeat that clearly. A tow driver who puts a failed AWD vehicle on a wheel-lift can cause additional drivetrain damage that becomes a billing dispute nobody wants to have on the side of a highway.

Also tell the driver the destination before they hook up the car. Some towing companies will deliver to any shop you choose. Others will try to take it to a preferred shop that may pay them a referral fee. Know where you want the car to go and say so upfront.

## Common Questions

**Q: Can I drive a car with a failed transmission to the shop if the shop is only 2 miles away?**
A: No. Two miles is enough to contaminate the fluid with metal particles and destroy components that could have been rebuilt. Pay for the tow.

**Q: What does a transmission failure feel like compared to a blown head gasket or engine failure?**
A: A transmission failure usually shows up as lost drive with the engine still running normally. You can rev the engine but the car won't move, or moves then stops. A blown head gasket typically comes with overheating, white smoke from the exhaust, and coolant loss. An engine failure often comes with a loud knock or seizure. If your engine is running fine but the car won't go, suspect the transmission first.

**Q: Will my car insurance or roadside assistance cover the tow for a transmission failure?**
A: Roadside assistance through your insurer or a membership like AAA covers the tow itself, usually up to a set mileage limit. The transmission repair is not covered by standard auto insurance unless you have mechanical breakdown insurance (MBI), which is a separate add-on. Check your policy before assuming.

**Q: Is a CVT transmission failure handled differently than a traditional automatic?**
A: Same rule applies: flatbed tow, no driving. CVTs (common in Nissans, Subarus, and many Honda and Toyota models) use a belt and pulley system instead of traditional gears. A failing CVT belt can snap completely under load, and metal shavings contaminate the fluid just as fast as in a conventional automatic.

**Q: How long can I wait on the shoulder before the tow truck arrives?**
A: Stay in the car with your seatbelt on and hazards flashing. If you are on an interstate, most tow trucks arrive in 30 to 60 minutes. If you feel unsafe on the shoulder, get out of the passenger side and move behind the guardrail. Never stand between your car and moving traffic.
