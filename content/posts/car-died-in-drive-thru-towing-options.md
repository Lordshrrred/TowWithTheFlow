> **Quick Answer:** Put your car in neutral and push it out of the lane with help from bystanders or staff. Then call your roadside assistance provider or a local tow truck. A short local tow will run $75 to $125. If your battery died, a jump start may get you moving in minutes. If the engine seized or the fuel pump quit, you need a tow, full stop.

## My Car Just Died in the Drive Thru Lane. What Do I Do First?

Get it out of the lane. That is the only priority right now. Put the car in neutral, turn your hazards on, and ask anyone nearby to help you push it into the parking lot or a nearby open space. Most drive thru lanes curve, which makes this harder, but even rolling it forward to the pickup window area buys you breathing room.

Once you are out of active traffic, call for help. Do not sit in the lane waiting for a tow truck while cars pile up behind you. Drive thru staff will often come out to help push, and most restaurants will let you use the parking lot while you wait for a tow.

If you cannot get the car out of the lane yourself and no one is available to help, call 911. They can dispatch non-emergency assistance or traffic control. This is especially important if you are blocking a lane that connects to a busy road.

## Is This a Dead Battery or Something Worse?

A dead battery is by far the most common reason a car dies in a low-speed situation like a drive thru. Signs point that way if your dashboard went dark, the engine just cut out quietly, or you had dim lights and sluggish cranking before it died. A jump start from another driver or a roadside technician will get you moving in 10 to 15 minutes if that is all it is. Keep in mind that if your [alternator has been failing](/alternator-died-while-driving-signs-and-towing-options/), the battery will just die again once you disconnect the jumper cables.

If the engine made a loud knock or clunk before dying, or if it got hot and then quit, you are looking at something more serious, possibly a seized engine or major mechanical failure. Do not try to restart it repeatedly. Cranking a seized engine causes more damage. In that case, a tow is the only right move. Check the [engine seized while driving guide](/engine-seized-while-driving-need-immediate-tow/) for what to expect next.

Other possibilities: a failed fuel pump (engine cranks but won't fire), a snapped serpentine belt (sudden power loss, may smell like burning rubber), or a stall from an overheating coolant system. Each of these requires different follow-up, but the immediate answer is the same: stop trying to restart it and get it towed.

## What Are My Towing Options From a Drive Thru?

You have four realistic options depending on what coverage you carry.

**Roadside assistance through your insurance:** GEICO, State Farm, USAA, and most major carriers offer roadside add-ons that cover towing. If you have this, call the number on your insurance card first. Response times vary but typically run 30 to 90 minutes. **AAA or similar membership:** AAA members get free towing up to a set mileage limit depending on membership tier. Classic members get 5 miles free, Plus members get 100 miles. If you are a member, call AAA first.

**Call a local tow truck directly:** Search for towing companies near you and call directly. A local tow in most metro areas runs $75 to $125 for the first 5 to 10 miles, with per-mile charges after that. Flat rates are common for short hauls. Get a price confirmed before they hook up your car.

**On-demand apps:** Urgent.ly and Honk connect you with local tow operators through an app. They show estimated arrival times and upfront pricing. Useful if you do not have a preferred company or roadside coverage.

One practical tip: tell the tow company you are in a drive thru lane when you call. Some operators send a flatbed specifically when they know the pickup location is tight.

## How Long Will I Be Stuck There?

In a city or suburb, expect 30 to 60 minutes for most tow trucks. Rural areas can stretch to 90 minutes or more. Roadside assistance dispatched through an insurance carrier sometimes runs slower than a direct call to a local shop.

Use the wait time productively. Check if your [car battery dead situation](/dead-battery-no-jumper-cables-options/) can be solved faster with a jump from another driver. Ask a nearby driver or the restaurant staff. If someone with jumper cables is available, a battery jump takes less than 5 minutes and may get you out of there without waiting for a truck at all.

## Common Questions

**Q: Can I leave my car in the drive thru and come back for it?**
A: No. The restaurant cannot operate that lane and will likely call a tow themselves within minutes. You will end up paying impound fees on top of towing costs. Get it moved to the parking lot first.

**Q: Will the restaurant call the police on me?**
A: If you are blocking the lane for more than a few minutes and not actively dealing with it, yes, they may call for non-emergency assistance. Be upfront with the staff. Most will give you reasonable time to get help arranged if you communicate.

**Q: How much does a tow from a drive thru to a mechanic cost?**
A: A short local tow of 5 to 10 miles typically runs $75 to $125 out of pocket. If you have roadside assistance through your insurance or AAA, you may pay nothing or just a small service fee.

**Q: My car cranks but won't start in the drive thru. Do I need a tow?**
A: Not always. Cranks-but-won't-start usually points to a fuel delivery problem or a failed ignition component, not a dead battery. Try a jump start to rule out battery. If it still won't fire after a jump, call a tow.

**Q: Can a tow truck get into a drive thru lane to hook up my car?**
A: Most flatbed trucks can maneuver into a drive thru area, but the operator may need to winch the car out to an open space first. Tell the dispatcher the location is a tight drive thru lane so they send the right equipment.
