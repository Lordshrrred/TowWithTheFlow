> **Quick Answer:** Who pays depends on how you ended up in the ditch. If another driver caused it, their liability insurance should cover the tow. If it was a single-car incident, your own roadside assistance or comprehensive/collision coverage typically applies. No coverage at all means you pay out of pocket, usually $150 to $400 or more for a ditch recovery. Call your insurer before you call a tow truck.

## Who Actually Pays When Your Car Goes in a Ditch?

The short answer: whoever is at fault, or whoever holds the applicable coverage. If another driver ran you off the road, their liability insurance is your first call. If you slid off on your own, say on ice or loose gravel, you need your own policy. A plain breakdown tow costs one price, but a ditch recovery adds equipment time and often a second truck, so the bill climbs fast.

The specific coverage that applies:

- **Roadside assistance add-on** (from your insurer, AAA, or your car manufacturer): Covers towing and sometimes basic recovery if you didn't roll or bury the vehicle.
- **Comprehensive or collision coverage**: May cover recovery costs if the ditch incident is tied to an accident, but always check your deductible first. A $500 deductible often wipes out the benefit on a $200 tow.
- **At-fault driver's liability insurance**: Their property damage liability pays for getting your car out and towed to a shop. Get their insurance info before anything moves.
- **No coverage**: You pay the tow company directly. Ditch recoveries are not standard tows. The truck may need a winch, chains, and a second operator, which adds real money.

## What Do I Do Right Now While Still in or Near the Ditch?

Stay in the car if you're on an active highway lane or shoulder with traffic passing. Turn on your hazard lights immediately. If the vehicle is stable and off the travel lanes, it's generally safer to stay put than to stand on the shoulder of a high-speed road.

Here's what to do in order:

1. Call 911 if anyone is injured or if the car is blocking active lanes. A trooper can call a contracted tow and also documents the scene, which helps with insurance claims.
2. Take photos of everything before any vehicle moves: tire tracks, road conditions, your car's position, any guardrail or signage damage.
3. Call your insurance company or roadside assistance. Do this before you agree to anything with a tow driver who stops on their own. Unsolicited tow trucks at accident scenes sometimes charge inflated rates and then demand payment before releasing the vehicle.
4. Get the tow company's name, license number, and a written estimate before they hook up. In most states, a tow company is required to provide this.

## How Much Does a Ditch Recovery Cost If I'm Paying Out of Pocket?

A standard local tow runs $75 to $150. A ditch recovery is not a standard tow. Expect $150 to $400 for a straightforward winch-out on flat terrain, and $400 to $800 or higher if the vehicle is deeply buried, on a slope, or requires a heavy-duty truck. Some recovery operators charge by the hour once equipment is deployed, typically $100 to $200 per hour.

## Will Filing an Insurance Claim for a Ditch Tow Raise My Rates?

It depends on the claim type and your insurer. A roadside assistance claim for a simple tow generally does not affect your premium at most carriers. A collision or comprehensive claim tied to an at-fault accident is a different story and can affect your rates at renewal.

If the other driver is at fault, the claim goes through their liability coverage, not yours, so your own rate should not move. That said, document everything: the other driver's name, insurance company, policy number, and license plate. Without that, you may end up filing under your own collision coverage and sorting out reimbursement later through subrogation, a process your insurer handles but one that can take months.

## Common Questions

**Q: Can I use AAA to get my car pulled out of a ditch?**
A: Yes, AAA covers winch-out services under most membership tiers, but there are limits. A basic AAA Classic membership covers a winch-out up to a certain distance and effort level. If the recovery is complex or requires a second truck, AAA may cover part of it and bill you for the rest. Call them first and describe the situation accurately so they send the right equipment.

**Q: What if a tow truck already hooked up my car before I called insurance?**
A: You're still able to file a claim, but your leverage on the bill is reduced. Pay with a card, get an itemized receipt, and submit it to your insurer. Most roadside assistance plans reimburse after the fact even if you didn't pre-authorize through them, up to your benefit limit.

**Q: Does the highway patrol or state DOT pay for the tow if road conditions caused the accident?**
A: No. State agencies are almost never liable for towing costs even if poor road maintenance contributed to the accident. You can attempt a claim against a state DOT, but it is a long and rarely successful process. Your own insurance is the practical path.

**Q: How do I know if a tow company is price gouging me at the scene?**
A: Get a written estimate before they move the vehicle. Compare it against the range above. If a company refuses to give you a written price, that is a red flag. In most states, you have the right to refuse service from any tow operator before they hook up. Once they are hooked up and moving, your options shrink significantly.

**Q: What if my car is totaled in the ditch, does insurance still pay for the tow?**
A: Yes. Even if the vehicle is a total loss, the insurance company needs it moved to a salvage yard or their authorized facility for the total-loss assessment. Towing to that location is typically covered under your collision or comprehensive claim, separate from the payout for the vehicle itself.
