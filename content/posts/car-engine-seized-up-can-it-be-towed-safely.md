> **Quick Answer:** Yes, a seized engine can be towed safely, but ask for a flatbed. A seized engine locks the crankshaft, which can lock the drivetrain too. Dragging the car on its drive wheels can grind the transmission, damage tires, or cause a skid. Get off the road, shut the engine off, do not try to restart it, and tell the dispatcher the engine is seized when you call.

## Can a Car With a Seized Engine Be Towed Safely?

Yes, a car with a seized engine can be towed safely on a flatbed. A wheel-lift or hook-and-chain tow is a bad idea, because the drive wheels stay on the pavement and the car may not roll freely.

Here is why it matters. A seized engine means the pistons, crankshaft, or bearings have locked up, usually from oil starvation or severe overheating. In a manual car, the engine is tied directly to the drive wheels, so the wheels may refuse to turn at all. In an automatic, the transmission's internals are dragged along, and towing with the drive wheels down can destroy the transmission even if the wheels do turn.

A flatbed removes all of that. All four wheels leave the ground, nothing spins, and nothing drags. If you're unsure whether your car qualifies, [here's how to tell if you need a flatbed tow](/how-do-i-know-if-my-car-needs-a-flatbed-tow/).

## How Do I Know My Engine Is Actually Seized?

An engine is seized when it won't turn over at all: no cranking, just a single loud clunk or click, or a starter that strains and stops. Before that, there are usually warnings: a loud knocking or banging from the engine, the oil pressure light coming on, the temperature gauge pegged, or smoke from under the hood.

A dead battery or failed starter can look similar. The difference is in the sound and the lights:

- **Dead battery:** dash lights dim or dead, rapid clicking, nothing else.
- **Seized engine:** dash lights bright, starter engages with a heavy clunk or hum, then stops.
- **Hydrolocked engine:** engine dies after driving through deep water. Same tow rules apply.

Do not keep trying to start it. Every attempt can bend rods or crack components that might otherwise have been saved. If the engine locked up while you were driving, the same overheating that causes seizure often shows up first as [smoke from under the hood](/smoke-coming-from-under-hood-while-driving-what-to-do/) or a [flashing engine light](/engine-light-flashing-on-highway-should-i-pull-over/).

## What Do I Do Right Now Before the Tow Truck Arrives?

Get the car as far off the travel lane as you can, turn on your hazards, and stay out of the engine bay. If the engine seized at speed, you were likely coasting to a stop with no power steering and weak brakes, so brake early and firmly and steer with both hands.

Then work through this list:

1. **Shut the ignition off** and leave it off.
2. **Turn on hazards** and, if it's dark, any interior light that helps you be seen.
3. **Get everyone out and away from traffic** if you're on a highway shoulder. Stand behind the guardrail if one exists, not beside the car. [Waiting safely on a highway shoulder](/car-died-on-highway-shoulder-safe-to-wait-for-tow-truck/) covers the details.

## What Should I Tell the Tow Company When I Call?

Say the words "seized engine" and "I need a flatbed," in that order. Dispatchers send whatever truck is closest unless you specify, and a wheel-lift truck showing up for a locked-up car wastes your time and money.

Give them:

- Your exact location (highway, mile marker, nearest exit)
- Year, make, and model, and whether it's front-, rear-, or all-wheel drive
- That the engine will not turn over
- Whether the transmission is automatic or manual
- Where you want the car delivered

Ask for the flatbed price up front. If the dispatcher says a flatbed will take much longer, it's usually worth the wait. A few extra minutes beats a ruined transmission. If the wait stretches, see [your options when a tow truck is taking too long](/tow-truck-taking-too-long-what-are-my-options-roadside/).

## Where Should I Have a Seized Engine Towed?

Tow it to a repair shop that does engine work, not just the nearest garage, because a seized engine is often a replace-or-rebuild decision, and the shop needs to tell you honestly whether the car is worth repairing.

Ask the shop for a teardown estimate before authorizing anything. Replacement engines for common cars often run into the thousands, and on an older vehicle that can exceed what the car is worth. Many people in that spot sell the car for parts or to a buyer who takes non-running vehicles. Towing it home first, then deciding, is a legitimate choice if the shop is far away and the tow is priced by the mile.

## Common Questions

**Q: Can I tow a seized engine with a rope or strap from another car?**
A: No. The wheels may not turn, you'll have no power steering or power brakes, and a locked drivetrain can send the car sliding. Use a flatbed.

**Q: Will towing make the seized engine worse?**
A: On a flatbed, no. On a wheel-lift or dolly with the drive wheels down, you risk damaging the transmission, axles, or tires, and the car may skid.

**Q: Can a seized engine be fixed without replacing it?**
A: Sometimes. If it seized from a lack of oil and no metal broke, a shop may free it up and rebuild it. If a rod went through the block or bearings welded to the crank, replacement is usually the answer.

**Q: Is it safe to sit in the car while it's being loaded onto the flatbed?**
A: No. Get out and stand clear, ideally well off the road, before the truck pulls onto or near the shoulder. Loading is when most roadside injuries happen.

**Q: Does insurance or roadside assistance cover towing a seized engine?**
A: Roadside coverage through AAA or your auto policy usually covers the tow itself, often up to a mileage limit. It generally does not cover the engine repair. Call before the truck arrives so the tow is authorized.
