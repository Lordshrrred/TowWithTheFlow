> **Quick Answer:** Call 911 immediately. Tunnels are one of the few places where a non-injury breakdown warrants a 911 call every time, because tunnel operators and emergency responders need to know fast. After you call, turn on your hazard lights, stay in your car if traffic is moving, and get out on the right side and move to the emergency walkway if you smell smoke or fuel.

## Who Do I Actually Call When My Car Breaks Down Inside a Tunnel?

Call 911 first, not your roadside assistance, not AAA. Tunnels are managed environments with dedicated traffic control systems, ventilation fans, fire suppression, and CCTV. Emergency dispatchers can contact tunnel operations directly, which gets hazard lights activated on the approach signs and gets the right equipment rolling. Your roadside provider cannot do any of that.

After you have spoken to 911 and confirmed help is coming, then call your roadside assistance or a tow company. A tow truck cannot just enter an active tunnel whenever it wants. Most major tunnels require tunnel authority clearance before a private tow truck is allowed in. The dispatcher will coordinate that access. If you try to call a tow company first, you will be waiting while they figure out who to call.

## Is It Safe to Stay in My Car or Should I Get Out?

Stay in your car with your seatbelt on if traffic is still moving around you and you do not smell smoke or fuel. Getting out into live tunnel traffic on foot is more dangerous than staying inside a vehicle. Keep your hazard lights flashing, do not open your hood inside the tunnel, and watch your mirrors.

Get out immediately if you see smoke, smell burning, or smell fuel. Most tunnels have a pedestrian walkway or emergency refuge area along the right wall. Move there. Do not stand between your car and moving traffic. Some tunnels, like the tunnels on I-70 through the Colorado Rockies or the Lincoln Tunnel in New Jersey, have recessed emergency call boxes and fire hose stations along the walls. Use them.

## What Happens After I Call 911 in a Tunnel?

Dispatchers alert the tunnel control room, which monitors every camera in the bore. Within minutes, overhead message signs flip to warn approaching drivers, ventilation systems may adjust, and a tunnel incident response team is dispatched. These are not regular police cruisers. Many major tunnels have dedicated highway maintenance vehicles or fire units stationed nearby for exactly this reason.

When responders arrive, follow their instructions exactly. They may push your car to the tunnel portal before bringing in a tow truck, because flatbeds and heavy wreckers need room to work and disrupting tunnel traffic for an extended period is a serious safety issue. Do not be surprised if your car gets pushed by a response vehicle rather than loaded on a flatbed inside the tunnel. This is standard practice.

Once you are out of the tunnel, the normal towing process kicks in. If you need help understanding your coverage options, [does State Farm roadside assistance cover towing](/does-state-farm-roadside-assistance-cover-towing/) breaks down what your insurance may pay for.

## Common Questions

**Q: Can I push my car out of the tunnel myself?**
A: No. Do not attempt to push your car through a live tunnel on your own. You would be on foot in traffic. Call 911 and let the tunnel response team move the vehicle safely with proper equipment and traffic control in place.

**Q: Will 911 really respond to a breakdown if no one is hurt?**
A: Yes, in a tunnel. Unlike a shoulder breakdown on an open highway where police response is lower priority, a stalled vehicle inside a tunnel is an immediate traffic and safety hazard that dispatchers treat urgently.

**Q: What if I'm in a tunnel and my car starts smoking?**
A: Get out immediately. Grab your phone and nothing else. Move to the nearest emergency refuge area or pedestrian walkway on the right wall. Activate the nearest emergency call box if you can find one. Fire inside a tunnel spreads fast and ventilation can funnel smoke toward you quickly.

**Q: What if I have roadside assistance through my insurance and want to use it instead of calling 911?**
A: Call 911 first, then call your roadside provider. Your insurance company cannot coordinate with tunnel operations or get access clearance for a tow truck. You need both calls, in that order.

**Q: How long will I be stuck waiting in a tunnel after calling 911?**
A: Response times vary, but tunnel incidents are high priority. In a major metro tunnel with on-site staff, you may see a response vehicle in under five minutes. In a rural mountain tunnel, it could be fifteen to thirty minutes. Stay calm, stay visible, and do not leave your phone.
