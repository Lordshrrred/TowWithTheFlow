> **Quick Answer:** On most U.S. highways, you have roughly 30 minutes to a few hours before a trooper tags your car for removal. The real limit is not police policy, it is your physical safety on the shoulder. A disabled vehicle on a high-speed highway is at serious risk of being struck, especially at night. Call for a tow immediately, stay out of the car if traffic is close, and never stand between your vehicle and moving lanes.

## How Long Do I Actually Have Before My Car Gets Towed by Police?

Most state highway patrol policies give an abandoned vehicle 24 to 72 hours before initiating a forced tow, but a car on a live highway shoulder is treated differently from one parked on a surface street. Troopers can and do call for an emergency removal within 30 to 60 minutes if the vehicle is blocking a travel lane, partially blocking a ramp, or creating a visible hazard during heavy traffic. If you are present and actively waiting for a tow, officers will typically give you more time, especially if you can show them a confirmed ETA from a tow company. Standing next to your car and showing your phone screen with an active tow request goes a long way.

The difference between "broken down" and "abandoned" matters legally. An abandoned vehicle on an interstate can be tagged and removed at the owner's expense within hours in states like Texas, California, and Florida. As long as you are physically there and communicating with police, you are not abandoned, and most troopers will work with you.

## Is It Safe to Wait Inside My Car on the Highway Shoulder?

No, not if traffic is within one lane of your vehicle. The shoulder of an interstate is one of the most dangerous places a person can stand or sit. A distracted or impaired driver drifting onto the shoulder will not brake in time. If you must wait, get everyone out of the car, move past the guardrail if there is one, and stay as far from the roadway as possible. Staying in the car feels safer emotionally, but the data on vehicles being struck from behind on shoulders is severe enough that most highway safety organizations specifically advise getting out.

If you are on a narrow shoulder with no barrier and traffic is moving at 65 mph or faster, put on your hazard lights, place reflective triangles or road flares 100 to 300 feet behind the vehicle if you have them, and wait behind the guardrail or on a slope away from traffic. For detailed steps on staying safe in this exact situation, read [how to stay safe broken down on a highway at night](/how-to-stay-safe-broken-down-on-highway-at-night/).

## What Should I Do While I'm Waiting for the Tow Truck?

Call your tow company first, then let someone know your location. The most specific you can be, the better. Use a highway mile marker if there is one nearby. On I-10 in Louisiana, for example, mile markers are posted every mile on green signs along the shoulder. Most state DOT apps and 511 services can also confirm your location if you have data. Give dispatch the mile marker, the direction of travel, and the nearest exit or interchange.

While you wait:

- Turn on hazard lights immediately and leave them on.
- Set out road flares or reflective triangles if you have them.
- Call 911 if you feel unsafe at any point.

Keep your tow company's number on the screen and monitor the ETA. If the wait exceeds 90 minutes and conditions are unsafe, call 911 and ask the dispatcher to expedite a highway patrol escort or contact the tow company on your behalf.

## What If the Tow Truck Is Taking More Than 2 Hours?

Call back and confirm the truck is still coming. Dispatch errors happen, and a job can get reassigned without anyone notifying you. If your ETA has passed and you cannot reach the original company, call a second tow company and cancel the first once the second confirms arrival. You are not locked into a provider just because you called them first.

If you have AAA, GEICO roadside, State Farm roadside, or any other membership-based assistance, call their dispatch line directly. They track their contracted trucks and can push for prioritization in a way you cannot as an individual caller.

A two-hour wait on a daytime suburban highway with a wide shoulder is manageable. A two-hour wait at night on a narrow interstate shoulder with no barrier is a different situation. At that point, consider accepting a police-arranged tow even if it costs more, because a secondary tow from a non-preferred shop is a lot cheaper than the alternative.

## Common Questions

**Q: Can police force me to accept a tow I didn't call?**
A: Yes. If an officer determines your vehicle is a hazard, they can call their contracted tow company and require removal. You will pay that company's rate, which may be higher than a shop you chose yourself. You can transfer the car to your preferred shop afterward.

**Q: How long do tow trucks usually take to arrive on a highway?**
A: Response times vary widely. In dense metro areas, 30 to 60 minutes is typical. In rural stretches or during bad weather, 90 minutes to two hours or more is realistic. Always ask for a firm ETA when you call.

**Q: Do I have to stay with my car while waiting for a tow?**
A: Not legally, but leaving your vehicle unattended on an active highway shoulder speeds up how fast it gets tagged for police removal. If it is unsafe to stay, get to safety first, then call 911 to notify them the vehicle is unoccupied.

**Q: What if I have no cell service and can't call a tow truck?**
A: Use your hazards, raise the hood as a distress signal, and stay visible. Highway emergency call boxes still exist on many interstates, typically spaced about one mile apart. A trooper on patrol will usually stop within 30 to 45 minutes on a heavily traveled highway. For a full guide, see [what to do if your car breaks down with no cell service](/car-broke-down-no-cell-service-on-highway-what-to-do/).

**Q: Will my insurance pay if police call their own tow company?**
A: If you have roadside assistance through your insurance, it typically reimburses a tow regardless of which company was dispatched, up to your policy limit. Get an itemized receipt from the tow company before they leave.
