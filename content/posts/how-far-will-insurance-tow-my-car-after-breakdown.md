> **Quick Answer:** Most insurance roadside add-ons tow between 5 and 100 miles, and many cover only a flat dollar amount, often $50 to $150, instead of a set distance. Basic policies without a roadside endorsement may not tow at all. Check your policy or app for the exact limit, then ask the dispatcher to confirm it before the truck hooks up your car.

## How Far Will My Insurance Actually Tow My Car?

It depends entirely on which roadside product you bought, and the range runs from about 5 miles to 100 or more. Some insurers cap by distance. Others cap by dollars, which means the miles you get depend on what the local tow company charges.

Here is how it typically breaks down:

- **Distance caps:** Common limits are 10, 15, 25, or 50 miles. Some premium tiers go to 100 miles.
- **Dollar caps:** A flat amount per incident, commonly $50 to $150. Past that, you pay the rest.
- **Nearest qualified shop:** Some plans tow to the closest repair facility only, not to your preferred shop or your driveway.
- **Per-year limits:** Some plans allow a set number of calls per year, usually three or four.

For named examples, see the breakdowns on [State Farm mileage limits](/does-state-farm-roadside-assistance-have-mileage-limits/) and [Progressive's towing coverage limit](/progressive-roadside-assistance-towing-coverage-limit/).

## What Do I Do Right Now to Find Out My Limit?

Open your insurer's app or call the number on your insurance card, and ask for the tow distance and dollar limit before anyone dispatches a truck. This takes two minutes and prevents the most common roadside mistake: agreeing to a tow that blows past your coverage.

1. Get safe first. Hazards on, pull as far right as you can, and exit the vehicle on the passenger side if you're near traffic.
2. Open the insurer's app. Most show your roadside benefits under "Coverage" or "Roadside" with the mileage listed.
3. Call the roadside line and ask three things: the mileage cap, the dollar cap, and whether your chosen shop falls inside it.
4. Tell the driver where you want the car taken. If that destination is 40 miles away and your cap is 15, you owe the difference.

If you can't reach anyone, a paid tow is better than sitting in a dangerous spot. Ask your insurer about reimbursement afterward, and read [whether AAA pays back towing costs](/does-aaa-pay-back-towing-costs-if-i-already-called-a-tow-truck/) for how reimbursement claims tend to work.

## What Happens If the Shop Is Farther Than My Coverage Allows?

You pay the overage out of pocket, usually at a per-mile rate that runs roughly $3 to $7 beyond the included distance, though rates vary by region and tow company. The insurer covers its portion, and the driver bills you for the rest, typically at the scene.

You have three practical options:

- **Tow to the nearest shop within your limit.** Fix it there, or arrange a second move later.
- **Pay the overage.** Often cheaper than a second tow if the repair shop you trust is a bit past the cap.
- **Split the trip.** Tow within coverage to a safe location, then arrange the rest separately. This rarely saves money, so do the

## What If I Don't Have Roadside Coverage on My Policy?

Standard liability or even full coverage often does not include towing, because roadside assistance is usually a separate add-on. Check your declarations page for a line called "towing and labor" or "roadside assistance." If it's absent, you're paying cash.

Liability-only policies almost never include it, as covered in [does liability only insurance cover towing](/does-liability-only-insurance-cover-towing/). Without coverage, expect to pay a hookup fee plus mileage. Prices and options are laid out in [how much roadside assistance costs without insurance](/how-much-does-roadside-assistance-cost-without-insurance/).

Other places coverage may be hiding: a credit card benefit, a manufacturer warranty program, or a membership like AAA. Check all of them before paying a tow company directly.

## Does an Accident Change How Far My Insurance Will Tow?

Yes. A breakdown and a collision are handled differently, and the tow after an accident often falls under collision coverage or the at-fault driver's liability instead of your roadside add-on. That can mean a longer tow is covered, but it can also mean a deductible applies.

If the other driver caused it, their insurer typically pays for the tow, which is detailed in [who pays for towing after a not-at-fault accident](/who-pays-for-towing-after-a-car-accident-not-my-fault/). Save every receipt and photograph the car before it moves.

## Common Questions

**Q: Is there a standard towing distance all insurance companies use?**
A: No. Limits vary by insurer and by plan tier, from about 5 miles to 100 or more. Some use a dollar cap instead of miles. You have to check your own policy.

**Q: Does insurance tow my car to my house or only to a repair shop?**
A: Many plans tow to a repair facility or the nearest shop, and some allow your home if it's within the mileage cap. Ask the dispatcher before the tow begins, since the destination can change what's covered.

**Q: How many times a year will insurance tow me?**
A: Many roadside add-ons limit you to three or four service calls per year. After that, you pay for each tow yourself. Check your policy for the annual count.

**Q: Will using roadside assistance raise my insurance rates?**
A: Roadside calls are generally treated differently from accident claims, but practices vary by insurer. If the cost is small, ask your company whether a roadside call is logged against your record before filing.

**Q: What if I'm stranded and can't reach my insurance company?**
A: Prioritize safety and call a local tow company if you must. Keep the invoice, then submit it to your insurer for possible reimbursement. Not every plan reimburses, so confirm the policy afterward.
