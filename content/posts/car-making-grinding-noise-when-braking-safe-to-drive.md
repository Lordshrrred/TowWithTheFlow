Your car is making a grinding noise every time you hit the brakes. You need to know right now whether you can keep driving or whether you need to pull over and call for help.

> **Quick Answer:** A grinding noise when braking almost always means metal-on-metal contact, either worn brake pads grinding against the rotor or a caliper dragging. It is not safe to drive more than necessary. If the grinding is consistent, your stopping distance has already increased. Drive slowly to the nearest shop, or call a tow if the pedal feels soft, the car pulls hard to one side, or you smell burning. Do not get on a highway.

## How Bad Is a Grinding Noise When Braking, Really?

It's a real problem that gets worse every mile you drive. The most common cause is brake pads worn down to the metal backing plate, which is now grinding directly against your brake rotor. Every stop grinds the rotor thinner and hotter. You can also hear grinding from a seized caliper that never fully releases, a loose brake pad that has shifted in its bracket, or debris like a small rock trapped between the pad and rotor.

The rotor situation matters. A rotor can be resurfaced if it's still thick enough. Once it's deeply scored or warped from heat, it needs to be replaced entirely. Driving another 50 miles on a grinding brake can turn a $150 pad replacement into a $400 rotor-and-pad job.

## Can I Drive to the Shop or Do I Need a Tow Right Now?

You can drive short distances at low speeds under one condition: the pedal still feels firm and the car stops in a straight line. If both of those are true, you have some margin. Keep it under 35 mph, avoid hard stops, and get to a shop within a few miles.

Call a tow immediately if any of these are happening:

- The brake pedal is soft, spongy, or sinking toward the floor
- The car pulls hard to the left or right when you brake
- You smell something burning, especially near a wheel
- You hear grinding even when you are NOT braking (seized caliper)
- The grinding started suddenly and is extremely loud

A soft pedal means brake fluid is likely leaking or air has entered the line. That is a hydraulic failure, not just worn pads. At that point, you do not have reliable brakes. Pull over safely and call for a tow. ## What Is Actually Causing the Grinding?

The most likely causes, in order:

**Worn brake pads.** The friction material is gone and the steel backing plate is contacting the iron rotor. This is the most common cause, and most pads have a small metal wear indicator tab that squeals before it reaches this stage. If you ignored the squeal, you are now at the grinding stage.

**Scored or warped rotor.** Deep grooves in a rotor create a grinding or pulsating sensation. You may also feel it through the pedal or steering wheel.

**Seized brake caliper.** The caliper piston or slide pins can corrode and stick, holding the pad against the rotor even when you are not braking. This causes constant grinding, heat buildup, and a burning smell near one specific wheel. It can also cause the car to pull to that side.

**Loose wheel bearing.** A failing wheel bearing can produce a grinding or rumbling noise that changes with speed and feels worse in turns. It may seem worse during braking because weight shifts to the front axle. This is a separate issue but equally serious.

## What Should I Do Right Now if I'm on the Road?

Stay calm and slow down. Avoid highway speeds entirely. Do not brake hard.

1. Reduce speed gradually and find a safe place to pull over.
2. Check your brake pedal feel. Firm means you have some time. Soft or sinking means stop now.
3. Look at your tires for any visible leaking fluid near the wheels, which would indicate a caliper or brake line problem.
4. If you are on a highway, signal and move to the right shoulder as soon as possible. Read [How Long Can You Wait on the Side of a Highway for a Tow?](/how-long-can-you-wait-on-side-of-highway-for-tow/) before you step out of the car.
5. Call a shop or tow service. Tell them the pedal feel, which wheel sounds loudest, and whether the car pulls to one side.

If you are driving and the brakes suddenly feel like they are fading or the pedal drops toward the floor, downshift if you have a manual transmission, use the parking brake gently and progressively, and steer toward a safe surface.

## What Will This Cost to Fix?

Brake pad replacement on a typical front axle runs $150 to $300 at most shops, parts and labor combined. If you have also worn into the rotors, add $100 to $200 per axle for rotor replacement. A seized caliper runs $250 to $450 per corner depending on the vehicle. A wheel bearing is $200 to $500 depending on the car and whether it is a press-in or hub assembly design.

The longer you drive on grinding brakes, the more likely you are to damage rotors that could have been saved. Most shops can inspect brakes within an hour and give you a firm quote before any work starts. If you need a tow to get there, check what your roadside assistance actually covers before you call. ## Common Questions

**Q: My brakes grind only the first stop of the day, then stop. Is that normal?**
A: Light surface rust on rotors after rain or overnight sitting is normal and usually clears within one or two stops. If the grinding stops completely after the first application and does not return, it is likely rust. If it persists through multiple stops, it is pad or rotor wear.

**Q: Can I drive 20 miles on grinding brakes to get home?**
A: Only if the pedal is firm, the car stops straight, and there is no burning smell. Keep speeds low and avoid traffic that would require hard stops. Every mile adds rotor damage and increases risk.

**Q: How long do brakes last before they grind?**
A: Most brake pads last 30,000 to 70,000 miles depending on driving style, vehicle weight, and pad compound. City driving with frequent stops wears them faster. Most pads squeal as a warning before they reach the grinding stage.

**Q: Is it the front or rear brakes causing the grinding?**
A: Most grinding comes from the front. Front brakes do 70 to 80 percent of stopping work on most vehicles and wear faster. If you feel the grinding more through the steering wheel, it is front. If you feel it more in the seat or hear it from the rear only during light braking, it is rear.

**Q: Can I keep driving if only one wheel is grinding?**
A: One grinding wheel means uneven braking, which causes the car to pull to that side under hard braking. That is a safety hazard, not just a mechanical inconvenience. Get it inspected the same day.
