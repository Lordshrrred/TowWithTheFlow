> **Quick Answer:** Pull completely off the road, turn on your hazard lights, and stay in your car with your seatbelt on. Walk to the nearest emergency call box if one is visible, or tie something bright to your antenna and raise your hood to signal passing motorists and highway patrol. Do not walk along the travel lanes. Help will come faster than you think if you signal correctly.

## What Do I Do the Moment My Car Dies and I Have No Signal?

Get off the road first, signal second. Steer onto the shoulder as far right as possible, set your hazard lights flashing, and put your vehicle in park or set the parking brake. Then take a breath. Your car is the safest place to wait as long as you are fully off the travel lanes.

Once stopped, check your mirrors before opening any door. If you have road flares or reflective triangles in your trunk, place them 100 to 300 feet behind your vehicle on the shoulder, not in the lane. This gives approaching drivers time to react. A bright piece of clothing tied to the antenna or door handle signals distress to passing highway patrol officers, who are trained to stop for it.

## Are There Emergency Call Boxes on This Highway?

Many interstate and state highway systems still maintain emergency call boxes, usually mounted on blue or orange poles along the shoulder, spaced roughly every mile. On highways like I-10, I-80, and I-40, these have been standard infrastructure for decades. They connect directly to highway patrol or a state DOT dispatch center, no cell signal needed, just pick up and speak.

Look in both directions before assuming there are none nearby. A quarter-mile walk on the shoulder, facing traffic, may get you to one. If you walk, move as far from the travel lanes as the terrain allows and never turn your back on oncoming traffic.

## How Do I Signal for Help Without a Phone?

Raise your hood. That is the universal distress signal for a broken-down vehicle. Combine it with hazard lights and something bright (a jacket, a plastic bag, anything) tied visibly to the driver-side mirror or antenna.

Stay visible from inside the car. If someone stops, crack the window to talk. You do not have to get out. A good Samaritan can relay your location and vehicle description to 911 on your behalf. Give them the highway number, your direction of travel, and the last mile marker or exit sign you remember passing. Mile markers appear on small green signs every mile along the shoulder and are the most precise location tool available when you cannot use a map app. For more on flagging help without a phone, see [how to signal for help if your car breaks down and you have no phone](/how-to-signal-for-help-car-breaks-down-no-phone/).

## Is It Safer to Stay in the Car or Get Out?

Stay in the car unless it is on fire or you are in immediate danger from traffic. This is not a comfort preference, it is a safety statistic. Pedestrians on highway shoulders are struck and killed far more often than occupants of parked vehicles on the shoulder. Keep your seatbelt on while you wait. If a vehicle drifts toward you and a collision looks unavoidable, bail out the passenger side away from traffic.

## How Long Until Someone Finds Me If I Stay Put?

Highway patrol units actively scan shoulders for disabled vehicles, especially on interstates, and response times in most states are under 30 minutes during daylight on major corridors. At night or in rural stretches, it can take longer, but raised hoods and flashing hazards are spotted consistently during routine patrols.

If you have a passenger, one person stays with the car and one can attempt to walk to a call box. Never both leave. The car is your reference point for rescuers and your protection from weather. If your breakdown has a secondary danger, like a [smoking hood](/car-smoking-from-hood-on-highway-what-to-do/) or the car is near a blind curve, that changes the calculus. Move further down the shoulder to a safer position if the immediate spot is unusually dangerous, but keep hazards flashing and your hood up.

## Common Questions

**Q: Can 911 still work with no cell bars?**
A: Sometimes. In the United States, cell phones can connect a 911 call through any available carrier tower, not just your own, even when you show no signal bars. Try calling 911 before assuming it is impossible. The call may go through even with zero bars displayed.

**Q: What if I don't have flares or a reflective triangle in my car?**
A: Use what you have. A bright jacket or hoodie tied to your mirror, your trunk lid propped open, or your hazard lights alone are all recognized distress signals. Highway patrol officers are trained to stop for any vehicle displaying unusual behavior on the shoulder.

**Q: Should I try to walk to the next exit to find a phone?**
A: Only if the exit is clearly visible and you can walk entirely off the paved surface. Highway shoulders are dangerous on foot, especially at speed. If the exit is more than half a mile away or you cannot see it, stay with the car and wait for patrol.

**Q: What information should I give a passing driver who offers to call for help?**
A: Give them the highway number and direction (northbound, southbound), the last mile marker or exit number you saw, a description of your car (color, make, model), and confirm whether anyone in the vehicle is injured. That is everything a dispatcher needs.

**Q: Does AAA or roadside assistance work without cell service?**
A: Not directly. AAA and most roadside apps require a data or voice connection to dispatch help. However, if a passing driver or highway patrol contacts dispatch on your behalf and gives your location, a tow can still be sent. Once you are near a signal or a call box, contacting your roadside plan immediately is the right move.
