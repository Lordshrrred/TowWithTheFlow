> **Quick Answer:** Call 911 first if anyone is hurt or the car is blocking traffic. Then call your insurance company before you call a tow truck. That one-sentence order matters because your insurer may dispatch their own approved tower, cover the cost directly, and start your claim with documentation that protects you. If you call a random tow first, you may pay out of pocket and complicate reimbursement.

## What Do I Do Immediately After My Car Hydroplanes Into a Ditch?

Get off the road and stay there. If you can exit the vehicle safely, do it through the uphill door, move up the embankment away from traffic, and turn on your hazard lights before anything else.

Once you're out of the car and standing somewhere stable, take stock. Check for injuries. If anyone is hurt, call 911 right now. If the car is partially blocking a travel lane, call 911 regardless, because a disabled vehicle on a wet highway is a second-accident waiting to happen.

If no one is hurt and the car is fully in the ditch off the road, you have a minute to breathe. Use it to take photos. Photograph the ditch, the tire tracks in the water, the car's position, and any road markings or signs nearby. These images are your evidence. A car stuck in wet mud looks very different an hour later after a tow truck has worked it.

## Should I Call Insurance or a Tow Truck First?

Call insurance first. Most major carriers, including State Farm, GEICO, Allstate, and AAA, have 24-hour roadside dispatch. When you report the incident immediately, they log a timestamp, assign a claim number, and in many cases dispatch a tow truck for you at no upfront cost.

If you call a random tow company first, you own that bill. Reimbursement is possible but not guaranteed, and it requires extra paperwork. More importantly, some insurers treat a delay in reporting as a reason to scrutinize the claim more closely.

The one exception: if your car is in a dangerous position, water is rising, or you are on a high-speed highway with poor visibility, call 911 and let them handle traffic control and towing. Safety beats process every time. Once you're safe, call insurance from the shoulder or from inside the cab of the tow truck.

Check your policy for roadside assistance coverage before the call. If you have comprehensive coverage or a roadside add-on, the tow is likely covered. If you don't, see what [your insurance actually covers for a non-driveable car](/does-insurance-cover-towing-if-car-is-not-driveable/) before you commit to anything.

## Does My Insurance Cover a Hydroplane Accident and the Tow?

Yes, typically, if you carry comprehensive or collision coverage. A hydroplane where you hit a ditch with no other vehicle involved is usually filed under collision. The tow itself may fall under a separate roadside assistance rider.

A deductible will likely apply to the collision claim. Standard collision deductibles run $500 to $1,000. The tow cost on its own, for a car pulled from a ditch, typically runs $150 to $300 for a local pull depending on depth and terrain. A flatbed transport to a shop adds another $75 to $200 depending on distance.

If your car went deeper into soft mud or off a steep embankment, a standard tow truck may not be enough. Ditch recoveries that require a winch line or a heavy-duty wrecker cost more, sometimes $400 to $600 or higher. That's exactly when having your insurer involved from the start pays off. They may send a recovery specialist rather than a standard tower, and they document the job type for your claim.

## What If I'm Stuck in the Ditch and No One Is Coming?

Stay with the vehicle if conditions are safe and your car is fully off the road. Hazard lights on, engine off if there's any smell of fuel or burning. If you're on a rural stretch with no cell signal, use your emergency SOS feature if your phone supports it, most iPhones and Android phones since 2022 have satellite emergency messaging built in.

If you do have signal, call your insurer's roadside line first, then 511 for state road conditions and traffic management in most states. Do not stand near the road on a wet highway at night. The biggest risk after a ditch incident is not the car. It's another vehicle losing control on the same wet pavement and hitting you while you're standing outside.

## Common Questions

**Q: Will my insurance rates go up if I file a claim for hydroplaning into a ditch?**
A: Possibly, yes. A single-vehicle collision claim can trigger a rate increase at renewal, typically 10 to 20 percent depending on your carrier and driving history. If the tow and repair cost is close to your deductible, it may be worth paying out of pocket and not filing.

**Q: Can I get my car pulled out of a ditch without a tow truck?**
A: Sometimes. If the ditch is shallow and the ground is firm, a recovery strap and another vehicle with a proper tow hitch can work. But on wet, soft ground after a hydroplane, traction is poor and you risk getting the second vehicle stuck too. A professional with a winch is the safer call.

**Q: What type of tow truck do I need for a car in a ditch?**
A: A wheel-lift or flatbed tow truck handles most shallow ditch recoveries. If the car is buried in mud, sitting at a steep angle, or has gone down an embankment, you need a heavy-duty wrecker with a winch. Tell the dispatcher exactly what you see so they send the right equipment.

**Q: Does AAA cover ditch recoveries or just standard breakdowns?**
A: AAA covers vehicle extraction and towing after a hydroplane or ditch incident under most membership tiers, including Basic. The distance limits and whether recovery labor is included vary by membership level. Classic and Premier tiers offer longer tow distances and more comprehensive coverage.

**Q: Should I move my car out of the ditch before the police arrive?**
A: No, not if anyone was injured or if the location is on a public road. Police may need to document the scene for an accident report, which your insurance will want. If you move the car first, you lose that documentation. Wait unless the car is a safety hazard to passing traffic.
