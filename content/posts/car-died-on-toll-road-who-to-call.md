> **Quick Answer:** Call the toll authority first, not 911 (unless you are in immediate danger). Every major toll road in the U.S. has its own incident response team, and they can reach you faster than a private tow truck. After that call, contact your roadside assistance provider or a tow company. Get off the travel lanes and stay behind a guardrail while you wait.

## Who Do I Call First When My Car Dies on a Toll Road?

Call the toll road's emergency line first. Look for the number on the toll plaza signage, the toll authority's website, or the printed receipt from your last transaction. If you cannot find it, dial 511, the national traveler information number, which most states connect to their DOT traffic management system. They can dispatch a road ranger or incident response vehicle directly to your location. These responders are free to drivers on most toll roads and can push your car to a safe area, change a flat, or provide a fuel can.

If you are in immediate physical danger, call 911. Otherwise, save that line for true emergencies. An idle car in a travel lane is serious, but toll road patrols are trained specifically for this situation and will often reach you faster than local police.

After you have made contact with the toll authority, call your insurance roadside assistance, AAA, or a private tow company to arrange a tow to a shop if the car cannot be quickly restarted.

## How Do I Stay Safe Until Help Arrives?

Turn on your hazard lights the moment the car dies. If you have any momentum left, steer toward the right shoulder or, better, an emergency pull-off. Do not stop in a live travel lane any longer than it takes to coast to the side.

Once stopped, get everyone out of the car and move behind the guardrail or up the embankment, away from traffic. Do not stand behind or beside the vehicle. If you have emergency triangles or road flares, place them at 50 to 100 feet behind the car to warn approaching drivers. For a solid breakdown of how those two warning options compare, see [emergency triangle vs flares on the highway](/emergency-triangle-vs-flares-which-is-safer-on-highway/).

Stay on the phone with dispatch until help arrives. Give them the nearest milepost marker or overhead sign number. Those green or white markers along the right side of the roadway are your most precise location identifier on a toll road, far more useful than a mile estimate.

## Will the Toll Road's Road Ranger Actually Tow My Car?

Road rangers on most toll roads will push or drive your car to the nearest safe pull-off or emergency crossover, but they are not a free tow to a shop. Their job is to clear the travel lanes. Once you are in a safe staging area, you still need a tow truck to transport the vehicle to a repair facility.

Some toll authorities will coordinate a tow directly and bill you later. Others will wait with you until your tow truck arrives. Ask the road ranger when they reach you exactly what they can do and whether they can hold until your tow arrives, since policies vary by state and toll operator.

If you are dealing with a more serious mechanical failure like a seized engine, your roadside situation is more involved. The article on [engine seized while driving](/engine-seized-while-driving-need-emergency-tow/) walks through the towing specifics for that scenario.

## What If I Do Not Have Roadside Assistance and Cannot Afford a Tow?

You still have options. The toll road's road ranger service is free in most states, and they can at minimum move you out of danger. Once you are safely off the travel lanes, you can use apps like Urgently or Honk to compare tow truck quotes in real time. You can also call local shops near the next exit directly. Independent shops sometimes dispatch their own trucks for less than a national dispatch service.

## Common Questions

**Q: Do toll roads have their own emergency phone number?**
A: Yes. Every major toll authority in the U.S. operates its own incident management line, and many post the number on the toll plaza, overhead signs, and their website. If you cannot find it, dial 511 to reach your state's DOT traffic line, which can connect you to the right responders.

**Q: Will I get charged a toll violation if my car breaks down on the toll road?**
A: No. Once you notify the toll authority of a breakdown, the violation is waived. Make sure you actually call them or speak with a road ranger so there is a record of the incident tied to your plate or account.

**Q: Can a tow truck come directly onto the toll road to get me?**
A: Yes, tow trucks are authorized to access toll roads. The toll authority can sometimes coordinate this. If you are calling a private tow company, give them the milepost number and direction of travel so their driver can find you quickly.

**Q: What if my car dies in the toll booth lane itself?**
A: Put it in neutral immediately and push it to the side if you can, or wave down a toll worker. Toll plazas have staff or cameras monitoring all lanes, and someone will respond within minutes. This is one of the safer places to break down on a toll road.

**Q: Is it safe to wait in my car on a toll road shoulder?**
A: No. Get out of the car and move behind the guardrail. Vehicles stopped on highway shoulders are struck by passing traffic with alarming regularity. Distance from the car is your best protection while you wait for help.
