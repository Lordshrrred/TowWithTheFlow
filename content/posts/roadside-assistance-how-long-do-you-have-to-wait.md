> **Quick Answer:** Most roadside assistance calls get a truck on scene in 30 to 90 minutes. Dispatch usually quotes an ETA when you call. Expect longer waits during storms, rush hour, holidays, and in rural areas, sometimes two hours or more. Give your exact location, say you're in a dangerous spot if you are, and call back for an update if the ETA passes.

## How Long Does Roadside Assistance Usually Take to Arrive?

Plan on 30 to 90 minutes for a normal call in a city or suburb. Dispatchers quote an estimated arrival time when you report the problem, and that quote is your best number.

Jump-starts and tire changes tend to land on the faster end because any nearby service truck can handle them. Tows take longer because dispatch needs the right equipment, and a flatbed has to be free and close to you.

Rural highways and long stretches between exits push waits past two hours. A truck may be coming from the next county.

## What Makes the Wait Longer?

Weather, traffic, and demand cause most long waits. The first cold snap of the year is the classic example: dead batteries spike all at once and every provider's queue backs up.

The usual culprits:

- **Storms, snow, and ice.** Trucks slow down and calls multiply.
- **Rush hour.** A truck stuck in congestion can't reach you quickly.
- **Holidays and weekends.** Fewer drivers on shift, more cars on the road.
- **Remote locations.** Fewer trucks, longer drives.
- **Unclear location.** Wrong exit or mile marker means a driver circling for you.

Your coverage plan can matter too. Some programs route calls to local contractors, and response depends on who is free nearby. Others have dedicated fleets in busy areas. ## How Do I Get Help Faster?

Give dispatch precise information and don't hang up without an ETA. Most delays I see come from bad location details.

1. **Use mile markers and exits.** "I-70 eastbound, mile marker 262, right shoulder" beats "somewhere past the mall." Drop a pin in your phone's map app and read the cross street or send the link if the provider has an app.
2. **Say if you're in danger.** A car stuck in a travel lane, on a blind curve, or on a bridge gets prioritized. Tell them plainly.
3. **Use the provider's app.** Many let you request service, share GPS, and track the truck, which cuts back-and-forth calls.
4. **Ask for an ETA and a callback number.** If the time passes, call back instead of waiting silently.
5. **Know what you need.** "Dead battery" or "flat tire, spare is good" sends the right truck the first time.

If the quote is unacceptable, you can call a local towing company directly and pay out of pocket, then ask your provider about reimbursement. Check that first. On a highway, the safest place is usually outside the car, behind the guardrail, upwind and well away from the lane. Staying inside makes sense only if you can't get clear of traffic, such as on a bridge or a narrow shoulder. In that case keep your seatbelt on and hazards flashing.

A few specifics:

- Don't stand between your car and traffic or behind it.
- At night, leave the dome light off and hazards on so you're visible but not blinding.
- If it's cold, run the engine only if the car is safe to run and exhaust isn't blocked by snow.
- In heat, get shade and water before you worry about anything else.

## When Is Waiting Too Long, and What Are My Other Options?

If the truck is more than 30 minutes past the quoted ETA, call and push for a status update. Past that point, you have a real decision to make. We break this down fully in [how long you can wait for a tow truck on the highway](/how-long-can-you-wait-for-a-tow-truck-on-highway/).

Your options:

- **Ask dispatch to escalate.** They can re-dispatch to a closer truck.
- **Call a local tow company yourself.** You'll pay and seek reimbursement later.
- **Call 911 if you're in danger.** A car stuck in a live lane or a medical issue is an emergency, not a roadside call. In some areas police or highway patrol can stage with flares or push a car clear.
- **Check your state's road conditions line.** Dialing 511 can show closures or crashes causing the delay.

## Common Questions

**Q: How long should roadside assistance take to get to me?**
A: Typically 30 to 90 minutes in cities and suburbs. Rural areas, bad weather, and holidays can stretch that past two hours. Dispatch gives you an estimate when you call.

**Q: Can I cancel and call someone else if the wait is too long?**
A: Yes. Tell your provider you're canceling before a second tow company arrives, or you could be billed for both. Ask whether the second call can be reimbursed.

**Q: Does roadside assistance prioritize dangerous situations?**
A: Usually, yes, if you tell them. Say you're in a live lane, on a blind curve, or on a bridge. Safety situations get moved up the queue.

**Q: Is the wait shorter if I pay for premium roadside coverage?**
A: Not always. Higher tiers mostly add miles, more services, and fewer out-of-pocket costs. Dispatch speed depends on truck availability near you, not just your plan.

**Q: What if the driver never shows up?**
A: Call dispatch back, give your exact location again, and ask them to re-dispatch. If it's still going nowhere, call a local tow company directly and keep your receipt.
