> **Quick Answer:** Do not drive an overheating car to the mechanic. The risk of warping the cylinder head or cracking the engine block is real and immediate, and those repairs run $1,500 to $5,000 or more. A local tow costs $75 to $150 in most cities. That math is not close. Shut it down, call a tow, and save yourself a far bigger bill.

## Is It Actually Dangerous to Drive an Overheating Car, Even a Short Distance?

Yes. Even a mile or two of driving on an overheated engine can warp the aluminum cylinder head, which warps because aluminum distorts at lower temperatures than the steel block below it. Once that seal is gone, you are looking at a blown head gasket at minimum, and often a cracked head or scored cylinder walls. Those are not $300 repairs.

The temperature gauge creeping into the red or the warning light flashing means coolant is already failing to keep up. Every additional minute you drive compounds the heat soak into metal parts that cannot recover once they deform.

## How Much Does Towing an Overheated Car Actually Cost?

A local tow for an overheated car runs $75 to $150 for the first few miles, with per-mile rates of $3 to $7 after that. Flatbed towing, which most shops prefer for AWD and low-clearance vehicles, adds $20 to $40 over a standard wheel-lift. If you are stranded far from a shop, say 20 miles out, expect $130 to $225 total out of pocket without roadside coverage.

For context, a warped cylinder head replacement on a common four-cylinder engine like the 2.5L found in a Toyota Camry or Honda Accord typically runs $1,000 to $2,500 in parts and labor. An engine block crack pushes that closer to a full engine replacement. The towing cost is trivial by comparison.

If you have roadside assistance through your insurer or AAA, the tow may cost you nothing. Check your policy before you call a private company. See [does State Farm roadside assistance cover towing](/does-state-farm-roadside-assistance-cover-towing/) if you are covered through them.

## When Should I Pull Over, and What Do I Do While Waiting for the Tow?

Pull over the moment the temperature gauge hits the red zone or a high-temp warning light appears. Do not wait to see if it comes back down. Get completely off the road, turn on your hazards, and shut the engine off.

Do not open the radiator cap. Coolant under pressure can be 250 degrees or hotter and will cause serious burns. If you see steam coming from under the hood, keep your distance and let it vent on its own. Once the engine has cooled for at least 30 minutes, you can safely check the coolant reservoir (the translucent plastic tank, not the cap on the radiator itself).

Set up emergency triangles or flares behind your vehicle if you are on a shoulder. For guidance on which is safer to use, see [emergency triangle vs flares on the highway](/emergency-triangle-vs-flares-which-is-safer-on-highway/).

Call your tow service, give them your exact location, and stay in the vehicle with your seatbelt on if you are on a highway shoulder.

## What If the Mechanic Is Only Half a Mile Away, Is That Close Enough to Risk It?

No. Half a mile of driving on a fully overheated engine can still warp a head or seize a water pump. There is no safe distance once the gauge is in the red.

The one partial exception: if the car cooled down completely, you refilled the coolant reservoir, and the gauge is reading normal, you might nurse it very slowly to a nearby shop while watching the temp gauge the entire time. The instant it starts climbing again, you pull over. This is only reasonable if you genuinely understand the risk and the mechanic is within a mile. If there is any doubt, tow it

## Does It Matter Why the Car Is Overheating When Deciding Whether to Tow?

The cause affects what the mechanic will fix, but it does not change your decision to tow rather than drive. A low coolant level from a slow leak is different from a failed water pump or a clogged radiator, but all three can destroy your engine equally fast if you keep driving.

One situation where the cause matters before you call: if the car is overheating but the coolant reservoir is completely full, that points toward a thermostat failure, a blown head gasket already in progress, or a water pump issue. The answer there is still to tow, but it helps you tell the shop what to check first.

## Common Questions

**Q: Can I let the car cool down for an hour and then drive it to the shop?**
A: Sometimes, with caution. If the car cools completely, you refill the coolant reservoir to the correct level, and the temperature gauge holds steady at normal, a very short drive may be possible. Watch the gauge every 30 seconds. If it starts climbing, pull over immediately and call a tow. Do not attempt this if the car is more than a mile or two from the shop.

**Q: How do I know if I already caused engine damage by driving while overheating?**
A: The clearest signs are white smoke from the exhaust, a sweet smell from the tailpipe, coolant mixing into the oil (oil looks milky or foamy on the dipstick), or the engine running rough or misfiring after it cools. Any of those means the head gasket is likely already compromised. Tell the shop specifically that the car overheated before it was towed.

**Q: Will my car insurance or roadside assistance pay for the tow?**
A: It depends on your policy. Many comprehensive auto policies include roadside assistance or offer it as an add-on, and that typically covers towing to the nearest qualified shop. AAA covers towing to any shop you choose, within mileage limits based on your membership tier. Call your insurance company or check your app before you book a private tow to avoid paying out of pocket unnecessarily.

**Q: What is the most common reason a car overheats suddenly while driving?**
A: A failed water pump or a burst radiator hose accounts for a large share of sudden overheating on the road. The water pump impeller can fail without warning, stopping coolant circulation entirely. You may hear a grinding noise from the front of the engine just before or as it fails. A burst hose usually announces itself with a sudden cloud of steam.

**Q: Is it worth fixing an overheated car or should I just sell it?**
A: It depends on whether engine damage occurred and what the car is worth. If you pulled over quickly and only need the original cause fixed (a hose, thermostat, or water pump), repairs are typically $150 to $600 and worth doing on a reliable vehicle. If the head gasket is blown or the head is warped, get a repair estimate and compare it to the car's current market value before committing. A mechanic can do a compression test and coolant pressure test to tell you what you are dealing with.
