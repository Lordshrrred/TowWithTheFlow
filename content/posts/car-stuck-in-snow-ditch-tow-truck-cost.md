## How Much Does It Cost to Get a Car Pulled Out of a Snow Ditch?

A basic ditch pull runs $75 to $150 in most markets. That covers a tow truck hooking up, using a cable or chain to drag your car back onto the road, and nothing more. No flatbed, no transport to a shop.

The price climbs fast when conditions get complicated:

- **Winch-out only (no tow needed):** $75 to $150
- **Winch-out plus tow to a shop:** $150 to $350 depending on distance
- **Deep ditch, steep embankment, or off-road recovery:** $200 to $500+
- **Rural location far from the nearest town:** Add $50 to $150 for the extra drive time

The difference between $100 and $400 usually comes down to one thing: angle. A car that slid off the road gently onto a flat shoulder is simple. A car that went hood-first down a steep embankment requires a heavy-duty winch, sometimes a second truck, and real labor. If your car is sitting at a severe angle, be upfront with the dispatcher about it so they send the right equipment.

## Does Insurance or Roadside Assistance Pay for a Snow Ditch Recovery?

Most roadside assistance plans cover a winch-out or ditch pull as a standard service call. AAA, for example, covers towing and basic extractions under all membership tiers, though reimbursement limits vary by plan level.

If you have comprehensive auto insurance with roadside assistance added on, check your policy before you call a random tow company. Progressive, GEICO, State Farm, and Allstate all offer towing and roadside riders that typically include extraction. Using that coverage costs you nothing out of pocket beyond your plan fee. Calling a private tow company first and asking for reimbursement later is slower and sometimes only partially covered. For more on how insurance covers these situations, see [does insurance cover towing if car is not driveable](/does-insurance-cover-towing-if-car-is-not-driveable/) and what Progressive specifically pays out at [Progressive insurance towing coverage](/progressive-insurance-towing-coverage-how-much-will-they-pay/).

One catch: if someone else caused the accident that put you in the ditch, their liability coverage should pay for the tow. Document everything before the truck arrives.

## Is It Safe to Stay in the Car While Waiting for a Tow in the Snow?

Yes, staying in your car is almost always safer than standing outside in cold, snowy conditions, with one important exception: if your car is in a travel lane or on a blind curve, get out and move well away from traffic.

If you're safely off the road in the ditch:

1. Turn on your hazard lights immediately.
2. Keep the engine running for heat only if the exhaust pipe is clear of snow. A blocked exhaust pipe forces carbon monoxide into the cabin. Check it before you run the engine.
3. Crack a window slightly to allow fresh air circulation.
4. Stay buckled. Another vehicle losing control on the same icy road can hit yours.
5. Call for help, then call someone who knows where you are.

At night, a car in a ditch on a dark rural highway is nearly invisible to other drivers. If you have road flares or reflective triangles, place them 100 to 200 feet behind your vehicle. Sometimes yes, but be honest with yourself before you try. If the car is on flat ground, the wheels are still on snow rather than ice, and the front or back hasn't dropped into a real ditch, you have options.

Things worth trying first:

- **Rock the car:** Shift between Drive and Reverse repeatedly in short bursts. Don't spin the wheels hard. Gentle rocking builds momentum.
- **Clear around the tires:** Dig snow away from the front and back of each tire with a shovel, floor mat, or whatever you have.
- **Traction under the tires:** Kitty litter, sand, cardboard, or rubber floor mats under the drive wheels can give enough grip to get moving.
- **Ask for a push:** Two people pushing while you give it gentle throttle is often enough on a mild slide-off.

If the car is hung up on the frame, the undercarriage is resting on packed snow, or a tire is off the ground entirely, stop. Trying to power out in that situation can damage your drivetrain, and you still won't move. Call the tow truck. ## Who Pays If the Ditch Is on a Highway or State Road?

You pay, unless someone else caused the incident. Being on a public highway does not mean the state covers your recovery. State DOT crews remove hazards from travel lanes, but extracting your privately owned vehicle from a ditch is your responsibility.

If road conditions were unusually dangerous and unmarked, you could theoretically pursue a claim against a municipality, but that's a long legal road and not a solution when you're sitting in a snow ditch right now. Your fastest path is your insurance company or roadside assistance plan. ## Common Questions

**Q: How long does it take for a tow truck to reach me in a snowstorm?**
A: During active winter storms, wait times stretch to 2 to 4 hours or longer because every truck in the area is handling multiple calls. Call immediately, give your exact location including the nearest mile marker or exit number, and stay in the car.

**Q: Will a tow truck damage my car during a snow ditch extraction?**
A: A professional using proper rigging points on your frame will not damage the car. The risk comes from inexperienced operators who hook to a bumper or suspension component. Ask where they plan to attach before they start.

**Q: Do I need a flatbed or will a wheel-lift truck work for a ditch recovery?**
A: Most simple ditch pulls use a wheel-lift or cable winch, not a flatbed. A flatbed is only needed if the car cannot roll or if there is suspected frame or drivetrain damage that makes driving it unsafe after extraction.

**Q: Should I call a tow truck or try 911 first when stuck in a snow ditch?**
A: Call 911 if you are injured, blocking a travel lane, or in immediate danger. Otherwise call your roadside assistance provider or a tow company directly. 911 dispatchers will often connect you to the right resource anyway, but non-emergency situations should skip the emergency line.

**Q: Does AAA cover a snow ditch extraction, or is that considered off-road recovery?**
A: AAA covers standard winch-outs from ditches adjacent to public roads under all membership tiers. True off-road recovery in a field or remote terrain may fall outside standard coverage. Call your AAA regional dispatch to confirm before the truck hooks up.
