> **Quick Answer:** Grip the wheel with both hands and keep the car straight. Ease off the gas and do not slam the brakes. Let the car slow on its own, turn on your hazards, and steer toward the right shoulder. Once stopped, get everyone out on the side away from traffic, stand behind the guardrail, and call for help. Don't change the tire on a freeway shoulder.

## What Do I Do in the First Few Seconds After a Tire Blows?

Hold the steering wheel firmly with both hands and steer straight. The pull and the bang will feel violent, but the car is controllable if you don't overreact.

Do these in order:

1. Keep your eyes up and aim where you want the car to go.
2. Lift off the gas smoothly. Don't stomp anything.
3. Turn on your hazard lights.
4. Let the car slow down to about 30 mph or less before you touch the brake. Then use light, steady pressure.
5. Check your mirrors, signal, and drift toward the right shoulder.

The biggest mistake is panic braking or a hard steering correction. A blown front tire yanks the car toward that side. A rear blowout makes the back end wobble. Either way, a sudden brake stab or jerk of the wheel is what causes spins and rollovers.

## How Do I Get to the Shoulder Safely?

Steer toward the right shoulder gradually and get as far from the travel lane as you can. If you can't reach a clean shoulder, a ramp, a wide gore area, or even a slow crawl on the rim is better than stopping in a live lane.

Yes, you can limp on a shredded tire for a short distance. You'll ruin the tire and probably scar the wheel, but a $300 wheel is cheaper than a rear-end collision. Keep speed under 15 mph if you're rolling on the rim.

On a left-side shoulder or median, only stop there if that's truly where you ended up. See [car broke down on interstate median](/car-broke-down-on-interstate-median-what-to-do/) for how to handle that, because the risks are different.

## What Do I Do Once I'm Stopped?

Get everyone out of the car on the passenger side, away from traffic, and move behind the guardrail or well off the shoulder. Do not sit in the car. Cars parked on freeway shoulders get hit, and the car is the most dangerous place to be.

Then:

- Leave the hazards on. Turn your wheels away from the road if you're on a grade.
- Call roadside assistance, 911 if you feel unsafe or are in a live lane, or 511 for your state's road conditions and highway help info.
- Stay with passengers and kids away from the road edge. If you have a baby with you, our guide on [being stranded on the highway with a baby](/stranded-on-highway-with-baby-what-to-do-roadside-emergency/) covers the specifics.

Don't set out flares or cones on a busy freeway if it means walking into traffic. Your safety beats your car's visibility.

## Should I Change the Tire Myself or Call for Help?

On a freeway shoulder, call for help. A fast shoulder tire change with trucks passing at 70 mph is one of the most common ways people

Change it yourself only if all of these are true: the shoulder is wide, you're well clear of the white line, the ground is flat and firm, you have a real spare and a working jack, and traffic is light. If any one is missing, wait for a pro.

Check what you actually have before you count on it. Many newer cars carry a compact "donut" spare or only an inflator kit, which does nothing for a blowout. A donut is rated for roughly 50 mph and short distances, so plan to get a real tire right away.

Does your coverage include this? Check [whether roadside assistance covers a flat tire or just towing](/does-roadside-assistance-cover-flat-tire-or-just-towing/) before you call, so you know what to ask for.

## Do I Need a Tow, and What Will It Cost?

You need a tow if you have no usable spare, the wheel or suspension is damaged, or you can't safely change the tire where you stopped. If you have a good spare and a safe spot, a roadside tire change is cheaper than a tow.

Costs vary by city and time of day, and freeway calls often run higher. Expect a basic roadside tire change to cost less than a tow, and a flatbed to cost more than a hook-and-chain. Nights and holidays add surcharges. See [how much emergency towing costs on weekends](/how-much-does-emergency-towing-cost-on-weekends/) for how timing changes the bill.

## Common Questions

**Q: Can I drive on a blown tire to the next exit?**
A: Only a short distance, slowly, and only if the shoulder is unsafe. Driving on the rim destroys the wheel and can cause you to lose control. If you can stop safely, stop.

**Q: Why did my tire blow out on the freeway?**
A: Usually underinflation, heat, a worn or aged tire, or road debris like a truck-tire fragment or a pothole strike. Low pressure builds heat at highway speed and is a common cause.

**Q: Is it safe to use a tire sealant or inflator kit on a blowout?**
A: No. Those kits are for small punctures in the tread. A real blowout leaves a torn sidewall or shredded tire that sealant can't fix, so you'll need a spare or a tow.

**Q: Should I replace just one tire after a blowout?**
A: Often yes if the others are in good shape, but on all-wheel-drive cars you may need to match tread depth across all four. Have a shop measure before you buy one.

**Q: Will my insurance cover a blown tire?**
A: Standard liability doesn't. Some comprehensive policies cover damage from road debris, and roadside add-ons may cover the tow or tire change. Check your policy or call your insurer.
