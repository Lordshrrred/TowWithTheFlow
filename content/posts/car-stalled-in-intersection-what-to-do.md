> **Quick Answer:** Put your hazard lights on immediately. If the car won't restart in one or two tries, get out and push it clear of traffic, or stay inside with your seatbelt on if pushing is not safe. Call 911 if you're blocking a busy intersection. Don't keep cranking the engine. Once you're out of traffic, then figure out whether you need a tow.

## What Do I Do the Second My Car Stalls in an Intersection?

Hazard lights first, every time. Reach for that button before you do anything else. It buys you a few seconds of visibility while you think.

Try a restart once, maybe twice. If the engine catches, drive clear of the intersection immediately, even if you just limp to a parking lot. If it doesn't start, stop cranking. Repeated cranking on a stalled engine can flood the intake on older carbureted engines or drain what's left of your battery on modern ones.

If you cannot move the car under its own power, your next call is whether it's safer to stay inside or get out and push. In light traffic, unlock the doors, get someone to help steer while others push from behind, and aim for the nearest curb or parking lot entrance. In heavy, fast-moving traffic, stay belted inside. A moving car hitting yours is safer to survive when you're strapped in than when you're standing behind the bumper.

## Is It Safe to Push a Car Out of an Intersection?

It depends entirely on traffic speed and volume. On a quiet neighborhood street with two or three cars waiting, pushing is the right move. On a four-lane arterial with cars moving at 45 mph, pushing puts you in the lane.

If you decide to push, keep it controlled. One person in the driver's seat to steer and brake, everyone else at the rear corners, not directly behind the bumper. Aim for a right turn into a parking lot or a curb, not a straight push across traffic.

If you are alone and pushing is unsafe, call 911. Dispatchers can send police to direct traffic around your car while you wait. This is exactly what that call is for.

## When Should I Call 911 vs. a Tow Truck?

Call 911 first if you are blocking active traffic and cannot move the car. Police can control the intersection and keep other drivers from hitting you. Get the scene secured before you worry about the mechanical problem.

Once you are out of the intersection and off the road, 911 is no longer your priority. At that point, call a tow truck or your roadside assistance provider. If your car stalled because of a dead battery or a flooded engine on a hot day, a jump start or a short wait might fix it. If the engine just cut out without warning and will not turn over at all, plan on a tow.

Common causes of sudden stalls in intersections include a failed fuel pump, a crankshaft position sensor failure, a dying alternator that dropped voltage below the engine management threshold, or a completely dead battery. None of those are safe to diagnose at an intersection. Get the car somewhere safe first. If you suspect a fuel delivery problem, the [fuel pump failed on highway guide](/fuel-pump-failed-on-highway-signs-and-next-steps-2/) covers the symptoms and next steps.

## What If My Hazard Lights Won't Work or My Battery Is Dead?

If the battery is completely dead, your hazards may not work. A dead battery after a stall usually means the alternator failed while you were driving, not that the battery simply ran down overnight.

In this case, use whatever you have: a flashlight from the glovebox, a phone

## What Happens After the Car Is Safe: Do I Need a Tow?

If the car restarted and you drove it clear, you still need to figure out why it stalled. A one-time stall from vapor lock or a brief sensor glitch is different from a car that stalled, restarted rough, and now has a flashing check engine light. A [flashing check engine light with shaking](/check-engine-light-flashing-car-shaking-pull-over-now/) is a signal to stop driving immediately and call for a tow.

If the car would not restart at all, you need a tow. Do not let anyone talk you into a roadside repair in a parking lot for a problem you cannot verify. Get it to a shop where it can be properly diagnosed on level ground with the right tools.

If you don't have roadside coverage, expect to pay between $75 and $150 for a local tow in most metro areas, more in high-cost cities. Knowing what to expect before you call prevents getting taken advantage of in a stressful moment.

## Common Questions

**Q: Can I leave my car in the intersection and walk to safety?**
A: Only if staying in or near the car is an immediate danger, like a fire or a collision that already happened. An unattended car blocking an intersection is a serious hazard to other drivers. Stay with the car, use your hazards, and call 911 to get traffic control on scene.

**Q: Why did my car stall suddenly with no warning?**
A: The most common culprits are a failed crankshaft position sensor, a failing fuel pump, a dead alternator dropping system voltage, or a faulty ignition switch. A sudden stall with no restart is more likely a sensor or fuel delivery issue than a battery problem.

**Q: Should I try to restart the car multiple times while blocking traffic?**
A: Give it one or two attempts, then stop. Repeated cranking wastes battery, can flood the engine on some vehicles, and delays the more important decisions about pushing clear or calling for help.

**Q: Will my insurance cover a tow from an intersection stall?**
A: It depends on your policy. Roadside assistance add-ons through carriers like State Farm or Progressive typically cover towing, but standard liability-only policies do not. Check your policy app before you call a tow truck so you know whether to route the bill through insurance or pay out of pocket.

**Q: What if other drivers are honking and getting aggressive while I'm stalled?**
A: Keep your doors locked if you feel unsafe. Turn on your hazards, stay calm, and call 911. Aggressive drivers are not your problem to manage. Police dispatch can get someone there to control the scene. Your only job is to stay safe until help arrives.
