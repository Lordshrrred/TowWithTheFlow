> **Quick Answer:** Get as far right as possible, ideally past the active work zone. Turn on hazards immediately. Stay in your vehicle with your seatbelt on unless fire or smoke forces you out. Call 911 first, not a tow truck. Construction zones have designated traffic control, and emergency services there can respond faster and more safely than anywhere else on a highway.

## What Do I Do the Moment My Car Breaks Down Inside a Construction Zone?

Keep moving if you can. Even a slow roll of 50 to 100 feet can get you past a lane taper, a concrete barrier gap, or a work crew. The second the car stops, hazard lights on. Do not get out.

Construction zones compress traffic into narrower lanes, often with no real shoulder. Drivers are already distracted by signage, reduced speeds, and lane changes. A stopped car becomes nearly invisible until someone is 30 feet away. Your job in the first 60 seconds is to avoid becoming a secondary collision.

Once stopped:
- Keep seatbelt buckled.
- Turn wheels toward the barrier or guardrail, not traffic.
- Call 911. Tell them the highway number, the direction you're traveling, and the nearest construction zone marker or milepost if you can see one. Construction zones often have posted project numbers or DOT reference signs.
- Do not call a tow truck first. Emergency services need to know you're there and will often coordinate with the tow.

If you have a passenger, send them a text or have them call while you focus on controlling the vehicle.

## Is It Safe to Get Out of the Car in a Construction Zone?

No, not unless the car is on fire, smoking heavily, or you have a medical emergency. Staying inside a modern vehicle gives you far more protection from a passing truck than standing outside does. The steel frame and airbag system absorb impact. Your body does not.

If you absolutely must exit, do it from the passenger side away from traffic. Move immediately behind the nearest concrete barrier or jersey wall if one is present. Do not stand behind your vehicle. A rear-end strike at 45 mph will push your car into you faster than you can react. Crouch low behind the barrier and stay there until emergency personnel arrive.

For more on managing a high-danger highway stop, the steps in [Broken Down on Highway at Night: Safety Tips and What to Do Right Now](/broken-down-on-highway-at-night-safety-tips-what-to-do/) apply directly here.

## Do I Use Flares or Emergency Triangles in a Construction Zone?

Skip both if you're in an active work lane or surrounded by construction equipment. Flares near fuel, hydraulic fluid, or sawdust from cutting operations are a fire risk. Triangles placed in a lane taper can confuse drivers who are already reacting to orange cones and lane shifts.

If you're stopped on a cleared shoulder or past the active work area, reflective triangles are your safer option. Place the first one directly behind your car, the second about 100 feet back, and the third about 300 feet back. In a standard construction zone with reduced speed limits (commonly 55 mph or lower), those distances give drivers a fighting chance to react.

## Who Do I Actually Call, and What Do I Tell Them?

Call 911 first. Give them: the highway number and direction, the closest milepost or construction zone sign, and what the car is doing (smoking, won't start, flat tire). They will dispatch highway patrol and notify the construction zone's traffic control coordinator. Most active construction sites have a dedicated flagger or crew supervisor who can slow or stop traffic in your lane while you wait.

After 911, call your roadside assistance provider or a tow company. Be specific: tell them it's an active construction zone, because the tow driver needs to approach correctly and may need a police escort to reach you safely.

If your issue is mechanical and you're trying to diagnose whether you even need a tow, [Engine Seized While Driving: What to Do and How to Get an Emergency Tow](/engine-seized-while-driving-need-emergency-tow/) walks through one of the worst-case scenarios in detail.

## What Happens After Help Arrives?

Stay in or behind the barrier until the tow truck driver or officer tells you it's clear to move. Do not walk toward the tow truck as it approaches. Let them come to you.

The tow driver will position the truck to block traffic and give you a safe path to the truck cab or a spot behind the barrier. If law enforcement is on scene, follow their directions exactly. They may need to close a lane or position a patrol car as a buffer before the tow can hook up safely.

Once the car is loaded, ride in the tow truck. Do not walk away from the scene along the road inside a construction zone. Flaggers are managing a moving work zone, not a pedestrian path.

## Common Questions

**Q: Can I just push my car out of the construction zone myself?**
A: Only if you have people to help and a clear path to a safe stopping point. Never push a car into active traffic. If there's a gap in the barrier or a cleared shoulder within 50 feet, it may be worth it. If not, leave it and wait for help.

**Q: Will I get a ticket for breaking down in a construction zone?**
A: No. A mechanical breakdown is not a traffic violation. Police may run your plates and check your registration, but breaking down is not a citable offense.

**Q: How long will a tow take to reach me in an active construction zone?**
A: Longer than a standard highway stop. The tow driver may need a police escort or to wait for a lane closure. Budget 45 to 90 minutes in a live work zone versus 30 to 45 on a normal highway shoulder.

**Q: What if my car breaks down in a construction zone at night?**
A: Night adds serious risk because visibility drops sharply in zones with temporary lighting. Stay in the car, keep hazards on, and call 911 immediately. Do not attempt to place triangles in the dark without a reflective vest.

**Q: Does my roadside assistance cover a tow out of a construction zone?**
A: Yes, a tow is a tow as far as most plans are concerned. The zone itself does not affect coverage. What may affect it is the extra time or a potential police-assisted tow, which some services bill differently. Check your plan details before you're in this situation.
