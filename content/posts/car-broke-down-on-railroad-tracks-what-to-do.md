> **Quick Answer:** Get everyone out of the car immediately, leave your belongings, and move at least 100 feet from the tracks, away from the direction the train will come from. Call the emergency number on the blue sign at the crossing, then 911. Do not try to restart the engine or push the car. A car can be replaced. You cannot.

## What Do I Do First If My Car Stalls on Railroad Tracks?

Get out of the car now, along with every passenger. Do not stop to grab your phone case, purse, or anything else.

Leave the doors open if it takes no extra time. Then move away from the tracks. If a train is approaching, run at a 45-degree angle toward the train's direction of travel, not away from it. This keeps you clear of the debris that flies off when a train hits a vehicle. If no train is visible, just get at least 100 feet back and stay off the tracks and the road.

Do not try to restart the engine. If the car is in gear and the starter turns over, a manual transmission can lurch forward, but it can just as easily stall again. Do not spend time on it.

## Who Do I Call When My Car Is Stuck on the Tracks?

Call the Emergency Notification System number posted on the blue sign at the crossing, then call 911. Do both, and do them from a safe distance.

That blue sign is mounted on the crossing gate or signal mast. It lists a phone number for the railroad and a crossing ID number, which is a short alphanumeric code unique to that crossing. Give the dispatcher the crossing ID and tell them a vehicle is stalled on the tracks. They can radio trains in the area and stop them. The ID matters because it tells them exactly where you are, even if you cannot describe the street.

If you cannot find the sign, tell 911 your location using cross streets, a mile marker, or a landmark. Ask them to contact the railroad. If you are on a major corridor, check the nearest road signs for a route number to give them.

## How Much Time Do I Actually Have Before a Train Hits?

You may have very little, and you cannot count on a warning. A freight train moving at 55 mph can take a mile or more to stop, so the engineer cannot swerve or brake in time once they see you.

That is why the instruction is always to leave first and call second. Do not stand near the car waiting for a tow. Do not try to flag the train down from the tracks. Do not stay inside because you think you can fix a small problem quickly.

If the crossing gates are down and lights are flashing while you are still in the car, get out and move away from the tracks. If the gate arm comes down behind you while you are stopped between the arms, drive forward through the gate. It is designed to break away.

## What Causes a Car to Stall on Railroad Tracks?

Most stalls on tracks come from the same short list: a dead battery, a failing [alternator that stops charging while you drive](/alternator-died-while-driving-symptoms-what-happens-next/), running out of fuel, or a low-clearance vehicle getting hung up on the raised crossing surface.

That last one catches people off guard.

Humped crossings, common on rural roads and older city streets, can high-center lowered cars, trailers, and long-wheelbase vehicles. The undercarriage rests on the rails and the wheels lose traction. If your car sits low or you are pulling a trailer, look at the crossing before you commit to it and do not stop on the tracks in traffic.

Another common cause is stopping on the tracks because traffic ahead is backed up. Never enter a crossing unless you can clear the far side completely. Wait before the tracks, not on them.

## Who Pays for the Tow After the Train Passes?

The tow is on you or your coverage, but only after the crossing is safe and the railroad has cleared the scene. Do not call a private tow company to the crossing while a train could still be coming.

If the car was not hit, the railroad or local police will tell you when the area is clear. After that, a flatbed is the safest way to move a vehicle that may have been disabled by a mechanical failure. Check whether your policy includes towing, since [Progressive](/does-progressive-insurance-cover-roadside-assistance-towing/) and [GEICO](/does-geico-cover-towing-and-roadside-assistance/) handle roadside coverage differently depending on the plan you added.

## Common Questions

**Q: Should I try to push my car off the railroad tracks?**
A: Only if the train is clearly not near and you can do it quickly with help. Do not wait to see how it goes. If you cannot move it in seconds, leave the car and get clear. Never risk your life for a vehicle.

**Q: Which direction do I run if a train is coming?**
A: Run toward the train at a 45-degree angle, away from the tracks. This keeps you clear of flying debris when the train hits the car. Running in the same direction the train is traveling puts you in the path of that debris.

**Q: Can the railroad actually stop a train in time if I call?**
A: Sometimes. If dispatchers get your crossing ID quickly, they can alert crews on that line. But a train cannot stop quickly, so do not rely on the call to save your car. Your priority is getting yourself and passengers out.

**Q: Will my insurance cover my car if a train hits it?**
A: Comprehensive coverage typically covers collisions with trains, though this depends on your policy. A train strike is not usually treated as an at-fault collision. Confirm with your insurer, and see [who pays for towing after an accident](/who-pays-for-towing-after-a-car-accident-not-my-fault/).

**Q: What if my car dies at a crossing with no gates or lights?**
A: Treat it the same. Many rural crossings have only a crossbuck sign, and trains still use them. Get out, move away, find the blue sign for the crossing ID, and call the number on it, then 911.
