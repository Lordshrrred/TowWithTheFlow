> **Quick Answer:** Get your car as far right as possible, turn on your hazards, and get out on the passenger side away from traffic. Tie something bright to your antenna or door handle. Walk to the nearest call box if one is visible, or to the next exit ramp. On most Houston freeways, TxDOT's Freeway Incident Response and Clearance (FIRC) patrol trucks actively drive major corridors and will stop to help or radio dispatch for you.

## What Do I Do Right Now If I'm Stuck on a Houston Highway With No Signal?

Stop moving, get right, and make yourself visible. If you can coast to an exit, do it. If you can't, pull as far onto the right shoulder as the road allows and kill the engine. Turn on your hazard lights immediately, even in daylight. On Houston freeways like I-10 or I-45, traffic moves fast and drivers do not expect stopped cars.

Once you're stopped, do not stand behind or beside your vehicle. Exit from the passenger side and move up the embankment or behind the barrier wall if one is present. A car sitting on the shoulder of I-610 near the Galleria or on US-59 (the Eastex Freeway) near Shepherd Drive is a real target for distracted or merging traffic.

Now signal for help. Raise your hood. Tie a white cloth, jacket, or piece of clothing to the antenna or door handle. These are the universally recognized distress signals that TxDOT FIRC drivers, Harris County Sheriff deputies, and Houston Police Department patrol officers are trained to look for.

## How Do I Get Help on a Houston Freeway If I Can't Call 911?

Try 911 anyway first. Even with one bar or "SOS only" showing on your phone, most modern phones can connect an emergency call by jumping to any available carrier tower. On 5G and LTE networks in Houston's core corridors (I-10, I-45, I-69/US-59, I-610, Beltway 8), complete dead zones are rare close to the city. Move around the car, hold the phone out the window, or walk a short distance along the shoulder.

If that fails, look for a blue emergency call box. TxDOT installs these on Texas highways, and while they're more common on rural stretches, some are present on HOV lanes and managed lanes like the Katy Freeway's Express Lanes on I-10. Press the button and a live operator connects directly.

Your next option is a FIRC truck. TxDOT Houston District operates these yellow-and-green trucks on the major freeways during peak hours. They carry gas, can help with minor issues, and will radio for a tow. You do not need a phone to access this service. They will find you if your hazards are on and your hood is up.

If you are near an exit, walk to it. The nearest gas station, convenience store, or fast food restaurant will have a phone you can use or a staff member who can call for you. On most Houston freeways, exits are spaced less than a mile apart inside the Loop.

## Is It Safe to Stay in My Car or Should I Get Out?

Get out, but stay close to the barrier. Staying inside a stopped vehicle on a Houston highway is one of the most dangerous decisions you can make. Secondary collisions, where a moving vehicle strikes a stopped one, happen regularly on high-speed corridors like I-45 near the downtown split and on I-10 near the Katy Managed Lanes entrance.

The safest position is behind a concrete barrier wall if one runs along the shoulder, or up on a grassy embankment if there is one. Stay near enough to see your car and flag down help, but not in the travel lane or the immediate shoulder path. Keep your hazards running.

## What Happens After I Get a Signal or Someone Stops to Help?

Call 911 first, then your tow service. Houston police or TxDOT dispatch will send a unit to your location and can coordinate a tow. If you have roadside assistance through AAA, your insurance, or a manufacturer program, call them next. Give them your exact location: the freeway name, the nearest mile marker or exit, and which side of the road you are on.

## Common Questions

**Q: Do Houston freeways have emergency call boxes I can walk to?**
A: Some do, particularly on HOV and managed lanes on I-10 and I-45, but they are not uniformly placed. Your more reliable option is the TxDOT FIRC patrol trucks, which actively drive major Houston corridors during daylight and peak hours and will stop if they see your hazards and raised hood.

**Q: Will TxDOT help me for free on a Houston highway?**
A: The FIRC (Freeway Incident Response and Clearance) service itself is free. They can provide fuel, help with a tire, or call a tow for you at no cost. The tow, if you need one, is a separate charge billed by the tow company.

**Q: What if I break down in a Houston highway tunnel or under an overpass with no signal?**
A: Walk to the nearest open-air section of the shoulder and try your phone again. Houston's downtown tunnels and the depressed sections of I-45 near the Pierce Elevated can kill signal entirely. The moment you get to open air, signal often returns. Keep hazards on and hood up regardless.

**Q: How long will it take for someone to find me on a Houston freeway?**
A: On major corridors inside Beltway 8, FIRC trucks typically pass through within 30 to 45 minutes during active patrol hours. Houston Police and Harris County Sheriff units also patrol the freeways. Outside peak hours or on less-traveled stretches, it can take longer. Visible distress signals cut that time significantly.

**Q: What should I keep in my car for a no-signal breakdown situation?**
A: A bright cloth or safety vest, a portable battery bank with a charged phone, a whistle, and a printed list of your roadside assistance info and tow company numbers.
