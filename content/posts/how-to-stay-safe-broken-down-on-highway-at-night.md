> **Quick Answer:** Get your car as far right as possible, turn on your hazard lights immediately, and get out of the vehicle on the passenger side away from traffic. Move up the embankment or behind a guardrail if one is available. Stay there, not in your car, until help arrives. Call 911 if you feel unsafe at any point, then call your roadside assistance.

## What's the First Thing I Should Do When My Car Breaks Down on a Highway at Night?

Get off the travel lanes. That is the only goal in the first thirty seconds. Steer toward the right shoulder while you still have momentum, even if the car is dying. A flat tire, a seized engine, a blown alternator: none of those are as dangerous as sitting in a live lane on a dark highway. Get the car as far right as you can, ideally past the solid white fog line and up onto the grass or gravel if you can reach it.

Once stopped, do these in order:

1. Turn on hazard lights. Do this before you even put it in park.
2. If you have road flares or reflective triangles, set them out behind the car at 100 feet, 200 feet, and 300 feet. This is not optional at night. Flares are more visible than triangles on unlit rural highways.
3. Turn on your interior dome light. It makes the car visible and helps rescuers or police see you are inside.
4. Call 911 if you are in a dangerous spot (no shoulder, active lane, low visibility area), then call for a tow.

Do not try to fix anything under the hood while parked on a live highway at night. That is how people get killed.

## Should I Stay in My Car or Get Out When Broken Down on a Highway at Night?

Get out of the car, but stay off the road itself. This is the part most people get wrong. Sitting in a parked car on a highway shoulder means you are inside a stationary object on a road where vehicles are moving at 65 to 80 mph. If a drowsy driver drifts onto the shoulder, the car takes the impact and so do you.

Exit through the passenger side door, away from traffic. Move up the embankment, behind a guardrail, or as far from the lanes as possible. Take your phone, your ID, and your keys with you.

If there is no safe place to exit, no shoulder, no guardrail, and traffic is close: stay in the car, keep your seatbelt on, move to the passenger seat, and call 911 immediately. In that specific situation, the seatbelt buys you more protection than fleeing into an unknown dark roadway.

## How Do I Make My Broken Down Car Visible Enough at Night?

Hazard lights are your baseline, but they are not always enough, especially on curves, hills, or unlit rural stretches. Do all of this:

- **Hazard lights on** as soon as the car stops, even if you are still rolling.
- **Road flares** placed at 100, 200, and 300 feet behind the car. Standard fusee flares burn for 15 to 30 minutes and are far brighter than LED triangles in dark conditions.
- **Interior dome light on** so the vehicle looks occupied and intentional, not abandoned.
- **Emergency strobe light** clipped to your roof or trunk if you carry one. A cheap magnetic LED strobe from any auto parts store runs for hours on batteries and is visible from a quarter mile.
- **White flag or light-colored cloth** tied to the antenna or door handle signals distress without requiring you to stand near the lanes.

If you are on a curve, prioritize getting flares out immediately. Drivers on a curve have almost no time to react to a stopped vehicle.

## What Should I Keep in My Car for a Highway Breakdown at Night?

Three flares, a reflective vest, a phone charger, and a flashlight will handle most night breakdown scenarios better than any expensive kit. Here is the practical short list:

- **Road flares (3 minimum).** Not LED triangles alone. Real fusee flares for dark conditions.
- **Reflective safety vest.** If you have to move around the car at any point, wear it.
- **Phone power bank.** A 10,000 mAh bank costs under $30 and will fully charge a dead phone twice.
- **Flashlight with fresh batteries.** Your phone flashlight drains your battery. Keep a dedicated light.
- **Warm layer and water.** Night breakdowns can mean a long wait. Hypothermia risk is real in fall and winter.
- **Roadside assistance card or app.** Know who you are calling before you need them.

That is a kit that fits in a small bag and costs under $50 assembled. The flares and vest alone make you meaningfully safer.

## Common Questions

**Q: Is it safer to stay in the car or stand outside when broken down on a highway at night?**
A: Outside and away from the road is safer in most cases. Exit on the passenger side and move to the embankment or behind a guardrail. Only stay inside with your seatbelt on if there is no safe place to exit, such as a narrow shoulder with close traffic.

**Q: What do I do if my hazard lights stop working after breaking down?**
A: Set out road flares immediately behind the car at 100, 200, and 300 feet. Turn on your interior dome light. Use a phone flashlight or emergency strobe pointed toward oncoming traffic. Call 911 to report the hazard so they can get a patrol car on scene.

**Q: How long does it typically take for roadside assistance to arrive at night?**
A: Wait times vary widely. Urban areas often see 30 to 60 minutes. Rural highways at night can run 90 minutes or more. Call early, give your exact mile marker or GPS coordinates, and stay off the road while you wait.

**Q: Can I get ticketed for being broken down on a highway shoulder at night?**
A: You generally cannot be ticketed for an involuntary breakdown, but police can and will require you to move the vehicle if it becomes a hazard. Cooperate fully and let them help direct traffic or call a tow. Most state laws protect you from fines for unplanned mechanical stops.

**Q: What if I break down in a tunnel or on a bridge at night?**
A: Get out and move to the emergency pedestrian walkway immediately if one exists. Never stop in a tunnel lane if you can avoid it. Turn on hazards and call 911 right away. Bridges and tunnels remove your ability to move off the road, so emergency responders need to know your location fast.
