> **Quick Answer:** If your car is stuck in snow but undamaged and on a flat surface, roadside assistance can usually pull or push you free with a winch or traction service. If the car is in a ditch, buried past the rocker panels, or has suspension or drivetrain damage, you need a tow truck. When in doubt, call roadside assistance first. They will escalate to a tow truck if the situation is beyond a simple extraction.

## What's the Actual Difference Between a Tow Truck and Roadside Assistance for a Snow Stuck Car?

Roadside assistance sends a service vehicle to free your car without taking it anywhere. They use a winch strap, traction boards, or a light-duty pull to get you back on pavement. A tow truck physically lifts or drags your car onto a flatbed or wheel-lift and transports it to a shop or safe location.

Most roadside assistance programs, including AAA and plans bundled with auto insurance, include a basic "stuck vehicle" service. That service covers situations where your car is mired in snow on a road, parking lot, or shallow roadside area. It does not cover a car in a ditch, against a guardrail, or with bent wheels.

## When Should I Call Roadside Assistance Instead of a Tow Truck?

Call roadside assistance when the car is stuck but mechanically fine and accessible. Specifically: you spun out on a snowy road, slid off the pavement a few feet into a soft snowbank, or got high-centered in a parking lot drift. The car starts, the wheels turn, and nothing looks bent.

A standard winch-out, the most common roadside assistance call for snow situations, typically costs $50 to $100 out of pocket if you do not have coverage. With AAA or roadside coverage through your insurer, it is often free or close to it. Check your policy before you assume it is covered. Some plans cap at one or two winch-outs per year.

If you also have a dead battery because the car sat overnight in the cold, read through [car battery died in cold weather: jump start vs tow](/car-battery-died-in-cold-weather-jump-start-vs-tow/) before you call, since a jump-start may be all you need alongside the extraction.

## When Do I Actually Need a Tow Truck?

You need a tow truck when the car cannot be safely driven even after it is freed. The clearest signs: a wheel is visibly tilted (bent control arm or tie rod), the car slid into a curb or barrier and now pulls hard to one side, the transmission will not engage, or the car is buried deep enough that a simple winch pull risks further damage.

Deep ditch recoveries also require a tow truck, not a roadside service vehicle. A car with the frame resting on a snow-packed ditch wall, or sitting at a steep angle, needs proper rigging. A roadside service tech with a strap is not equipped for that. For more on ditch situations specifically, see [car stuck in snow ditch: who to call right now](/car-stuck-in-snow-ditch-who-to-call/).

If there was a collision involved, even a low-speed slide into a curb, towing costs may fall on the at-fault party or your collision coverage. [Who pays towing costs after a not at fault accident](/who-pays-towing-costs-after-not-at-fault-accident/) covers exactly how that works.

A winch-out or basic snow extraction through a roadside service runs $50 to $150 without coverage. A full tow truck call-out starts around $75 to $125 for the hook-up fee alone, then adds $3 to $7 per mile depending on the region. A local

## What Do I Do Right Now While I Wait for Help?

Stay in the car if you are not in a traffic lane and the exhaust pipe is clear of snow. A blocked exhaust pipe with the engine running causes carbon monoxide buildup inside the cabin fast. Get out and check the tailpipe if you are sitting in a drift.

Turn on your hazard lights immediately. If you have road flares or reflective triangles in the trunk, deploy them behind the car, especially on any road with moving traffic. Crack a window slightly for fresh air if you keep the engine running for heat.

Do not spin the tires repeatedly trying to free the car yourself. Packing snow tighter under the tires makes extraction harder and can overheat an automatic transmission in minutes. If you have a floor mat, slide it in front of a drive wheel for traction. Slow, gentle throttle application is the only DIY approach worth attempting once.

If you slid on ice before getting stuck, check whether the car drove oddly before it stopped. Pulling to one side, vibration through the steering wheel, or a grinding sound from a wheel are all reasons to request a tow rather than a simple pull-out.

## Common Questions

**Q: Will my car insurance cover a snow extraction or winch-out?**
A: It depends on whether you have roadside assistance added to your policy. Comprehensive or collision coverage does not cover extractions. Roadside assistance is a separate add-on, and most policies that include it do cover winch-outs up to a set dollar amount, often $50 to $100 per incident.

**Q: How long does a roadside assistance snow extraction usually take?**
A: Expect 30 to 90 minutes in normal winter conditions. During a major snowstorm, response times can stretch to 2 to 4 hours because demand spikes fast. Calling as early as possible gives you better queue position.

**Q: Can I dig my car out myself and avoid the call entirely?**
A: Sometimes, yes. If the car is only lightly stuck and you have a shovel, clearing snow from in front of and behind all four drive wheels, then using a floor mat for traction, can free the car without any outside help. If the undercarriage is resting on packed snow, stop and call for help.

**Q: Does AAA cover towing if my car gets damaged in the snow and can't be driven?**
A: Yes. AAA's roadside assistance includes towing to a shop when the vehicle is not driveable, up to the mileage limit of your membership tier. Classic members get 3 to 5 miles free, while Plus and Premier tiers cover significantly more. Miles beyond your limit are billed at the standard local rate.

**Q: If I call roadside assistance and they say I actually need a tow, do I pay twice?**
A: Usually no. Most providers will dispatch a tow truck at that point and apply the service call toward the tow, or waive the initial fee. Confirm this before agreeing to any additional charges. Get the dispatcher's name and write down what they promised.
