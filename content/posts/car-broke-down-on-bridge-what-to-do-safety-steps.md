> **Quick Answer:** Turn on your hazard lights, steer to the right shoulder if the car will still roll, and stop as far from traffic as possible. On a bridge with no shoulder, stay buckled in with your hazards on and call 911 immediately. Do not walk the bridge deck. Call for a tow only after you've reported your location and exact position on the span.

## What Do I Do the Second My Car Dies on a Bridge?

Turn on your hazards, keep your foot steady on the brake, and steer toward the right edge while you still have momentum. Don't wait to figure out why it died.

When the engine quits, you lose power assist to the steering and brakes. The wheel gets heavy and the pedal gets stiff, but both still work. Press harder than normal and keep steering. You've got a few hundred feet of coasting in most cars, so use it to reach a shoulder, a pull-off, or the end of the bridge.

If the car is rolling in a travel lane, shift to neutral and try one restart. If it fires, keep driving until you're off the bridge. If it doesn't, stop trying. Cranking a dead engine drains the battery you need for hazard lights.

## Should I Get Out of the Car or Stay Inside?

Stay inside with your seatbelt on unless the car is on fire, smoking heavily, or about to be hit. A stopped car on a bridge deck is dangerous, but a person on foot next to moving traffic is worse.

Bridges have narrow or nonexistent shoulders, concrete barriers on both sides, and no escape room. If you exit, you're standing in the one place a driver can't swerve around you. Most bridge crashes involving stopped vehicles happen because the oncoming driver doesn't see the car until it's too late, especially at night, in fog, or on a curve.

Here's the decision:

- **You made it to a real shoulder:** Stay in the car, belted, hazards on. Turn your wheels away from traffic.
- **You're stuck in a travel lane:** Stay belted, hazards on, call 911 now. Don't open your door into traffic.
- **Fire, heavy smoke, or fuel smell:** Get out on the passenger side, move behind the barrier if one exists, and get as far from the car as the bridge allows.
- **Flooded or icy deck:** Stay put. Walking on ice or wet steel grating near the edge is a bad trade.

Never climb over a bridge railing or walk along the outside. Bridges with pedestrian walkways are the only exception, and only if you can reach one safely.

## Who Do I Call First When I'm Stuck on a Bridge?

Call 911 first if you're in a travel lane or can't get off the roadway. Call a tow company second.

Tell the dispatcher three things: the name of the bridge or the highway and direction, the nearest mile marker or exit you passed, and that you're stopped on the span. In a major metro, a bridge can be a mile long, so "I'm on the bridge" isn't enough. If you can't read a sign, drop a pin in your phone's map app and read the coordinates aloud.

Many states run highway assistance patrols that clear stalled cars off bridges and busy corridors at no charge. You can usually reach them through 511, your state DOT's traffic app, or by asking the 911 dispatcher. Don't assume one exists on your route, ask.

## How Do I Make My Car Visible and Stay Safe While Waiting?

Make the car as visible as possible from the inside, since you shouldn't be standing outside it. Hazards on, dome light on at night, and brake lights off by keeping your foot off the pedal once you're stopped in park.

Don't set up flares or triangles on a bridge. There's no safe place to walk back 100 feet, and the person placing them is the one who gets hit. Save those for roads with real shoulders.

If you're on the passenger side of a stopped car and traffic is close, keep your head and body away from the driver's side of the vehicle. A rear-end hit pushes the car forward and sideways.

Call someone you trust and stay on the line. It helps you stay calm and gives a second person your location. If the wait stretches long, [how long can you wait for a tow truck on the highway](/how-long-can-you-wait-for-a-tow-truck-on-highway/) covers what's reasonable and when to escalate.

## What Will the Tow Cost and Can the Truck Even Reach Me?

Expect a normal local tow rate plus possible extras for a difficult recovery. Bridge pickups can cost more if the driver needs traffic control or a longer wait.

The truck may not be able to reach you directly. Many bridges are one-way, divided by a median, or closed to stopping, so the driver may need police help to block a lane first. That's why 911 comes before the tow call. The driver will likely load the car onto a flatbed if the problem is a dead drivetrain, steering failure, or a car that won't roll freely. ## Common Questions

**Q: Can I walk off a bridge to get help?**
A: Not unless a marked pedestrian walkway runs along the span and you can reach it safely. Walking the traffic deck is far more dangerous than waiting in a belted car with hazards on.

**Q: Is it illegal to stop on a bridge?**
A: In most states, stopping on a bridge is prohibited except in an emergency, and a breakdown counts. Hazards on, and getting out of the travel lane when you can, is what officers expect.

**Q: What if there's no cell service on the bridge?**
A: Try 911 anyway, since emergency calls can connect through any available network even without your own carrier. If nothing connects, stay belted in with hazards on, since bridge traffic usually means someone will notice you quickly.

**Q: Should I leave my hazards on if my battery is dying?**
A: Yes, hazards are your best protection, and they use less power than most people expect. Turn off the radio, AC, and phone charging off the car's battery to stretch the hazard time.

**Q: Will my roadside assistance cover a bridge breakdown?**
A: Usually yes, standard towing and roadside plans cover any public roadway, but check whether your plan's mileage limit applies. [How far will insurance tow my car after a breakdown](/how-far-will-insurance-tow-my-car-after-a-breakdown/) covers typical limits.
