> **Quick Answer:** Turn your wheels straight, clear mud from around the tires, and try rocking the car by shifting between Drive and Reverse in short bursts. If traction mats, floor mats, or a tow strap with another vehicle don't free you within a few attempts, stop spinning. Continued wheel spin digs you in deeper. At that point, call a tow or recovery service.

## What Should I Do First If My Car Is Stuck in Mud?

Stop spinning your wheels immediately. Every second of wheel spin removes more ground from under the tires and makes extraction harder and more expensive.

Once you stop, do this in order:

1. Shift into Park and turn the wheels as straight as possible.
2. Get out and look at all four tires. How deep are you? Is the frame or undercarriage resting on the ground? If the frame is sitting in mud, a tow is the call, not a DIY fix.
3. Clear mud from in front of and behind the drive tires using a stick, your hands, or a small shovel if you have one.
4. Place traction material under the drive tires: commercial traction mats, your floor mats, a piece of cardboard, or even tree branches and rocks.
5. Rock the vehicle slowly. In an automatic, shift gently between Drive and Reverse, letting the car move a few inches each direction before switching. In a manual, use the clutch lightly and keep RPMs low.

If you have not moved in three to four attempts, stop. You are past the self-recovery window.

## Will Flooring the Gas Help Get My Car Out of Mud?

No. High RPMs in mud are the single biggest mistake drivers make. Spinning the tires at speed turns soft ground into a pit under your wheels and can burn out your differential or overheat your transmission, especially in an automatic that is slipping repeatedly between Drive and Reverse. Keep throttle input light and deliberate.

Four-wheel-drive helps, but it does not override physics. Even a 4WD truck can dig itself down to the axles in deep clay mud.

## Can Another Car Pull Me Out, and Is It Safe?

Yes, another vehicle can pull you out, but the setup matters. You need a proper recovery strap, specifically a kinetic or snatch strap, not a tow strap, and not a chain. A kinetic strap stretches and uses stored energy to break the vehicle free without a sudden hard jolt that can snap a bumper or snap the strap itself into someone. Attach the strap only to a frame-mounted tow hook or a recovery point, never to a plastic bumper cover or a trailer ball.

Have the pulling vehicle position itself on firm ground. Both drivers agree on a signal. The pull vehicle accelerates gently while you apply light throttle. Keep bystanders well away from the strap line in case it breaks.

If the pulling vehicle starts spinning or sliding toward you, abort immediately.

## When Should I Stop Trying and Just Call a Tow?

Call a tow if any of the following are true:

- The vehicle frame or undercarriage is resting on the mud.
- You are sinking further with each attempt.
- The tires are more than halfway submerged.
- You have no traction material and no one to pull you out.
- You are in a remote area with limited phone signal and conditions are worsening (rising water, incoming rain).

A standard tow truck may not be the right call for a mud recovery. Many towing companies carry a winch, but deep mud and off-road situations sometimes require a dedicated recovery service with a heavy-duty winch and ground anchors. When you call, describe exactly where you are, how deep the vehicle is, and whether

## What Will a Mud Recovery Tow Actually Cost?

A basic winch-out on accessible terrain from a paved road or parking lot typically runs $75 to $150. Off-road recovery in deep mud, away from pavement, can run $200 to $500 or more depending on the equipment needed, distance, and time on site. If the tow truck has to travel a long distance to reach you on a rural property or remote trail, expect a mileage charge on top of the base rate.

Some situations require two trucks: one to winch, one to pull. That doubles the cost. If you are off-road in clay-heavy terrain after rain, that is exactly the kind of job that brings out the heavy equipment.

Getting a quick quote over the phone is reasonable, but the driver will often adjust the price once they see the actual condition on site.

## Common Questions

**Q: Can I use my floor mats to get unstuck from mud?**
A: Yes, floor mats work in shallow mud situations. Shove them under the drive tires, textured side down, and apply very gentle throttle. They will likely get ruined, but they can give just enough grip to break free. Do not use this trick if the vehicle is deeply buried.

**Q: Does AAA cover mud recovery and winch-outs?**
A: Standard AAA memberships include winch-out service, but only within a certain distance from a paved road, typically 100 feet or less. If you are further off-road or on private land, they may decline or charge extra. Call AAA dispatch before assuming you are covered.

**Q: What if I am stuck in mud and water is rising around the car?**
A: Get out of the vehicle immediately and move to higher ground. A car stuck in mud is replaceable. If water is entering the cabin or the current is moving, do not stay inside waiting for help. Call 911 once you are safe.

**Q: Is it bad for my transmission to rock a car back and forth in mud?**
A: Yes, repeated rapid shifts between Drive and Reverse while the wheels are spinning can overheat an automatic transmission. Make each shift slowly, let the car stop its motion before changing direction, and limit total attempts to three or four before calling for help.

**Q: Will my insurance cover a tow if I got stuck off-road on purpose?**
A: Comprehensive or roadside assistance add-ons sometimes exclude off-road recoveries or limit winch-out coverage. If you drove off-road intentionally and got stuck, some policies treat that differently than a road breakdown. Call your insurer before assuming coverage.
