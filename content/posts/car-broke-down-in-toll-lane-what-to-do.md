> **Quick Answer:** Turn on your hazards and coast as far toward the shoulder or barrier as you can. If you can't reach safety, stay buckled in with hazards on and call 911, since a stopped car in a travel lane is an emergency. If you can reach a safe spot, get out away from traffic and call roadside assistance or the toll authority's posted number.

## What Should I Do the Second My Car Dies in a Toll Lane?

Turn on your hazard lights immediately and steer toward the nearest shoulder, median, or barrier while you still have momentum. Don't brake hard if you don't have to. Coasting gives you more distance.

On most vehicles, a dead engine still leaves you with steering and brakes, just heavier. Power steering and power brakes lose assist when the engine quits, so expect to push harder on both. Keep a firm grip and steer smoothly.

If you're in an open-road tolling lane with no booth, cars behind you are traveling at highway speed and may not expect a stopped vehicle. Get your hazards on before anything else.

## Do I Stay in the Car or Get Out?

Stay in the car with your seatbelt on if you cannot reach a shoulder or a protected area. Getting out in a live lane is more dangerous than staying belted in, because the most common serious injury in these situations is a rear-end impact from a driver who didn't see you.

If you made it to a wide shoulder, a median pullout, or a spot behind a barrier, the answer flips. Get out on the side away from traffic and move well clear of the car. Stand behind a guardrail if there is one. Leave the hazards running.

At a staffed toll plaza, you're usually near booths, cameras, and attendants, which is a real advantage. Stay put, keep your hazards on, and flag an attendant if you can do so safely.

## Who Do I Call First?

Call 911 first if your car is blocking a travel lane or you feel unsafe. Say you're stopped in a live toll lane and give your direction of travel, the nearest exit or plaza, and any mile marker you can see.

If you reach a safe spot off the lane, your next call is roadside assistance. Check your insurance card, your auto app, or your credit card benefits. If you're not sure what you have, see [does roadside assistance cover a flat tire or just towing](/does-roadside-assistance-cover-flat-tire-or-just-towing/) for what these plans usually include.

Many toll roads also post a help number on signs, on your toll transponder paperwork, or on the agency's website. Use that posted number rather than searching for one under stress. Plenty of toll authorities run their own patrol or response crews that clear stalled cars quickly, sometimes at no charge for the first assist. For the broader picture, see [car broke down on a toll road, who do I call](/car-broke-down-on-toll-road-who-do-i-call/).

## Will They Move My Car Right Away?

Expect fast action on a blocked lane, but not always a free tow. A stopped vehicle in an active toll lane is a hazard, so patrol units or the toll agency's contractor will often push or tow you to the nearest safe area quickly.

## Can I Get a Toll or Fine for Stopping There?

You won't typically be fined for an emergency breakdown, but you may still get billed for the tolls you incurred. If your transponder or plate was read before the breakdown, that charge stands. If you were towed off the toll road, you may also need to settle any unpaid toll through the agency's account system or pay-by-plate process.

Keep your phone and document everything. Photograph your car's position, any signage, and the tow operator's truck and paperwork. If you're later charged something that seems wrong, that record helps. See [how to dispute a towing charge if you were overcharged](/how-to-dispute-a-towing-charge-if-overcharged/) if the bill doesn't match what you were told.

If your toll account is linked to a card on file, check it a day or two later. A stopped car near a gantry can occasionally trigger a double read.

## What Should I Do While I Wait?

Stay visible and stay still. Keep hazards on. If it's dark, turn on your dome light so patrol and tow crews can spot you. Keep your seatbelt on if you're still in the lane.

Don't try to push the car yourself or change a tire in a live lane. A flat in the travel lane is a call-for-help situation, not a fix-it-yourself one.

Know what you're asking for. A dead battery needs a jump or a tow. A fuel problem needs fuel delivery. A mechanical failure usually needs a flatbed. If you're not sure, say so and let the operator assess.

## Common Questions

**Q: Is it illegal to stop in a toll lane?**
A: Stopping because of a real breakdown is not a violation, but you must get help and clear the lane as quickly as possible. Stopping for convenience or to avoid payment can draw a citation.

**Q: Should I stay in my car or get out in a toll lane?**
A: Stay belted in if you're in a live lane with traffic behind you. Get out only if you can reach a safe spot like a wide shoulder or protected area, and exit on the side away from traffic.

**Q: Does AAA come out to toll roads?**
A: Generally yes, but some toll roads require their own patrol or an approved contractor to handle the initial clearing. Call your provider and tell them you're on a toll road so they can route the right unit.

**Q: Who pays for a tow off a toll road?**
A: It depends on the agency and your coverage. Some toll authorities offer a free first assist or a short push to safety. A longer tow usually falls to you or your roadside plan, so confirm the cost before you agree.

**Q: What if I can't get a signal to call for help?**
A: Keep hazards on and stay belted in, then try again, since a short move may improve reception. If you have an in-car emergency button or an onboard assistance service, press it, because it uses a different connection.
