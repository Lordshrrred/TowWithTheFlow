> **Quick Answer:** With a failing alternator, most drivers get 30 minutes to 2 hours of drive time before the battery drains completely and the car dies. That window shrinks fast if you're running headlights, AC, or the radio. Turn off every non-essential electrical load, drive straight to a shop or safe location, and do not ignore the battery warning light.

## How Long Can I Actually Drive Before the Car Dies?

Thirty minutes to two hours is the real window, and it depends almost entirely on how much you're pulling from the battery. The alternator normally keeps the battery charged while the engine runs. When it fails, you're running on whatever charge is left in the battery, nothing more.

A fully charged 12-volt battery on a modern car holds roughly 40 to 80 amp-hours of capacity. Your headlights alone can draw 10 to 15 amps. Add the blower motor, heated seats, and stereo and you can be pulling 30 amps or more. Do that math and a full battery can be dead in under an hour.

Daytime, windows down, radio off: you might squeeze closer to two hours. Night driving with headlights on: assume 30 to 45 minutes. Do not gamble on the longer estimate if you can avoid it.

## What Are the Warning Signs the Alternator Is Actually Failing?

The battery warning light is the clearest signal. It looks like a small battery icon on the dash, and when the alternator stops producing voltage, that light comes on because the system voltage drops below roughly 13.5 to 14.5 volts. Do not mistake this for a simple battery problem.

Other signs you'll notice in sequence as the battery drains:

- Dim or flickering headlights
- Dashboard lights dimming or going out
- Radio cutting out or sounding distorted
- Power windows moving slowly
- The engine starting to run rough or stumble

By the time the windows are sluggish, you have minutes, not miles. Pull over before the engine stalls in traffic.

If you've already seen the [battery warning light on while driving](/battery-warning-light-on-while-driving-what-does-it-mean/), that article breaks down exactly what the light tells you and what it doesn't.

## Should I Keep Driving or Stop Right Now?

Stop if you're in traffic, on a highway at night, or more than 15 miles from a shop. Keep driving only if you are close to your destination, it's daylight, and you can cut all non-essential electrical loads.

Here's the quick decision:

- **Distance under 10 miles, daylight, light traffic:** Turn off the AC, radio, rear defroster, and any seat heaters. Drive directly there. Do not stop and restart the engine. Restarting costs a significant burst of amperage and shortens your window.
- **Distance over 15 miles or night driving:** Do not push it. Call for a tow. A stalled car in the left lane of a highway is far more dangerous than the cost of a tow truck.
- **Already stalled:** Your battery is dead. A jump start will get the engine running again briefly, but without a working alternator it will die again quickly, sometimes within a mile. Know that going in.

If the alternator has already quit completely while you were driving, [alternator dies while driving: signs and what to do](/alternator-dies-while-driving-signs-and-what-to-do/) covers the exact moment-by-moment steps.

## Will a Jump Start Get Me to the Shop?

Maybe, for a very short distance. A jump start restores charge to the battery but does nothing to fix the alternator. The moment you disconnect the jumper cables and the engine is running on its own, the battery starts draining again because the alternator isn't replenishing it.

Some cars die again within a quarter mile. Others run for 20 minutes. There is no reliable way to predict which. If the shop is more than a few miles away, call a tow truck instead of trying to limp there on a jump. Stalling mid-route and needing a second tow costs more than just getting it towed once.

A jump start does makes sense if you're in a dangerous location and need to move the car to the shoulder or a nearby parking lot before calling for help.

## Common Questions

**Q: Can a bad alternator destroy my battery?**
A: Yes. A failing alternator that's putting out too little voltage will slowly drain the battery dead. One that's putting out too much voltage (above 15 volts, which happens with a bad voltage regulator) can overcharge and permanently damage the battery. Either way, you often end up replacing both.

**Q: Can I drive with a bad alternator if my battery is brand new?**
A: A new battery gives you a full charge to draw from, so your window is as long as it's going to get, probably closer to 90 minutes to two hours. But the math is the same: you're still draining a finite resource with no way to recharge it. Get to a shop, don't assume a new battery buys you unlimited time.

**Q: How much does it cost to replace an alternator?**
A: Most alternator replacements run $400 to $800 at an independent shop, parts and labor included. Dealer pricing is higher. The alternator itself typically costs $150 to $350 depending on the vehicle, and labor is usually one to two hours of shop time.

**Q: What causes an alternator to fail?**
A: The most common cause is a worn-out set of brushes or a failed diode pack inside the alternator itself. Bearing failure is also common, and you'll often hear a grinding or whining noise from the front of the engine before the alternator stops charging. A snapped serpentine belt will also kill the alternator instantly since that belt is what spins it.

**Q: My alternator light came on but went off again. Am I okay?**
A: No, not necessarily. An intermittent warning light usually means the alternator is failing but hasn't fully given out yet. The voltage may be fluctuating. Get it tested at an auto parts store (most do this free) or a shop before your next long drive. An alternator that's borderline is not one to ignore.
