> **Quick Answer:** Pump the brake pedal hard and fast several times to rebuild pressure. If that fails, downshift one gear at a time, ease on the parking brake, and steer toward an exit ramp, runaway lane, or grass. Turn on your hazards. Once stopped, do not drive the car again. Call a tow. A pedal that hits the floor means the brake system has lost hydraulic pressure.

## What Do I Do Right Now If My Brake Pedal Goes to the Floor?

Pump the pedal quickly and firmly, three to five times, while you look for a safe place to slow down. A pedal that sinks usually means fluid or pressure is leaking, and pumping can build enough pressure in the lines to give you some braking.

Work through this sequence:

1. **Hands tight, eyes up.** Do not panic-swerve. Pick your path.
2. **Hazards on.** Warn traffic behind you.
3. **Pump the pedal hard.** Fast, firm strokes. Sometimes it comes back.
4. **Downshift.** In an automatic, step down through the gears (L, 2, 1, or use the manual/paddle mode). Do it gradually so the rear wheels don't lock. In a manual, drop one gear at a time.
5. **Parking brake, gently.** Pull or press it slowly and hold steady pressure. Yanking it can lock the rear wheels and spin the car. If it's a button-style electronic brake, hold the button only if you're at low speed.
6. **Steer to safety.** Use an exit ramp, an uphill grade, a runaway truck ramp, or a wide shoulder. Scrape a guardrail or ride onto grass if you have to. Slowing with friction beats hitting traffic.

Do not turn the engine off while moving. You'd lose power steering and, on many cars, the brake booster.

## Why Did My Brake Pedal Suddenly Go to the Floor?

The pedal drops because the hydraulic system can no longer hold pressure, and the usual causes are a fluid leak or a failed master cylinder. Your brakes work by pushing fluid through steel lines and rubber hoses to the calipers or wheel cylinders at each wheel. Lose fluid or pressure and the pedal has nothing to push against.

The common culprits:

- **Burst brake hose or rusted line.** Old rubber hoses balloon and split. In salt-belt states, steel lines corrode through.
- **Failed master cylinder.** The internal seals bypass, so the pedal slowly sinks under steady pressure, or drops all at once.
- **Leaking caliper or wheel cylinder.** Check for fluid on the inside of a wheel.
- **Air in the lines.** Usually after recent brake work done poorly, giving a spongy pedal that can bottom out.
- **Overheated fluid.** Long mountain descents can boil old brake fluid, creating vapor bubbles. The pedal goes soft or sinks. This is called brake fade, and it often recovers once the fluid cools.

If you recently had brakes serviced, tell the shop exactly what happened. That context matters.

## Is It Safe to Drive After the Pedal Comes Back?

No. If the pedal returned after pumping or cooling, the underlying fault is still there and it can fail again at the worst moment. A pedal that recovers is a warning, not a fix.

Pull over somewhere flat and safe, shift to park, set the parking brake, and look for these signs:

- Brake warning light on the dash
- Wet, oily fluid under the car or on the inside of a wheel (brake fluid is clear to amber and slick)
- A pedal that sinks slowly when you hold steady pressure at a stop

## Do I Need a Tow After My Brakes Fail?

Yes, tow it. A car with a pedal that hit the floor has a compromised hydraulic system, and driving it even a short distance to a shop risks a collision. A tow costs far less than a crash.

Ask for a flatbed if you can. It's the cleanest way to move a car with a hydraulic fault, since there's no concern about brake drag or a loose wheel. If you're stuck waiting, get out of the car if you're on a busy shoulder and stand well away from traffic. Our guide on [whether it's safe to wait on the highway shoulder for a tow truck](/car-died-on-highway-shoulder-safe-to-wait-for-tow-truck/) covers where to stand and what to do.

Expect repair costs to vary widely. A replaced brake hose or a bled system is a modest job. A master cylinder or multiple calipers costs more. A shop should pressure-test the system and find the leak before quoting you.

## What Should I Tell the Mechanic?

Give the shop the specifics: when the pedal dropped, what speed you were at, whether it recovered after pumping, and any warning lights. Mention any recent brake work, because a loose fitting or missed bleed from a prior job is a real possibility.

Ask them to inspect the hoses, lines, master cylinder, calipers, and fluid condition, and to bleed the full system once the fault is fixed. Old fluid absorbs moisture and boils at a lower temperature, so a full flush is often smart if your fluid is dark.

## Common Questions

**Q: Should I put the car in neutral if my brakes fail?**
A: No. Stay in gear and downshift. Engine braking helps slow you, and neutral removes that help. Only shift to neutral if the engine itself is the emergency.

**Q: Can I use the parking brake to stop a car with no brakes?**
A: Yes, but apply it gradually and keep steering. It acts on the rear wheels only, so grabbing it hard at speed can lock them and cause a spin. Squeeze it on slowly and hold steady pressure.

**Q: Why does my brake pedal go to the floor but then come back up?**
A: Pumping rebuilds pressure in a leaking system, or cooled fluid stops boiling. Either way the fault is still there. Don't drive it, because it can drop again without warning.

**Q: Will the brake warning light come on when this happens?**
A: Often, but not always. A low-fluid or pressure-imbalance sensor usually triggers it, though a sudden hose burst can drop the pedal before the light registers. A sinking pedal alone is reason enough to stop.

**Q: Is it okay to just add brake fluid and keep driving?**
A: No. Low fluid means a leak, and refilling doesn't fix the leak. The pedal can still fail, and the fluid will run out again. Tow the car and have the system inspected.
