Your run flat tire warning light just came on. You want to know if you can keep driving or if you need to pull over right now. The answer depends on one thing: how that warning light came on and what your tire actually looks like.

> **Quick Answer:** Most run flat tires give you 50 miles at up to 50 mph after pressure loss, but that range drops fast if you drive faster, carry heavy loads, or the tire has sidewall damage. Check your tire visually right now. If the sidewall is bulging, cracked, or the rim is riding low, stop and call a tow. If the tire looks intact and your TPMS reading is above zero, you can usually drive to the nearest tire shop, slowly.

## How Far Can I Actually Drive on a Run Flat After the Light Comes On?

Fifty miles at 50 mph is the standard manufacturer guideline for most run flat tires, including common fitments like Bridgestone DriveGuard and Michelin ZP (Zero Pressure) tires. That number assumes the tire is otherwise undamaged and you're on flat pavement. Exceed 50 mph and that range shrinks. Carry four passengers and luggage and it shrinks further. The run flat's reinforced sidewall holds the car up after pressure loss, but that sidewall generates heat when flexed under load, and heat is what destroys it.

If your TPMS shows a pressure reading above zero (even 10 or 15 PSI), you have more margin. If it reads zero, treat the 50-mile limit as a hard cap, not a suggestion.

## What Do I Look for Before Deciding Whether to Drive or Stop?

Get out and look at the tire before you make any decision. A damaged sidewall kills the 50-mile window entirely.

Look for these red flags:

- Sidewall bulging outward or visibly deformed
- Chunks of tread missing or deep cuts in the rubber
- The rim sitting noticeably lower than the other three wheels
- Any scraping or grinding sound when you move slowly

If you see any of those, stop driving. The run flat's reinforced sidewall can handle a slow pressure loss, but it cannot handle structural damage. Driving on a blown-out or physically damaged run flat will destroy the tire completely and can bend or crack the wheel, turning a $250 tire replacement into a $600-plus rim repair on top of it.

If the tire looks visually intact, no bulge, no missing chunks, and the car is tracking straight without pulling hard to one side, you can drive it to a shop. Keep it under 50 mph. No highway speeds.

## Can the Run Flat Tire Be Repaired or Does It Have to Be Replaced?

In most cases, the tire has to be replaced. Run flat tires cannot be safely patched or plugged after they have been driven on with zero or near-zero pressure. The internal reinforcement layer gets stressed in ways that do not show up visually, so a shop cannot confirm it is structurally sound even if it looks fine from the outside.

Bridgestone, Michelin, and Continental all publish guidance saying run flat tires should not be repaired after being driven in a deflated state. A few shops will disagree and plug one anyway. That is their call and your risk. Most tire manufacturers void any warranty the moment a run flat has been operated flat.

## What If I Have No Spare and the Run Flat Is Destroyed?

Many vehicles equipped with run flats from the factory ship with no spare at all, which is exactly why run flats exist. If the tire is beyond driving on, your options are a tow to a shop that stocks your size, or a tire shop that offers mobile service.

Not all shops carry run flat replacements in stock, especially for European vehicles like BMW, Mini, or Mercedes that use low-profile run flat sizes. Call ahead before you tow to confirm they have your size. ## Common Questions

**Q: Does the run flat warning light mean the tire is completely flat?**
A: Not necessarily. The TPMS light triggers when pressure drops 25% or more below the recommended level. Your tire may be at 15 PSI instead of zero, which still gives you driving range. Check your TPMS display if your car has one to see the actual pressure reading.

**Q: Can I drive a run flat on the highway after the light comes on?**
A: No. Keep it under 50 mph. Highway speeds generate too much heat in the flexed sidewall and will destroy the tire before you reach the 50-mile limit. Get off at the next exit and take surface streets to a tire shop.

**Q: How do I know if my car has run flat tires?**
A: Check the sidewall for the letters RFT, ROF, ZP, or SSR depending on the brand. Bridgestone uses RFT, Michelin uses ZP, Continental uses SSR, and Goodyear uses ROF. If none of those appear and your car has no spare tire at all, it almost certainly came with run flats from the factory.

**Q: Will my insurance or roadside assistance cover a tow for a run flat tire?**
A: Most roadside assistance plans, including AAA and coverage through carriers like State Farm and GEICO, cover towing regardless of the cause. The tire itself is not covered by roadside assistance. Check your policy before you call so you know what to expect. [What GEICO roadside assistance actually pays for towing](/geico-roadside-assistance-towing-how-much-will-they-pay/) breaks that down specifically.

**Q: Can I put a regular spare on a car that came with run flats?**
A: A compact spare (donut) will physically fit most wheels, but check your owner's manual first. Some AWD and all-wheel-drive systems restrict or prohibit use of a compact spare because the size difference affects the drivetrain. If your car is AWD, call the dealer or a shop before mounting a donut.
