> **Quick Answer:** Pull over immediately. Smoke from under the hood means something is overheating or burning, and driving even another mile can destroy your engine. Turn off the car, get everyone out and away from traffic, and call for a tow. Do not open the hood until the smoke stops. This is not a wait-and-see situation.

## What Do I Do Right Now If My Car Is Smoking on the Highway?

Get off the road. Signal, move to the rightmost lane, and get onto the shoulder as fast as you safely can. Turn the engine off the second you stop. Smoke means heat is building somewhere it should not be, and continued driving risks a seized engine, a coolant system blowout, or in the worst case, fire.

Once stopped:

1. Turn off the engine immediately.
2. Turn on your hazard lights.
3. Get all passengers out of the car and move well behind the guardrail or up the embankment, away from traffic.
4. Do not open the hood. A pressurized cooling system can spray scalding coolant in your face. Wait until the smoke completely stops, which typically takes 10 to 20 minutes.
5. Call a tow truck or roadside assistance. Do not try to drive to the next exit.

For more on staying safe while you wait, read [how to stay safe waiting for a tow truck on the highway](/how-to-stay-safe-waiting-for-tow-truck-on-highway/).

## What Is Actually Causing the Smoke Under My Hood?

White or gray smoke usually means coolant is burning off, most often from a leaking radiator hose, a blown head gasket, or a cracked radiator. Blue-tinged smoke means the engine is burning oil, typically from a leak dripping onto a hot exhaust manifold. A sharp, acrid smell with dark smoke points more toward an electrical short or burning rubber from a serpentine belt contacting a hot surface.

The most common culprit on a highway breakdown is a coolant system failure. Your temperature gauge probably spiked before the smoke became visible. If the gauge was already in the red, assume the engine has been running dangerously hot and treat it accordingly: do not restart the car.

A burning rubber smell with smoke can also mean your [serpentine belt snapped](/car-serpentine-belt-snapped-can-i-drive/), and the friction is causing what looks like smoke before the belt fully clears the pulleys.

## Is It Safe to Open the Hood and Add Coolant?

Only after the smoke has stopped completely and you have waited at least 15 to 20 minutes. Even then, place a thick cloth over the radiator cap and turn it only to the first stop, letting pressure escape slowly before removing it fully. If you open a hot, pressurized cooling system too fast, you will get burned.

If you do not have coolant with you, plain water works as an emergency fill to limp a short distance, but only after the system has fully cooled. Adding cold water to a hot engine can crack the block. If you are not certain the engine has cooled completely, do not add anything. Wait for the tow.

## What If the Smoke Turns Into Flames?

Get out immediately. Do not grab bags, phones, or anything else. Get everyone out, move at least 100 feet away from the vehicle, and call 911. A car fire can go from a small flame under the hood to fully engulfed in under two minutes once it reaches the fuel system or interior materials.

Do not use water on a car fire. If you have a dry-chemical fire extinguisher rated for Class B fires in your vehicle, you can use it on a small, contained engine fire from a safe angle, but only if the flames are small and you have a clear escape path behind you. If there is any doubt, stay back and let the fire department handle it.

## Common Questions

**Q: Can I keep driving if my car is only smoking a little?**
A: No. Even light smoke from the hood means something is burning or overheating. Pull over and diagnose it before continuing. A small coolant leak can empty your system within minutes at highway speed.

**Q: My temperature gauge looks normal but there's smoke. What does that mean?**
A: It likely means something is burning externally, such as oil or a belt dripping onto the exhaust, rather than the engine overheating internally. The temp gauge only measures coolant temperature, not the exhaust manifold surface, which can reach over 800 degrees Fahrenheit.

**Q: How long should I wait before opening the hood after the smoke stops?**
A: At least 15 to 20 minutes after the smoke fully clears. The radiator cap and upper hose area can still be under pressure even after the smoke stops.

**Q: Will my roadside assistance pay for this tow?**
A: Most roadside assistance plans cover a breakdown tow. AAA, State Farm, GEICO, and Progressive all offer towing coverage, but reimbursement limits and free tow distances vary by plan. Call your provider before you call an independent tow company.

**Q: Is white smoke from the hood always coolant?**
A: Usually, but not always. White steam on a cold morning that clears quickly is often just condensation burning off the exhaust. Sustained white or gray smoke from under the hood while driving is almost always coolant or, less commonly, a burning plastic component near the engine bay.
