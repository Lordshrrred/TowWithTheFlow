> **Quick Answer:** Call 911 first if your car is blocking a lane or you feel unsafe. Otherwise, call the toll authority's roadside help number, printed on signs and your toll tag paperwork, since many toll roads run free patrols. Then call your own roadside plan or a tow company. Get the car off the road if it moves, stay buckled in or behind the guardrail, and keep your hazards on.

## Who Do I Call First When My Car Breaks Down on a Toll Road?

Call 911 first if you're stopped in a travel lane, hit something, or feel in danger. If you're safely on the shoulder, call the toll road's own help line instead.

Most toll roads post a roadside assistance number on mile markers, call boxes, and the signs near entrances. Operators like the Pennsylvania Turnpike, Florida's Turnpike, the Illinois Tollway, and the Ohio Turnpike run their own patrols or contract dispatch centers. Those crews know exactly where you are because toll roads use exit numbers and mile markers instead of street addresses.

If you can't find the number, 911 will route you. Don't Google a random tow company first. Unvetted tow operators get in trouble on toll roads, and many toll authorities restrict who can tow from the roadway.

## What Should I Do in the First Two Minutes?

Get the car as far right as you safely can, turn on your hazards, and decide whether to stay in the car or leave it. Do this before you make any phone calls.

1. **Coast to the right shoulder.** Even a dead engine rolls. Steer early and use the momentum.
2. **Hazards on, wheels turned away from traffic.** If you're hit from behind, the car drifts away from the lane, not into it.
3. **Know your marker.** Note the nearest mile marker or last exit number. That's your address. Dispatch needs it.
4. **Decide: in or out.** On a high-speed toll road, most people are safer outside the car, behind the guardrail, well away from traffic. In rain, at night, or with no barrier available, stay buckled in with your seatbelt on and your foot off the brake pedal so your brake lights don't confuse drivers behind you.
5. **Don't stand between your car and traffic.** Don't try to change a tire on the traffic side.

If you're stuck in the left lane or the median side, don't try to cross traffic. Stay buckled in and tell 911 exactly that. See [car broke down on interstate median: what to do right now](/car-broke-down-on-interstate-median-what-to-do/) for that situation.

## Will the Toll Authority Tow My Car for Free?

Usually the toll authority will help you with a safety response, but a free tow is not guaranteed. Policies vary widely by road.

Many toll roads send a patrol vehicle that will put out flares, block a lane, jump a dead battery, add a little gas, or help with a flat. Those basics are often free. Towing is where it varies. Some authorities operate a rotation of approved tow companies and charge you a set rate. Others hand you off to your own roadside plan.

Ask the dispatcher two things before you agree to anything: who is towing, and what will it cost. Get the answer before the truck hooks up. That one question prevents most billing disputes.

## Should I Call My Roadside Assistance or Insurance Instead?

Yes, call your roadside provider once you've alerted the toll authority or 911, not instead of them. Your provider handles the tow, but they can't clear a hazard on a restricted road.

AAA, your insurer's roadside add-on, or a credit card benefit can all send a truck. Tell them you're on a toll road and give your mile marker. Some providers can't tow from certain limited-access roads without the toll authority's approval, which can add time. If you drive a rideshare vehicle, check [whether Lyft or Uber offers roadside assistance](/does-lyft-or-uber-offer-roadside-assistance-to-drivers/), because it's often less than drivers assume.

## What Happens If I Can't Pay the Toll or My Transponder Fails?

A breakdown does not cancel your toll obligation, but you won't be fined for being stopped. Toll roads bill by transponder or by plate photo, and a disabled car on the shoulder doesn't trigger a violation.

If your car gets towed off at an exit, you may pass through a toll point on the way out. Tell the tow driver. Many authorities waive the toll for a disabled vehicle exiting, but ask. Keep any receipt or incident number the dispatcher gives you, since that's your proof if a toll bill shows up later.

## Common Questions

**Q: Is it illegal to stop on a toll road shoulder?**
A: No, stopping on the shoulder for a real breakdown is allowed. Stopping just to rest, take a call, or check directions is usually not, and you can be cited.

**Q: Can I walk to the next exit for help?**
A: Almost never. Walking on a high-speed toll road is dangerous and often illegal. Stay with the car behind a barrier and let a patrol or tow come to you.

**Q: How long will help take on a toll road?**
A: Toll road patrols often reach you faster than a private tow because they're already driving the route. Private tows depend on traffic and whether they're allowed on the road. Ask dispatch for an estimated arrival time.

**Q: What if I have no cell signal on the toll road?**
A: Use an emergency call box if you see one, or flag down the next patrol vehicle by staying in the car with hazards on. Many toll roads have call boxes at intervals along the shoulder.

**Q: Will my insurance pay for the tow if I'm not at fault?**
A: Only if you have roadside or towing coverage on the policy, regardless of fault. If another driver caused a crash, their insurer may reimburse you later.
