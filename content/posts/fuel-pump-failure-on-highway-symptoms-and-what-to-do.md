> **Quick Answer:** Fuel pump failure on the highway usually shows up as sputtering, surging, or sudden power loss under load, followed by a stall. Turn on your hazards, hold the wheel straight, and coast to the shoulder while you still have momentum. Don't keep cranking the engine. Once you're stopped, call for a tow, because a failed pump will not fix itself.

## What Are the Symptoms of a Fuel Pump Failing on the Highway?

The classic sign is the engine sputtering or losing power at highway speed, especially when you press the gas to merge or climb a grade. A weak pump can't keep up with fuel demand, so the engine starves exactly when you need power most.

Watch for these, roughly in the order they tend to appear:

- **Sputtering or hesitation at steady speed**, like the engine is coughing.
- **Surging**, where the car lurches forward and drops back without you touching the pedal.
- **Loss of power going uphill or accelerating**, while idling seems fine.
- **A whining or loud humming noise from the rear seat or trunk area**, where most in-tank pumps sit.
- **Sudden stall**, often with the dash lights still on and the engine turning over but not catching.

Many cars give you warning days earlier: long cranking before the engine starts, or stalling when hot that goes away after cooling off. A highway failure is often the third or fourth time the pump has tried to quit.

## What Do I Do the Moment My Car Starts Losing Power at Highway Speed?

Turn on your hazard lights immediately and steer toward the right shoulder while you still have momentum. Power steering and brakes will feel heavier once the engine dies, but both still work. You just need more effort.

1. **Hazards on.** Do it before anything else so traffic behind you reads the situation.
2. **Hold the wheel steady.** Don't jerk. Check mirrors and signal toward the shoulder.
3. **Shift to neutral if the car is bucking.** Neutral lets you coast without the engine fighting you.
4. **Press the brake firmly and steadily.** With the engine off, the brake booster has a reserve for a few presses. Don't pump it repeatedly.
5. **Coast as far onto the shoulder as you can.** Get all four wheels past the white line.
6. **Park, set the parking brake, and keep the hazards running.**

Don't try to restart on the live lane. If the car dies in traffic with no shoulder, stay buckled in, keep the hazards on, and call 911 before you consider getting out. ## How Can I Tell It's the Fuel Pump and Not Something Else?

Check these in order: a dead fuel pump usually means the engine cranks normally but won't fire, and you won't hear a faint hum from the rear when you turn the key to the "on" position.

Try this once, safely, after you're parked on the shoulder:

- Turn the key to "on" without starting. Listen near the back of the car. A healthy pump hums for 2 to 3 seconds as it primes. Silence points to the pump, its relay, or a fuse.

## Should I Tap the Fuel Tank or Try to Fix It Myself?

No, don't try a roadside repair on the shoulder. The old trick of smacking the tank to wake up a stuck pump can occasionally work once, but it is not worth standing beside traffic at highway speed.

The one thing worth checking is the fuel pump fuse and relay, located in the fuse box (your owner's manual shows the layout). If you have a spare fuse and the box is easy to reach, swapping a blown fuse costs little. But a blown fuse often means a failing pump is pulling too much current, so the fix is temporary at best.

Replacement means dropping the fuel tank or accessing the pump through a panel under the rear seat. That's a shop job. Expect parts and labor to vary widely by vehicle, from a few hundred dollars on a common sedan to well over a thousand on trucks and some imports.

## Does a Failed Fuel Pump Need a Tow?

Yes, a car with a failed pump should be towed, not coaxed home. A pump that fails hot may restart after cooling, but it will fail again, usually at the worst moment.

Call your insurer's roadside coverage, AAA, or a local flatbed. Tell the dispatcher you have a no-start from suspected fuel delivery failure so they send the right truck. ## Common Questions

**Q: Can I keep driving with a failing fuel pump?**
A: You can for a short while, but it's a gamble. A weak pump can quit completely without warning, killing power steering assist and leaving you stalled in traffic. Drive straight to a shop at low speed only if you must.

**Q: Will the check engine light come on for a bad fuel pump?**
A: Sometimes, often with low fuel pressure codes, but not always. Many pumps fail with no warning light at all, so don't rely on the dash to tell you.

**Q: Why does my car sputter at highway speed but run fine in the city?**
A: Highway driving demands steady, high fuel flow. A worn pump that handles low-load city driving can fall short at 65 mph or on a hill, so symptoms show up on the highway first.

**Q: How long does a fuel pump usually last?**
A: Many last well over 100,000 miles, but running the tank near empty regularly shortens life because the pump uses fuel to cool itself. Keep at least a quarter tank when you can.

**Q: Could it be a clogged fuel filter instead of the pump?**
A: Yes. A clogged filter causes nearly identical sputtering and power loss. It's a cheaper fix, so a shop should check fuel pressure before replacing the pump.
